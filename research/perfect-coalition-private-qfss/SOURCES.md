# Sources and scope audit

1. Alex B. Grilo and Ramis Movassagh, [Quantum Function Secret Sharing, arXiv:2501.18928v2 (10 October 2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18928v2), also [ePrint 2025/151](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/151). The latest arXiv version's §1.1.3 explicitly invites techniques other than the construction used in the source, and lists distinct verification and computational questions. Section 3.1, Eq. (4), Definition 1 and Algorithm 1 fix the target probability, local copies, projector and classical answer reconstruction. Definition 1 gives arbitrary share spaces S_i; Algorithm 2 in §3.2 describes a particular representation of those shares as selected circuit descriptions. The scheme here fits the former syntax and does not purport to be an instance of the latter representation. Eq. (5) uses the literal coalition range |I|<t_source, although the paper elsewhere describes its result in terms of singleton collusion and parameter 1. We state the maximum number t of colluders directly; at most t means the literal Eq. (5) threshold t_source=t+1, or parameter t in the maximum-coalition convention. Correctness in Definition 1 is for each fixed C, state and projector, followed by the generation and evaluation randomness; this does not give a proof for adaptively chosen inputs after viewing public dither. The v2 introduction cites classical PIR as a related application of FSS; it does not give the dynamic quantum table construction.

2. Benny Chor, Oded Goldreich, Eyal Kushilevitz, and Madhu Sudan, [Private Information Retrieval, JACM 45(6), 1998](https://doi.org/10.1145/293347.293350); Amos Beimel, Yuval Ishai, and Eyal Kushilevitz, [General constructions for information-theoretic private information retrieval, JCSS 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022000005000309). These are antecedents for efficient information-theoretic replicated-table retrieval and collusion-aware general constructions. Standard PIR solves the classical problem once a common table exists; the parties here instead receive only copies of a quantum state and a measurement and must build aligned data locally.

3. Stanislav Kruglik, Son Hoang Dau, Han Mao Kiah, Huaxiong Wang, and Liang Feng Zhang, [Verifiable Information-Theoretic Function Secret Sharing, ePrint 2024/453](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/453.pdf), §2.2, pp. 9–11, explicitly write polynomial-based r-server t-private PIR protocol Π0. Their database polynomial F_x at an injective index encoding E(i) returns the record; their query curve has degree t and constant point E(i); server j answers F_x(c(u_j)); r>=dt+1 suffices for interpolation at zero, with public points and one field-element answer. This is the exact classical architecture used here, with Boolean coordinate selectors and d=L, applied to each dither. It is not claimed to be new. Their result presumes an already jointly available classical database and does not address estimation of an input-dependent quantum one. The subsequent point/comparison function sharing and verification portions are not imported.

4. Robert M. Gray and Thomas G. Stockham, Jr., [Dithered Quantizers, IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 39(3), 805–812, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1109/18.256489). Addition of a random offset before quantization is classical. Here the role of the uniform finite offset is specifically to avoid numerical discontinuities jointly across a whole table and all parties under a common error bound. We give the closed-arc/counting argument directly; no generic noise-whitening property is claimed. Wassily Hoeffding, [Probability Inequalities for Sums of Bounded Random Variables, JASA 1963](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830), is used for the Bernoulli averages and independent-offset majority.

The primary-paper check covered QFSS revisions and follow-ups searched under QFSS, information-theoretic collusion, PIR and quantum circuit probability; the nearest retrieved concrete compiler is the classical Π0 above. Classical finite-field polynomial sharing and dither, quantum state secret sharing, QPIR with quantum messages/shared entanglement, and delegated evaluation of a jointly shared input are different components or models. The limited contribution claimed here is applying the known polynomial PIR to a dynamic quantum probability table with finite, shared rounding and honest-sampling correctness, within the stated QFSS syntax. We do not claim priority for a classical or general extrapolation technique, or any result about an actual protocol implementation. Searches did not locate an earlier statement of this exact quantum-input compilation; that is not an exhaustive priority assertion.
