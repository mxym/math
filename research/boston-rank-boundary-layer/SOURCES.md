# Sources and scope (19 November 2026)

* Geoffrey Pritchard and Mark C. Wilson, [*Asymptotic Welfare Performance of Boston Assignment Algorithms*](https://doi.org/10.1287/stsy.2022.0104), *Stochastic Systems* 13 (2023), 247–270. Section 8.2, printed p. 269, separates its question on the expected worst rank under naive Boston from a prediction about expected average rank. The authors already give fixed-round and fixed-preference-rank limits (in particular their Theorems 7–9), and their finite one-round occupancy calculations precede the present use of the count process. The rank of a specified student late in the priority order is not the largest realized assigned rank. The paper also points out a hashing analogy in §8.2.
* Jacky Lo and Mark C. Wilson, [*New algorithms for matching problems*](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~mcw/Research/Outputs/LW2017.pdf), Proposition 3.4, earlier describes the equivalent FIFO-queue implementation for a common priority order. No claim is made here for inventing a serial exposure.
* Josué Ortega, [*A note on the strategic vulnerability of the Boston mechanism in random markets*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00355-026-01655-9), Conjecture 1, revisits the predicted leading-factor comparison. Ortega's later [*Asymptotic Equivalence of Immediate and Deferred Acceptance*, arXiv:2607.24970v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24970v2), Theorem 1 and §5.1, already proves that expected average rank is asymptotic to log n for naive and adaptive IA. Its §3.1 introduces the within-round sequential accounting used in the proof here while respecting actual priorities. The new calculation on the mean is additive, not a new answer to the first-order conjecture. Nothing here gives an additive comparison with general deferred acceptance.
* Ethem Akyol, [*Inefficiency of Random Serial Dictatorship under incomplete information*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2023.105741), *Journal of Economic Theory* 213 (2023), 105741, compares random Boston and random serial dictatorship by interim welfare and asymptotic rank-probability dominance in a cardinal-value model. The original fixed-rank/interim comparisons are credited; a largest rank across the realized market and an additive constant for its unnormalized average rank are different observables.
* The infinite product for sinh is classical. Furthermore the exponential series underlying −log A₁ is not a new probability law: up to a deterministic scale its Laplace transform is the t=1 sinh family treated by Jim Pitman and Marc Yor, [*Infinitely Divisible Laws Associated with Hyperbolic Functions*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-mathematics/article/infinitely-divisible-laws-associated-with-hyperbolic-functions/D91B384C84FA02C55A332B396BB85385). What is proved here is the assignment-process limit, including its coupling to the centered realized average.

Targeted searches for later results citing the original work, terminal/egalitarian rank of immediate acceptance, asymptotic average-rank corrections, and parallel uniform probing did not locate the same joint law or constants. The scope is sincere complete independent uniform rankings in balanced one-seat markets. Neither adaptive Boston nor many-to-one markets are covered by this second-order proof.
