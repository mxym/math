# Definable canonical prefixes and dense limiting first-order probabilities

Let $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathrm{Geom}(M)$ be any infinite hereditary permutation class and $\rho$ its exponential growth rate. Let $\mathrm{Lim}(\mathcal C)$ be the set of limiting probabilities of first-order sentences about the two linear orders under uniform sampling at size $n$. The accompanying convergence proof shows that all such limits exist. We prove:
1. When $\rho>1$, this set is dense in $[0,1]$.
2. Every limit belongs to the single real number field $\mathbb Q(\rho)$. Given a presenting matrix, a finite relative avoidance basis and a first-order sentence, the limit is effectively computable as an algebraic number.

For the polynomial alternative ($\rho=1$ by the count asymptotic), Falgas-Ravry, Markström, Skerman and Räty already give the **exact finite** set of possible probabilities in Theorem 1.2. Their Theorem 1.4 proves density for every exponentially growing *small* permutation class. We adapt their Theorem 4.1 small-event covering strategy to the canonical cell code; no priority is claimed for the density criterion or those prior cases. The present hypothesis of geometric griddability is not limited by an upper-growth threshold. For example the one-row matrix with $b$ increasing cells codes permutations with at most $b-1$ descents. It has growth $b$: the $b^n$ assignments of value ranks to blocks map onto the permutations, with at most $(n+1)^b$ choices of boundaries per permutation.

**A first-order canonical gridding.** Refine once to a partial multiplication matrix as in the shape proof, and fix its row and column signs and a total order on its finite cell alphabet. Consider a permutation as points with position order $<_x$ and value order $<_y$. Each interior vertical boundary may be specified by the initial segment of $<_x$ that lies to its left. Such an initial segment is either empty (encoded by a separate finite disjunct), or is specified by its last point (the whole order is allowed). Do the same for horizontal boundaries and $<_y$. Require the sequence of cut ranks in each direction to be nondecreasing. Repeated points and repeated empty cuts are permitted, so empty strips, including the first and last, cause no difficulty. The predicate that a point lies in a particular cell is obtained by taking differences of the indicated initial segments. All this uses only finitely many first-order variables and comparisons because the matrix is fixed.

For a proposed set of cuts, require that no point occupies a zero cell. On the points define directed edges $p\to q$ for distinct points in a common column when their position order is forward in that column's sign; also insert $p\to q$ in a common row when their value order is forward in the row's sign. Both arcs are inserted on a contradictory pair. A gridding is geometrically realizable precisely if this union digraph is acyclic: a geometric word order topologically sorts it, and conversely every topological order, used as the temporal order of its cell letters, reproduces the proposed within-strip orders and hence the same gridded permutation.

Acyclicity here is first-order on the proposed cuts. In any shortest directed cycle with length $s\ge4$, a chord oriented in *either* direction closes a shorter directed cycle along one of the two arcs of the cycle. Hence no nonconsecutive vertices share a column, since any two distinct points in a column are ordered in at least one direction. A column can contain at most two vertices in this cycle. Thus $s\le 2c$, where $c$ is the number of columns; cycles of lengths two and three are separately tested. There are no loops. Testing for directed cycles of all lengths $2,\ldots,\max(3,2c)$ is a finite first-order condition. A triangle wholly within one column cannot arise there from the strict column order alone; any opposite additional row edge would already create a two-cycle. No assumption about all triangles is required by the bound.

Among feasible griddings, the AABRV canonical one lexicographically maximizes the column sizes then the row sizes. Equivalently lexicographically maximize the vector of cumulative column cuts followed by the cumulative row cuts (when the first differing size is compared, all earlier sizes agree). Comparing any two proposed vectors of such cuts is first-order, using proper initial-segment containment at the first differing coordinate. Requiring that no feasible competitor is larger therefore defines the canonical cut **ranks**. Any equivalent witnesses give the same cells.

**Reading the first $h$ cell letters.** On this gridding the lexicographically smallest topological word is obtained by repeatedly taking an available element of smallest cell letter (available means no incoming arc from a remaining point). There is never a tie between distinct available points in the same cell, because that column orders them. This greedy procedure indeed yields the smallest word: each finite acyclic remainder has a topological completion beginning with any chosen available point. The topological words of the strip constraints are precisely those realizing the given gridded permutation, so this is the same canonical word as in the shape proof. For each fixed $h$ and prescribed cell word $w=w_1\cdots w_h$, its prefix event is expressible by a first-order TOTO sentence: choose the canonical cuts and distinct $p_1,\ldots,p_h$; at step $j$ require $p_j$ to be in $w_j$ and available after removing the finitely many previous $p$'s, and forbid any remaining available point in a smaller cell. This is a separate finite sentence for each $h,w$, not an assumption that transitive closure or a variable number of iterations is first-order. For $n\ge h$ the prefix events partition the entire class.

**Size of prefix atoms.** Use the finite unambiguous path presentation of the shape proof, with its *original cell alphabet* and all productive initial and terminal states. Given a prefix word $w$ and a productive end state $q$, there is at most one path from the initial-state set to $q$ labeled $w$: otherwise append the same fixed terminal continuation from $q$ to produce two accepting paths labeled by the same word. Paths ending at different states need not be equal. For each $q$, fix any path of length $b_q$ from the initial-state set to $q$. Prepending this path bounds the number of accepting continuations of length $m$ starting at $q$ by $|\mathcal C_{m+b_q}|$. The bound counts paths, using the global bijection between accepted paths and ungridded permutations. Hence the number of permutations beginning with $w$ at size $n\ge h$ is at most $\sum_q |\mathcal C_{n-h+b_q}|$. By the full (nonmodular) asymptotic $|\mathcal C_n|\sim c_0 n^{d-1}\rho^n$, for each fixed $h$ the limit probability of **every** such atom is at most
$$
\rho^{-h}\sum_q\rho^{b_q}.
$$
The finite coefficient is independent of $h$. For $\rho>1$ these upper bounds tend to zero. Order the finitely many length-$h$ atoms arbitrarily: partial sums of their limiting probabilities increase from zero to one in steps no larger than the bound. A finite union of their first-order sentences realizes each such partial sum. This proves density; it does not assert that every real number is attained by one sentence.

**Field and computation.** For a fixed sentence $\psi$, let $a_n=|\mathcal C_n|$, $b_n=|\{\pi\in\mathcal C_n:\pi\models\psi\}|$. Both generating functions $A(z),B(z)$ are rational over $\mathbb Q$ with effective descriptions from the indicated input by Braunfeld, *Decidability in geometric grid classes of permutations*, Theorem 5.2: conjoin the finite avoidance predicates to $\psi$ for $B$, and use avoidance alone for $A$. Let $\tau=1/\rho$. It is the first positive pole of the reduced rational function $A$ (positive coefficients and the count asymptotic), is real algebraic, and $A(z)\to+\infty$ as $z\uparrow\tau$. Given the convergence $b_n/a_n\to L$, the quotient $B(z)/A(z)$ also tends to $L$ along that interval: it is the weighted average of $b_n/a_n$ with weights $a_n z^n/A(z)$ and all finite initial weights vanish. Evaluate the quotient of the rational functions at $\tau$ after cancellation; it has a removable singularity or is already finite there, so $L\in\mathbb Q(\rho)$. Rational-function arithmetic and real algebraic root isolation make this computation exact for supplied $M$, basis, and sentence. Rational generating functions *alone* would not prove an ordinary probability limit. The field conclusion makes no claim that all elements of this field in $[0,1]$ occur.
