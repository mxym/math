# Chronology of the optimal bases for all-one events under even-wise independence

Let n≥3, 2≤k=2m≤n−1, and let M(n,k,p) be the largest all-one probability among n k-wise independent Bernoulli(p) variables. This is the count linear program of Berend, Ernst, Kontorovich and Kumar (BEKK): a law of S on {0,…,n} must give every polynomial of degree ≤k the same expectation as X~Bin(n,p); one maximizes its mass at n. Indeed, symmetrization preserves the objective and constraints; given the count, choose a uniform subset of that cardinality. The inclusion probabilities through order k agree by matching falling-factorial moments; inclusion–exclusion gives every k-bit pattern.

Write N=n−1. A paired basis I consists of n together with J, a union of m disjoint pairs {a_j,a_j+1}⊆{0,…,N} with increasing starts. Let w_s^I(p)=E L_s^I(X), where L_s^I are the interpolation cardinal polynomials. These signed masses uniquely reproduce degrees ≤k by interpolation. They are *count* probabilities when feasible, hence have the same signs as the per-string masses used in BEKK. Define the interior feasibility set B(I)={0<p<1: all w_s^I(p)≥0}. A phase basis is one with B(I) of positive length, and its phase interval is the closure of B(I) in [0,1].

**Theorem.** Each B(I) is an interval relative to (0,1), possibly empty or a singleton. The phase intervals form a consecutive partition of [0,1] with only shared endpoints. The phase bases in increasing p are strictly increasing lexicographically; more strongly, at each transition the ordered vector of pair starts changes by a nonzero vector in {0,1}^m. The number of phases is at most 1+m(n−2m). If there are b phases in this ordering, basis b+1−j is obtained from j by reflecting its nondistinguished sites around N/2 and fixing n, and their closed phase intervals are reflections around 1/2.

This gives the phase-order statement of BEKK Conjecture 5.2, the indexed form of the symmetry in 5.10 with the reflection specified in the preceding prose there, and the counting inequality of their conditional Proposition 5.8 without its transition hypotheses. The interior reflection and optimal-count-law uniqueness are known from Roy Hermann; the missing step is chronology. Literal endpoint feasibilities differ from these phase closures, as explained at the end.

## 1. Coverage and uniqueness, including degeneracies

We recall the standard paired basis characterization (BEKK Theorem 3.3) and give a direct argument for coverage. The dual LP minimizes E Q(X) over polynomials of degree ≤k with Q(n)≥1 and Q(x)≥0 at all integer x<n. For interior p the binomial weights are positive, so each nonempty bounded-objective sublevel has bounded point evaluations and is compact in polynomial coefficient space. An optimal vertex exists by finite LP duality (the bounded minimizing face has a vertex). Scaling down proves Q(n)=1 at an optimum. A vertex needs k+1 linearly independent active constraints, so it also vanishes at k distinct sites <n. There can be no additional roots. The normalized product over those roots has positive leading coefficient. The open intervals between each first and second, third and fourth, etc., sorted root have negative sign; there cannot be an integer strictly between the two members. Thus the roots give the stated pairs. Complementary slackness puts each optimal count law on I. The moment equations restricted to I are invertible by interpolation, proving feasibility of its signed weights.

Conversely for any feasible paired basis I, the polynomial Q_I(x)=∏_{s∈J}(x−s)/(n−s) is nonnegative on the integer grid, is 1 at n, and is strictly positive at every grid site outside I. It majorizes the indicator of {n}, with equality on I. Thus this basic law attains the optimum and every optimizing law is supported on I, hence equals it by interpolation. In particular two feasible bases at a transition have exactly the same strictly positive support. This uses no nondegeneracy. Also w_n^I=E Q_I(X)≥p^n>0, whether or not the other weights are feasible. These observations and the following interpolation identity are Hermann's Lemma 2 and equations (5)–(9). They do not classify nonsymmetric bit-vector laws.

## 2. Tilting out all the other pairs

For s∈J let l_s be the cardinal polynomial for J. Since L_s^I(x)=(n−x)l_s(x)/(n−s), binomial size reduction yields
  w_s^I(p)=n(1−p)/(n−s) E l_s(D),    D~Bin(N,p).
Fix a pair (a,a+1), and write G(x)=∏_{b≠a}(x−b)(x−b−1), over the other starts. Then G is nonnegative on the integers and strictly positive at both a and a+1. The relevant cardinal polynomials are
  l_a(x)=(a+1−x)G(x)/G(a),   l_{a+1}(x)=(x−a)G(x)/G(a+1).
Consequently the two weights are nonnegative precisely when
  a ≤ u(p) ≤ a+1,    u(p)=E[D G(D)]/E G(D).
The denominator is positive. Under the tilt of Bin(N,p) by G, d u/d(log(p/(1−p))) equals its variance by finite-sum differentiation. This variance is strictly positive because both members of the pair have positive tilted probability. The empty product also works. Hence the allowed p are an interval relative to (0,1); the finite intersection of these conditions is B(I), relatively closed there. Every weight is strictly positive at every interior point of a positive-length B(I), since strict increase forbids attaining either equality there. At an interior-parameter right endpoint the only weights that can vanish are *lower* members of pairs: the left-side strict inequalities forbid u=a there. At a left endpoint, only *upper* members can vanish.

There are only finitely many bases. Positive-length intervals cannot have intersecting interiors since their strict supports are the whole bases and the optimal count law is unique. They and some singleton sets cover (0,1). The finite union of singleton sets cannot fill an open gap, so the closed positive-length intervals cover [0,1], successively abutting without overlapping interiors. In particular, a basis feasible only at a single transition cannot insert an additional phase.

## 3. Simultaneous transition rule

At a common interior breakpoint let H be the shared strictly positive support other than n. In the left phase every upper member of a pair lies in H, so any missing-from-H site in its basis is a lower member whose successor is positive. In a consecutive run [u,v] of H, v must be an upper member: otherwise it would require the nonpositive v+1 to be an upper member. Working backwards from v tiles the run by consecutive pairs. If the run length is even, nothing outside it is used. If odd, u−1 is the additional partner. No pair can remain unassigned, as every pair contains a positive upper member. Similarly, working forward from u in the right-hand basis tiles the same run, with even runs unchanged and odd runs using v+1 instead. This also explains why a needed exterior partner must be within the grid in an actual transition. Even if consecutive runs are separated by just one missing site, the left basis uses possible extras only *before* a run and the right only *after*: there is never any ambiguity within either basis.

The numbers of pairs assigned to the run agree before and after. In their order, on an odd run left starts u−1,u+1,…,v−1 become u,u+2,…,v; on an even run the starts stay fixed. Concatenating these lists preserves start order. Distinct phases imply at least one change, proving the claimed coordinatewise and hence lexicographic order. Summing the starts increases by at least one at each transition. The minimum and maximum possible ordered starts have entries 2(j−1) and n−2m+2(j−1), respectively, so the total possible increase is m(n−2m). This proves the count bound; it does not by itself assert that the set of departing individual *sites* is always translated by two, or that all exceptional points are rational.

## 4. The indexed reflection and endpoint conventions

Put I*={n}∪{N−s:s∈J}. The cardinal interpolation formula of §2, applied to N−D~Bin(N,1−p), shows that w_{N−s}^{I*}(1−p) is a positive multiple of w_s^I(p) for interior p. Thus B(I*)=1−B(I) (Hermann, Theorem 1, including degeneracies). Now the established total chronological and lexicographic order implies I_j*=I_{b+1−j}. The same identities hold for the endpoints of closed phase intervals. Each pair {a,a+1} maps to {n−a−2,n−a−1}, including the first pair in the original list. This is the reflection described immediately before Conjecture 5.10 in BEKK; the printed last pair in the display does not apply that rule to a_1.

IMPROPER ENDPOINT IDENTIFICATION MATTERS. In the literal BEKK definition of L(I) using all p∈[0,1], every basis is feasible at p=1 (the law is concentrated at n). At p=0 feasibility holds precisely if 0∈I: the first moment forces the point mass at 0; conversely it gives the correct solution. These isolated endpoint memberships need not belong to the phase interval. For n=3,k=2,I={0,1,3}, the interior test is 0≤2p≤1, so its literal set is [0,1/2]∪{1}. The phase closure [0,1/2], not this disconnected set, participates in the chronological statement and endpoint reflection. The phase definition agrees with the interior of the intervals in the authors' examples and retains exactly their positive-length bases. We make no statement that every literal L(I) is connected. The excluded case k=n has a unique full count law; for even n its only paired basis has all sites, and the strict tilted variance in §2 still holds for 0<p<1.
