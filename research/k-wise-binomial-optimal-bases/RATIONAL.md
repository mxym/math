# Rationality of exceptional six-wise phase parameters

Keep the hypotheses and notation of PROOF.md: even k, 2≤k≤n−1, N=n−1, and an interior breakpoint p₀ between positive-length phases. Let r be the number of zero masses among the k nondistinguished sites J of its *left* phase at p₀. This counts all zero masses, not just sites that depart at the next phase. Then p₀ is algebraic of degree at most floor((k−r)/r), and it is either rational or of odd degree at least three. In particular p₀ is rational whenever 4r>k. At an exceptional transition in the sense of BEKK Conjecture 5.9 at least two distinct sites depart. Every such site had zero mass at the transition by shared positive support. Thus this proves their conjecture for k=6 (as well as k=4); it does not settle it for general even k.

**Proof.** At an ordinary interior breakpoint r≥1. By the tilted-mean sign rule all upper members of the old pairs have positive mass; precisely r lower members have vanishing mass. Let H be the positive part of J and A(x)=∏_{h∈H}(x−h). Its degree is d=k−r. It is the product of k/2−r full consecutive-root quadratics, nonnegative on the integer grid, and r singleton linear factors. Hence the nonzero sequence A(0),…,A(N), with its zeros omitted, changes sign at most r times: the quadratics are strictly positive on surviving sites, and no real polynomial of degree r can alternate signs there more than r times.

At p₀ the reduced quadrature on H matches all moments through k−1 of D~Bin(N,p₀) (PROOF.md §2, or cardinal interpolation on J). Hence E[(D)_j A(D)]=0 for 0≤j<r, where (x)_j=x(x−1)⋯(x−j+1). For a general p write F(p)=E A(Bin(N,p)). This rational-coefficient polynomial has degree exactly d because E[(D)_d]=(N)_d p^d, A is monic, and N≥k. In odds z form the polynomial
  G(z)=(1+z)^N F(z/(1+z))=Σ_{x=0}^N binom(N,x) A(x) z^x.
At z₀=p₀/(1−p₀)>0, the displayed zero-moment equations are exactly z₀^j G^{(j)}(z₀)=0, up to the nonzero normalizing factor (1+z₀)^N. Descartes' rule of signs applied to its nonzero coefficients bounds the total multiplicity of its positive roots by r. Thus z₀ is its only positive root and has multiplicity exactly r. So p₀ is the only root of F in (0,1), also of multiplicity r.

There are no real roots of F outside [0,1]. To see this for p<0 write the distinct sites in increasing order h₁<⋯<h_d; h_i≥i−1. The product of the first l factors has falling-factorial expansion Σ_{j=0}^l (−1)^{l−j} c_j(x)_j with c_j≥0 and c_l=1. Indeed on adjoining h_{l+1} the nonleading coefficients are c'_{j}=c_{j−1}+(h_{l+1}−j)c_j≥0 for 0≤j≤l (missing indices have coefficient zero); c'_{l+1}=1. When p<0 every nonzero term in F(p) has the same sign (−1)^d by the binomial factorial-moment identity, with its leading term nonzero. For p>1 replace each site by N−h and set p'=1−p<0: the reflected expectation polynomial at p' equals (−1)^d F(p), an identity of polynomials since it holds on [0,1].

Finally let B be the irreducible rational minimal polynomial of p₀. Since it is separable and F has multiplicity r at p₀, B^r divides F; hence r deg B≤d. If B is nonlinear it cannot vanish at 0 or 1; any real conjugate would be another root of F away from the endpoints, impossible. Its remaining roots come in nonreal conjugate pairs, so its degree is odd and at least three. In particular if r>k/4 only degree one is possible. This arithmetic consequence uses the phase sign information, not a nondegeneracy assumption on the transition.
