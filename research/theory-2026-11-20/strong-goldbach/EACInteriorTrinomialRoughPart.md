# A polynomially large rough part at invertible interior terminal signatures

A genuine all-composite terminal in the [Bożek prime-divisor graph](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09900) has distinct odd prime labels \(x_i\), all coprime to its even center \(N\), with
\[
 x_i+\prod_j x_j^{A_{ij}}=N,\qquad 3\le x_i\le N/3.
\]
The exponent matrix \(A\) is irreducible, nonnegative, integral, has zero diagonal and each row sum is at least two. Here *invertible* refers to \(A\) itself, **not** \(I-A\).

**Theorem.** Fix an invertible signature \(A\) such that \(w=A^{-1}{\bf1}\) has every coordinate strictly between zero and one. Let \(D\) be the least common denominator of these coordinates and put \(d_i=Dw_i\). For each fixed finite set of primes \(F\) and each \(\epsilon>0\), all sufficiently large actual centers realizing this signature satisfy
\[
 N_F\le N^{\,1-1/D+\epsilon},
\]
where \(N_F\) is the part of \(N\) supported on \(F\). In particular such a signature cannot recur at centers \(N\) with \(N/N_F=N^{o(1)}\). Independently of \(F\), it cannot recur at centers which are integral \(D\)-th powers.

For fixed \(k,\delta>0\), this rules out, at all sufficiently large near-smooth centers, every all-composite terminal with at most \(k\) labels, invertible \(A\), and smallest label at least \(N^\delta\). Indeed, there are finitely many matrices since every row sum is at most \(1/\delta\); each occurring on an unbounded sequence has \(\log x_i=w_i\log N+O_A(1)\) by the logged row equations, so \(w_i\ge\delta\). Every label appears in a product with another occurrence of a label, giving \(w_i\le1-\delta\). One may maximize \(D\) over this finite family to obtain a common positive rough-part exponent (with an arbitrary fixed loss in the resulting exponent). No claim is made uniform in growing dimensions or denominators, or about singular \(A\), vanishing weights, or binary Goldbach itself.

The main new obstruction below is that the normalized polynomial version with center \(T^D\) is incompatible with genuine prime values. It uses the irreducible factorization of the trinomial forced by a minimum-degree vertex.

## 1. The curve selected by the positive branch

Write \(s=N^{-1/D}\), \(Y_i=s^{d_i}x_i\). The normalized system is
\[
 \prod_jY_j^{A_{ij}}=1-s^{D-d_i}Y_i .
\]
At \(s=0,\ Y={\bf1}\), the Jacobian in \(Y\) is \(A\), invertible. The analytic implicit-function theorem gives a unique convergent small branch \(Y_i(s)\), with rational Taylor coefficients by coefficient recursion. It contains all sufficiently large physical solutions: the original logged equations first give \(\log x_i=w_i\log N+O_A(1)\) since \(x_i\le N/3\). Hence \(x_i/N\to0\); applying \(A^{-1}\) to \(\log(1-x_i/N)\) gives \(\log Y_i\to0\). See also the [explicit positive-branch construction](EACPositiveBranchJets.md).

Let \(K=\mathbb Q(s^{-D},s^{-d_1}Y_1,\ldots,s^{-d_k}Y_k)\subset\mathbb Q((s))\). This is a function field of transcendence degree one: the polynomial implicit equations with nonzero Jacobian define a smooth curve germ in the variables \((s,Y)\) at \((0,{\bf1})\), so its coordinates are algebraic over \(\mathbb Q(s)\); equivalently the smooth local ring has dimension one and \(s\) is nonconstant. The field \(K\) contains \(s^{-D}\). Its constant field is precisely \(\mathbb Q\) (an element of \(\mathbb Q((s))\) algebraic over \(\mathbb Q\) is constant, by formal differentiation in characteristic zero). The resulting actual affine curve is the spectrum of the coordinate subring generated by \(N,x_i\) in \(K\); power-series identities hold on the convergent germ, hence all sufficiently large physical integral points belong to this curve. The \(s\)-adic place has rational residue field and is a point at infinity, denoted \(P_\infty\). Its value group is all of \(\mathbb Z\), since \(\gcd(D,d_1,\ldots,d_k)=1\); hence its normalized pole orders of \(N,x_i\) are exactly \(D,d_i\).

If there are infinitely many such physical points, [Siegel’s integral-point theorem; see Alvanos–Bilu–Poulakis](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.2097) gives genus zero and at most two geometric places at infinity on the normalized curve. The one found above is rational. Were there two, both would be rational; since the complete genus-zero curve with a rational point is a projective line, there would be a rational function \(h\) with divisor their difference. Both \(h\) and \(h^{-1}\) are regular on the affine normalization, hence integral over the original \(\mathbb Q\)-coordinate ring. After clearing fixed coefficient denominators in their monic equations, both have bounded denominators at nonsingular integral points: for example a fixed integer multiple of each value is an algebraic integer in \(\mathbb Q\). The finitely many singular points are irrelevant. There are only finitely many rational numbers with this property simultaneously for the number and its inverse, contradicting infinitude since \(h\) is nonconstant.

Thus \(P_\infty\) is the only puncture. Choose a rational parameter \(z\) with a simple pole there. The coordinate functions are rational polynomials \(N=Q(z)\) and \(x_i=G_i(z)\), of respective degrees \(D,d_i\). Also \(z\) is regular on the affine normalization so its rational specializations have bounded denominator; scaling by an integer gives an integer parameter at the physical points. Change its sign, or pass to an infinite subsequence, so \(z\to+\infty\).

## 2. A single root of the center polynomial is impossible

Suppose \(Q(z)=c(z-\beta)^D\). Here \(\beta\in\mathbb Q,\ c>0\) rational. Since \(x_i/N^{d_i/D}\to1\), the leading coefficient of \(G_i\) is \(c^{d_i/D}\), rational. Bézout applied to \(D,d_i\) gives \(c^{1/D}\in\mathbb Q\). Put \(T=c^{1/D}(z-\beta)\). At the infinitely many physical points \(T\) is positive rational and \(T^D=N\in\mathbb Z\), so \(T\) is an integer. In this parameter \(x_i=P_i(T)\) for monic rational polynomials of degree \(d_i\), with identities
\[
 T^D-P_i(T)=\prod_j P_j(T)^{A_{ij}}. \tag{1}
\]

Every \(P_i\) is actually integral: at any rational prime \(\ell\) its coefficient Gauss norm \(h_i=\max_j|[T^j]P_i|_\ell\) is at least one by monicity. Since \(d_i<D\), the Gauss norm of \(T^D-P_i\) equals \(h_i\). Multiplicativity in (1) gives \(A\log h=\log h\), where \(\log h\ge0\). Irreducibility and spectral radius at least two (all row sums at least two) rule out a nonzero nonnegative eigenvector for one, by Perron–Frobenius. This applies at every \(\ell\). No \(P_i(0)\) is zero, since otherwise \(T\mid P_i(T)=x_i\), contrary to \(\gcd(x_i,N)=1\) at large integral \(T\). Taking logarithms of absolute values at zero in (1) likewise gives a nonnegative one-eigenvector, since the constants are now nonzero integers. Therefore **all constants are \(\pm1\)**. All \(P_i\) are distinct monic irreducibles over \(\mathbb Q\): distinctness follows from the distinct prime labels; a proper factorization in \(\mathbb Z[T]\) would give two factors of absolute value larger than one at every sufficiently large integer \(T\).

Put \(m=\min d_i,\ M=\max d_i\). Since a maximum-degree label occurs in a product of total degree \(D\) with at least two factors, \(m+M\le D\). The log identities on the positive branch give
\[
 P_i(T)=T^{d_i}\big(1+O_A(T^{M-D})\big).
\]
Thus every degree-\(m\) polynomial equals \(T^m+1\) or \(T^m-1\); if \(m+M<D\) neither nonzero constant would be possible. In particular \(D\ge2m\). If \(D=2m\), all weights must equal \(1/2\): every row has at least two factors of degrees \(\ge m\) and all columns occur. Thus \(D=2,\ m=1\) by minimality of \(D\); there could only be the two distinct polynomials \(T+1,T-1\), both needed as no loop is allowed. The \(T+1\) row would have \(T^2-T-1\) as its product, irreducible, impossible. Hence assume \(D>2m\).

We use [Ljunggren’s trinomial theorem in the precise form of Boyd–Martin–Thom, Lemma 4.1](https://doi.org/10.1112/S1461157014000436); see also [Tverberg, Math. Scand. 8, 121–126](https://doi.org/10.7146/math.scand.a-10599). For \(T^n\pm T^m\pm1\) the noncyclotomic part is irreducible if nonconstant, with multiplicity one; in particular a factorization into at least two positive-degree factors requires a cyclotomic factor, in the cases below. This result concerns the polynomial factors, not a primality conjecture about their values.

*No plus minimum.* If \(T^m+1\) occurs, irreducibility makes \(m\) a power of 2 (including 1). Its row in (1) requires \(T^D-T^m-1\) to be reducible, so it has a root of unity \(\zeta\). From \(\zeta^D=1+\zeta^m\), the two summands have absolute value one and their sum does too. It follows that \(\zeta^D\) is a primitive sixth root and \(\zeta^m=(\zeta^D)^2\). The subgroup generated by these two powers equals that generated by \(\zeta^g\), \(g=\gcd(D,m)\); consequently \(\zeta^g\) has order six and \(m/g\) is even. In particular \(2g\le m\). Because \(g\) is a power of two, \(\operatorname{ord}(\zeta)=6g\): writing its order \(n\) gives \(n/\gcd(n,g)=6\), whose 2-adic valuation forces \(v_2(n)=1+v_2(g)\). Thus the irreducible factor \(\Phi_{6g}(T)=T^{2g}-T^g+1\) occurs among the vertex polynomials. Degree \(2g<m\) violates minimality, while \(2g=m\) is incompatible with the classification \(T^m\pm1\) of every minimum polynomial. This excludes the plus sign.

*A minus minimum also fails.* Irreducibility of \(T^m-1\) gives \(m=1\), leaving \(P=T-1\). Then \(T^D-T+1\) must be reducible. Its unit-circle roots, if any, are precisely the primitive sixth roots and require \(D\equiv2\bmod6\): indeed \(|\zeta-1|=1\) gives \(\zeta=e^{\pm\pi i/3}\), with \(\zeta-1=\zeta^2\). They are simple since \(D|\zeta|^{D-1}>1\). The remainder
\[
 G(T)=\frac{T^D-T+1}{T^2-T+1}
\]
is irreducible by Ljunggren and nonconstant since \(D>2\). Unique factorization in (1) makes \(G\) another vertex polynomial. But \(D\) is even and \(G(-1)=3/3=1\). Thus \(T^D-G(T)\) vanishes at \(-1\), so its factorization in (1) requires the vertex polynomial \(T+1\). That is the forbidden plus minimum. This contradiction proves that \(Q\) has at least two distinct geometric roots.

## 3. Rough part, and unrestricted power centers

Let \(m_0\le D-1\) be the largest geometric root multiplicity of \(Q\). For any fixed \(F,\eta>0\), at the integer parameters above,
\[
 [\,|Q(z)|\,]_F\ll_{Q,F,\eta}(1+|z|)^{m_0+\eta}. \tag{2}
\]
Here values \(Q(z)=N\) are integers. To see (2), at each \(\ell\in F\) choose a nearest root \(\alpha_\ell\) to \(z\) in \(\overline{\mathbb Q}_\ell\). Apart from this root all distinct roots are separated by a positive fixed \(\ell\)-adic distance whenever one distance is small. Factoring \(Q\) gives
\[
 \min(1,|z-\alpha_\ell|_\ell)^{m_0}\ll_{Q,\ell}|Q(z)|_\ell .
\]
Since \(z\) is integral the individual distances are also bounded above by fixed constants. The algebraic-coefficient [\(p\)-adic Subspace Theorem, Evertse, Theorem 1.1](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~evertsejh/dio2011-padicsubspace.pdf), applied to \((z,1)\) with coordinate forms at infinity and \(X-\alpha_\ell Y,Y\) at the finite places, gives \(\prod_{\ell\in F}|z-\alpha_\ell|_\ell\gg(1+|z|)^{-1-\eta/m_0}\), outside finitely many integers. There are finitely many choices of the roots, and a rational exceptional line contributes at most one integer \(z\). This proves (2), also in the presence of primes at which there is no integral root. For the known simple-root polynomial-value estimate see [Bugeaud–Evertse–Győry](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.08290). As \(Q(z)\asymp z^D\), (2) proves the theorem.

Finally suppose instead that infinitely many actual centers are exact \(D\)-th powers, without any smoothness assumption. On the same parametrization write \(c>0\) for the leading coefficient of \(Q\); the leading coefficients of \(G_i\) again show that \(c^{1/D}\) is rational. Expansion at positive infinity gives
\[
 Q(z)^{1/D}=c^{1/D}z+b+O(1/z),\qquad b\in\mathbb Q.
\]
At the selected points the left side is an integer and \(z\) is an integer, hence the difference from the rational linear polynomial, of fixed denominator, must vanish for all large such \(z\). This forces \(Q(z)=(c^{1/D}z+b)^D\) identically, already excluded. All statements concern a necessary terminal obstruction, not a classification of represented or unrepresented centers.
