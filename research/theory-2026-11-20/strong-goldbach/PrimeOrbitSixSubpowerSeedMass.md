# The fixed seed contributes only subpower factors in a five-macroscopic six-terminal

Fix an odd prime p, a finite set F of primes and δ>0. Write N_F for the full F-part of N. Consider any sequence of even N→∞ coprime to p such that

    log(N/N_F) = o(log N).

Suppose six distinct odd primes x₀=p,x₁,…,x₅ coprime to N form a strongly connected factor-closed all-composite component: each N−x_i is composite, has all its prime factors among the six, and i→j denotes divisibility by x_j. Assume x_j≥N^δ for all j>0. Put A_ij=v_{x_j}(N−x_i) and a_i=A_i0. Then
    
    max_i a_i = o(log N),       equivalently    max_i p^{a_i}=N^{o(1)}.

In addition the five moving weights are bounded away from 1 along the sequence, and the seed column is in the rational span of the other five columns for all sufficiently large terms. The new assertion is about the factor *mass*, not bounded arity: a_i may still tend to infinity. No positive Goldbach representation, or exclusion of all six-label components at these centers, follows. The earlier [smooth-center interior obstruction](PrimeOrbitInteriorEisenstein.md) covers the fully F-smooth special case; this note allows the outside cofactor to diverge, with no power-class restriction.

## Near-smooth scalar separation

Write L=log N and S=F∪{p,∞}. Use the rational S-adic Subspace Theorem, e.g. [Evertse, Theorem 1.1](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~evertsejh/dio2011-padicsubspace.pdf).

1. A positive rational R of logarithmic height O(L), whose numerator and denominator have outside-S parts of log size o(L), cannot satisfy 0<|R−1|<N^(−c) for fixed c>0 on an unbounded sequence. Take the coprime pair numerator,denominator; use the difference and denominator at infinity, coordinates elsewhere in S. The coordinate product before replacement is exp(o(L)), by the product formula. The replacement gains O(N^(−c)); the two-dimensional theorem puts the pair on finitely many rational lines. A fixed ratio tending to 1 is 1. Bounded height is harmless.
2. For fixed D₀ and nonzero integer polynomials P_N(T) of degree ≤D₀, whose coefficients have log absolute size o(L) (use log max(1,|c|)), we have P_N(N)≠0 eventually and v_p(P_N(N))=o(L). Pass to any putative countersequence, fixing the nonzero exponents j in T. The highest power of N dominates all lower terms even with the moving coefficients. For a single term the valuation assertion follows from p∤N. Otherwise use the integral vector (c_j N^j)_j, with coordinates at infinity and all finite places in S except that at p one coordinate is replaced by the sum. Each coordinate has total product over S at most exp(o(L)), because N/N_F and the coefficients have this log bound. Its p-valuation is o(L) before replacement. A sum of p-valuation ≫L would therefore make the S-product exponentially small, whereas the projective log height is O(L). The outside-S max norms are ≤1, so this suffices in the projective formulation as well. A resulting fixed nonzero rational linear relation is impossible by dominance of its highest N-power. This proves the assertion even for varying coefficients; no moving algebraic forms are used.

## Column spaces and interior weights with no Ω cutoff

Order the nonseeds and take a subsequence fixing the five bounded columns A′ of A and all limiting weights w_i=lim log(x_i)/L; w₀=0. Since each input is itself a factor of another composite odd complement, x_i≤(N−3)/3. By [moving-seed independence](PrimeOrbitMovingSeed.md) and the six-vertex upward-forest bound, B=I−A has nullity two and e₀ is nonzero modulo its row space. Thus B′, obtained by dropping column 0, has dimension-four column space col B (otherwise some row combination is supported just on p). All these facts require only N_F≥N^(1/2+ε), true eventually here. The geometric lemma in [the five-macroscopic analysis](PrimeOrbitSixTerminalFiveMacroscopic.md), under exactly these size, connectivity and nullity assumptions, shows there is at most one weight-one y, and if it exists its sole parent x is nonhigh and nonseed with N−x=u y, u=p^b≥p, log u=o(L). The lemma does not use smoothness.

If a is not in col A′, choose a fixed integral left annihilator c of A′ with D=cᵀa>0 after passing to a subsequence and orienting (a finite integral basis suffices). Put m=Σc_i. The row equations give
    
    D log p = m log N + Σ c_i log(1−x_i/N).

If there is no weight-one label, exp of the difference on the left from m log N is 1+O(N^(−η)) for some η>0. If y has weight one, all other labels are ≤N^(1−η) after shrinking η. Replace its correction by log(1−1/u), with error O(N^(−η)), since (1−y/N)/(1−1/u)=1+x/((u−1)N). In each case the ratio is an almost S-unit as in scalar separation 1: the exponents m,c_y are fixed, D=O(L), and log u=o(L). Hence exact equality holds. Without y it would give p^D=N^m, impossible. With y it gives D=−bc_y by p-adic valuation, hence N^m=(u−1)^(−c_y)>1, a positive power of N of subpower size, also impossible. Thus a∈col A′ eventually.

The rank of A is ≥5: otherwise the spectrum includes 0,0,1,1 (geometric multiplicity suffices) and the Perron root ρ≥2, forcing −ρ−2 from trace zero, contrary to the spectral radius. Thus rank A=rank A′=5. No weight-one y can now occur: take a fixed nonzero integral left-null vector d of A′, also annihilating A. The exact log-complement relation first gives Σd_i=0 since the corrections are bounded. It gives d_y=0 because its correction has magnitude ≥y/N=N^(−o(1)) and the other corrections are polynomially small. The single-parent y column also gives d_x=0. A nonzero left eigenvector at 1 vanishing at x exists by dimension two, and vanishes at y by the same column. It has mixed signs by orthogonality to a strictly positive Perron vector. Delete x,y: a nonnegative zero-diagonal 4-by-4 matrix retains a left zero-eigenvector and a mixed left vector at 1. A spectral radius r>1 forces eigenvalues 0,1,r,−r−1; if r=1 then a nonnegative Perron vector is independent of the mixed vector, forcing 0,1,1,−2. Both are impossible. Compactness excludes max w=1 on all subsequences, proving the interior assertion.

## Eliminate two or more rows of positive seed mass

Take limits α_i=lim a_i log(p)/L after subselection; call a row hot if α_i>0, cold otherwise. The seed row is never hot. If there are ≥2 hot nonseed rows, there are at most three cold nonseed labels. All hot labels equal N modulo p^H, where H=min_{hot i} a_i ≫L. Fix the actual cold exponents a_i in a particular term for the following algebraic test; their coefficient heights are o(L), not necessarily bounded. Write T for the center, replace all hot labels by T and denote the cold nonseed variables X_i. Keep only the seed and cold row equations:

    T−p   = T^{u₀} ∏ X_j^{b₀ⱼ},
    T−X_i = p^{a_i} T^{u_i} ∏ X_j^{bᵢⱼ}.

Here u_i counts all hot multiplicities and b_ij all cold ones. These are fixed integers after subselection, b_ii=0. Each true row arity a_i+u_i+Σb_ij≥2, and A′ is fixed. The original cold prime tuple solves these equations with T=N modulo p^H.

**Algebraic impossibility.** The displayed system has no torus solution over the algebraic closure of Q(T) for any of these concrete cold coefficients. Indeed take a place over T=p of a finite function field containing a hypothetical solution, normalizing the order of T−p to be positive. The residue field is a number field (or use its algebraic closure). A zero/pole refers to the order of X_i at this place, not a p-adic order. The seed product forces at least one zero; with no cold variables it immediately contradicts evaluation at T=p.

If there is a pole, its row must use another pole, since the input cannot use itself. With ≤3 variables there are exactly two poles and a single zero. The zero row then cannot use a pole, since T−X_i has order 0 and it cannot use its own zero to offset a pole. This row at T=p would give p=p^{a_i+u_i} with a_i+u_i≥2, impossible.

Suppose all cold variables are integral at T=p. A zero input cannot use any zero, and any row that uses a zero takes value p. If at most one cold value lies outside {0,p}, that one cannot use a zero (otherwise it would be p), or itself, so it equals p−p^k with k its actual arity ≥2. Thus all the nonzero cold values, and the value p of each hot label and the fixed seed, have ordinary p-adic valuation one. The zero row's product has valuation equal to its arity ≥2, but it must equal p. This covers multiple zeros and all cases of at most two cold variables.

The last possibility is one zero and two values X,Y outside {0,p}. Neither row uses the zero. At the place their equations, and the zero row's equation, become

    p−X=p^a Y^e,       p−Y=p^b X^f,       p=p^c X^i Y^j,

where a,b,c count the corresponding seed and hot factors together, e,f,i,j≥0, and a+e,b+f,c+i+j≥2. If i+j=0 the last equation is already impossible. If e or f is zero, its row gives a value p−p^k of valuation one, and the other row then gives a value of valuation one (its product has valuation ≥2). The zero row again contradicts its arity. Assume e,f≥1. If ef=1 then e=f=1, a,b≥1: the two linear equations have nonzero determinant 1−p^{a+b}, and their solutions have p-adic valuation one (e.g. X=p(1−p^a)/(1−p^{a+b})). Again impossible.

For ef>1 the numbers X,Y are integral outside p: substitution produces a polynomial of degree ef with leading coefficient a signed power of p for each value. If both i,j>0, their product relation makes X and Y units outside p. If just one occurs, that one is a unit outside p. Hence at least one of p−X,p−Y is a unit outside p. At *every* place over p in Q(X,Y), X,Y have valuations ≤0 with v(p)=1. Indeed, if one is positive the other must be positive by its partner's equation. A minimum m between 0 and 1 would have a row product of valuation at least (a+e)m or (b+f)m, thus >m, while its left side has valuation m. Hence both would have valuations ≥1, excluded by the zero row as before. It follows that the selected complement which is a unit outside p also has nonpositive p-valuation at every place over p. Its absolute number-field norm is therefore at most 1.

For any complex embedding put M=max(|X|,|Y|). If M≥1 its occurrence on the right of the partner row gives either M²≤p+M (exponent at least two) or pM≤p+M (exponent one, coefficient ≥p). Thus M≤(1+sqrt(1+4p))/2, also for M<1. For p≥5 this is strictly less than p−1, so both |p−X| and |p−Y| exceed 1 at every complex embedding, contrary to that norm.

For p=3, suppose an embedding has |3−X|≤1 (swap X,Y for the other choice). Then |X|≥2 and the bound M<2.31 gives 3^b |X|^f=|3−Y|<6. Since b+f≥2 this forces b=0,f=2. Also |Y|≤1 by the first row, while |3−X²|≥|X|²−3≥1. Equality forces |X|=2 and X=2 by |3−X|≤1, so Y=−1 and a=0. But a=b=0 means that both of these original rows contain neither the seed nor *any* hot factor. They did not use the zero label, and have no self factors. Thus these two labels form a closed two-label block, contradicting strong connectivity of the actual six. Hence every embedding has both complement magnitudes >1 after all. This finishes the algebraic impossibility, including p=3.

**Uniform transfer to the actual congruences.** Append z∏X_i−1=0 if there are cold variables; this enforces the torus and the physical point extends with z a p-adic unit. By the effective weak Nullstellensatz over Q(T) there is a certificate of 1 with degrees in the unknowns bounded in terms of the numbers of variables and the common degree bound alone; any standard bound suffices, e.g. [Kollár, *Sharp effective Nullstellensatz*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0894-0347-1988-0944576-7) (one may use a uniform degree bound ≥3). Expand such a certificate using unknown polynomial coefficients for the multipliers at all monomials up to that bound. It is a solvable *fixed-size* linear system over Q(T). Select independent columns spanning the target and an invertible square row minor. Cramer's rule yields after denominator clearing a nonzero integer polynomial P_N(T) in the ideal generated over Z[T,X,z] by our equations. Its T-degree is bounded and its coefficient log-sizes are O(1+max_{cold} a_i)=o(L), since every matrix element has fixed T-degree and coefficients bounded by exp(O(1+max a_i)); determinant size is fixed. For zero cold variables use the single nonzero seed polynomial directly. Evaluation at the physical p-adic units gives p^H|P_N(N), contradicting scalar separation 2. This proves that at most one row can be hot.

## A single positive mass violates the spectrum

Let a denote the whole seed column. We have a∈col A′ and e₀−a∈col B′, where both spaces are fixed. After multiplication by log(p)/L and passage to the limit, α belongs to both spaces. If its only positive entry is at h, then e_h belongs to both. For any actual matrix on this subsequence, its entire two-dimensional left eigenspace at 1 therefore vanishes at h (since col B=col B′), as does its nonzero left null vector (since col A=col A′). Deleting h gives a nonnegative zero-diagonal 5-by-5 principal submatrix with a nonzero left zero-eigenvector and a two-dimensional left eigenspace at 1 each of whose nonzero elements in the retained space changes sign. This follows by restriction, since those full left vectors were orthogonal to the positive right Perron eigenvector of A. If its radius r>1 the five eigenvalues must include 0,1,1,r,−r−2; if r=1 an additional nonnegative left Perron vector is independent of the two-dimensional mixed space, forcing 0,1,1,1,−3. Both contradict the spectral radius, and r<1 is impossible. No hot row remains on any subsequence, proving the asserted little-o conclusion.

The class studied here is the prime-factor component framework of [Bożek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09900), not the set of ordinary Goldbach exceptions. The proof above uses no numerical infeasibility or unverified manuscript.

[Kelanemer's 2026 preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7274460) uses the closely related irreducible exponent-matrix framework. Only its public abstract was accessible for comparison; no priority or comprehensive nonoverlap claim is made.
