# Every one-mixed-row component: effective height for each exponent signature

Use the [pure-cycle theorem](EACPureCycleHeight.md) and [Bożek's definition](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09900) of a nontrivial non-Goldbach autonomous component (EAC). Say that a row is pure if its complement has just one distinct prime factor and mixed otherwise. The result concerns the exact primes in such a component, NOT a center asserted to lack a Goldbach representation.

**Theorem.** For each fixed exponent signature, the even centers admitting an EAC with exactly one mixed row are effectively bounded. With no assumed cardinality bound, the minimum label is N^{o(1)} along every unbounded sequence of such EACs. This does not rule out all one-mixed components, all EACs, or all Goldbach exceptions.

Suppose exactly one row of a k-label EAC is mixed; place its complete factorization at the end of the following pure chain:
    
    xᵢ^{aᵢ}=N−xᵢ₊₁   (1≤i<k),
    N−x₁=xₖ^{aₖ}∏_{2≤i<k} xᵢ^{bᵢ} .                                (1)

Each aᵢ≥2 for i<k, aₖ≥1, bᵢ≥0, and some bᵢ>0. For ANY pivot j with bⱼ>0, the following criterion gives an effectively computable finite bound for N in terms of the signature:

    a₁,…,aⱼ are pairwise coprime.                                    (2)

In particular the algorithm applies whenever some bⱼ>0 with j≤k−2, or aₖ≥2. It also applies to the residual pattern if (2) happens to hold. In particular the projector alone covers all mixed rows with at least three **distinct** output primes. The only pattern requiring a separate argument is a terminal single occurrence N−x₁=xₖ xₖ₋₁^b with aₖ₋₁ sharing a divisor with some earlier pure exponent. The rational expansion at the end of this note settles that remaining fixed-signature case.

### Why (1) and (2) occur

Every EAC label divides an odd composite complement, so 3≤x≤N/3. Different pure rows cannot have the same output prime: their different complementary prime powers lie in [2N/3,N] and their ratio would be at least 3. In the orientation from a row to its output prime, retain the k−1 pure arrows only. Their sources are distinct and their targets distinct. A directed cycle would be a proper factor-closed subset, inconsistent with strong connectivity. Hence the arrows form a single spanning path (all degrees at most 1 in and out, k−1 edges, no cycles). List labels in reverse path order so its terminal vertex x₁ is the mixed *source* and its vertex xₖ is the only unused pure *target*. Strong connectivity forces xₖ in the mixed row; x₁ cannot divide its own complement since it is coprime to N. This gives (1). Every xᵢ with i<k is ≤√N, and so is xₖ if aₖ≥2. The no-common-exponent gap from the pure-cycle theorem applies to any pair of pure rows whose shifted labels xᵢ₊₁, xₕ₊₁ are ≤√N. Hence a₁,…,aₖ₋₂ are pairwise coprime, as are all k−1 pure exponents if aₖ≥2. This proves the claimed coverage.

Fix a signature and a pivot satisfying (2). Put t=Xⱼ, β=aⱼ, L=∏_{i<j}aᵢ and n an indeterminate for N. Work throughout in the explicitly presented commutative integer polynomial ring modulo (1) (with N=n) and Xⱼ=t, denoted R; take the other Xᵢ as indeterminates. No irreducibility, smoothness, or finite free basis for R is presumed.

### A finite-dimensional reduction modulo the pivot

Modulo t, the mixed equation gives X₁=n. Recursing the pure equations forward with Xᵢ₊₁=n−Xᵢ^{aᵢ} then gives Xᵢ=Bᵢ(n) up through j−1, where B₁(n)=n. At step j it forces F(n)=Bⱼ(n)=0, a polynomial monic up to sign of degree exactly L: every power exponent in the recursion is ≥2 and the starting polynomial has degree 1. Starting at Xⱼ=0 and recursing the rest gives each remaining Xᵢ as an integer polynomial too. Conversely these polynomials satisfy all specialized equations modulo F, exactly: the mixed row has a pivot factor. Thus R/tR is ℤ[n]/(F), freely generated by 1,n,…,n^{L−1} as an abelian group.

More explicitly one can reduce **any** integer polynomial modulo the presentation and t^m to integer coordinates on the Lm monomials t^h n^s with h<m,s<L. Given a polynomial A, compute its ordinary residue H(n) of degree <L using the substitutions at t=0 above and monic division by F. This gives an identity A−H=t A₁ modulo the defining relations, with A₁ an integer polynomial. For example, every substitution Xᵢ→Bᵢ can be tracked by the usual difference-of-powers factorization; in the original relation each replaced specialization creates a multiple of t, starting with X₁−n from the mixed pivot. The relation F(n) similarly equals a multiple of t modulo the defining relations, since Bⱼ(n)−Xⱼ is such a multiple. Alternatively enumerate polynomial integer multipliers of the finitely many defining relations and A₁ in increasing degree and coefficient order until this exact identity occurs: existence has just been proved and there is no infinite oracle. Repeat on A₁ for m steps. This is a terminating exact reduction. Select any fixed deterministic implementation; normal forms need not be unique beyond order one. In contrast to division by the leading coefficient of the unspecialized eliminant, this procedure never inverts t or a nonconstant coefficient.

There is a formal solution of (1) over ℤ[[t]] with ALL coordinates, including n, of order ≥1 and Xⱼ=t. First view n as parameter: the map Xᵢ↦n minus its complementary monomial is a strict contraction on the tuple of formal series in nℤ[[n]], since every complementary monomial has total degree ≥2. The fixed point has all Xᵢ=n+O(n²); the jth formal series has an integral compositional inverse n=n(t)=t+O(t²). This supplies the claimed solution. Evaluating all reduced coordinates on it modulo t^m gives a rank-m rational map, since 1,t,…,t^{m−1} already span its image. Consequently, for any chosen polynomials U, the integer coordinate columns obtained by reducing n^m U have rank at most (L−1)m: on this solution their image is zero. This reasoning does not require the reduction algorithm to be intrinsic or linear on all inputs.

### Distinct sizes at a positive prime tuple give an integral projector

Use only variables Xᵢ for i<j. For indices 0≤νᵢ<aᵢ let v(ν)=β∑_{i<j}νᵢ/aᵢ. These L values are distinct mod 1 by (2) and the Chinese remainder theorem. Set m=2L(1+βj). Select monomials U=t^h∏_{i<j}Xᵢ^{νᵢ} of weight H=h+v(ν)<m with h≥0. There are T≥L(m−βj)>(L−1)m. Thus the reduced columns of n^m U defined above have a nontrivial integer relation. Compute one by rational elimination and clearing denominators: its nonzero polynomial P (in the distinct U) obeys n^m P ∈ t^m R. This is a finite algorithm depending only on the signature. Let A be its maximum absolute coefficient (≥1).

At a physical tuple t=xⱼ and gcd(N,t)=1, so t^m divides P(N,x) over ℤ. Moreover for i<j, xᵢ^{aᵢ}/t^β=(N−xᵢ₊₁)/(N−xⱼ₊₁) lies in [2/3,3/2]. Hence every selected monomial of weight H lies between (2/3)^j t^H and (3/2)^j t^H. Distinct weights differ by at least 1/L and each is ≤m−1/L. If t>(2TA(3/2)^{2j})^L, the unique highest-weight term of P has strictly greater magnitude than the sum of all other terms, whereas the total magnitude is <t^m. This contradicts divisibility. Therefore, explicitly in terms of the computed integer relation,
    
    t ≤ (2TA(3/2)^{2j})^L,   N ≤ (3/2)t^β .

We claim an effective algorithm, not a practical uniform running time or the pure theorem's elementary closed-form height bound.

### The only remaining mixed pattern: complete the fixed-signature argument

Write p=xₖ₋₁, β=aₖ₋₁. Now N−x₁=xₖ p^b with b≥1, xₖ=N−p^β, and by assumption some i≤k−2 has g=gcd(aᵢ,β)>1. Then U=xᵢ^{aᵢ/g} is an odd integer, M=β/g an integer ≥1, and
     
    N=(p^{β+b}−x₁)/(p^b−1),        Uᵍ=p^β/(1−p^{−b})−W,
    W=xᵢ₊₁+x₁/(p^b−1)>0 .

Since N≤(3/2)p^β, an unbounded sequence forces p→∞. We now hold the entire exponent signature fixed. From xₖ=(N−x₁)/p^b we get N=p^β(1+O(p^{−b})); all xₕ, h<k, are ≤√N. For h≤k−2 their pure equations consequently give xₕ=p^{β/aₕ}(1+O(p^{−δ})), where δ=min(b,β/2)>0; for h=k−1 the formula is exact. It follows that

    W ∼ C p^γ,      γ=max(β/aᵢ₊₁, β/a₁−b) ≤ β/2,

where C=2 if the powers tie and 1 otherwise. Put V=p^M(1−p^{−b})^{−1/g}. By factoring Vᵍ−Uᵍ=W, since U∼V∼p^M,
    
    V−U ∼ (C/g) p^{M−β+γ}.                                          (3)

The exponent is negative unless g=2 and aᵢ₊₁=2. That latter possibility cannot occur at i<k−2: aᵢ and aᵢ₊₁ would both be even among the pairwise coprime initial exponents. If it occurs at i=k−2 then β=2 and M=1. In this boundary case γ=1 and C=1 since β/a₁−b<1.

If b≥M, the binomial expansion gives V−p^M→0 for b>M and →1/g for b=M. Meanwhile (3) tends either to zero or, in the just-described boundary, to 1/2. Consequently |U−p^M|<2 for sufficiently large p, contradicting that these are different odd prime powers (their bases xᵢ and p are distinct). This also disposes of the boundary M=1 since b≥1.

In the remaining case 1≤b<M, (3) tends to zero. Write c₀=1 and cₗ₊₁=cₗ(l+1/g)/(l+1); these are the positive rational binomial-series coefficients of (1−z)^{−1/g}. Put h=⌊M/b⌋≥1 and
    
    Q(p)=∑_{l=0}^h cₗ p^{M−bl}.

This is a fixed rational polynomial, and its first omitted term gives V−Q(p)∼cₕ₊₁ p^{M−b(h+1)}, which tends to zero. Hence U−Q(p) tends to zero; since U is an integer and Q(p) has a fixed denominator on integer p, it must vanish for large p. This is impossible: the leading power and coefficient of V−Q would have to agree with those of V−U in (3), while 0<cₕ₊₁<c₁=1/g≤C/g even if the exponents coincide.

Here is an explicit uniform threshold test to substantiate effectivity. For an integer trial T≥2 write l=1−3T^{−δ}, u=1+3T^{−δ}; increase T until l>0. At every p≥T, every xₕ/p^{β/aₕ} for h<k belongs to [l,u]: before taking roots its difference from 1 is at most (3/2)p^{−b}+√(3/2)p^{−β/2}, by N−p^β=xₖ≤(3/2)p^{β−b}. Taking an aₕ-th root moves the positive ratio toward 1. Put H=|β/aᵢ₊₁−(β/a₁−b)|; take S=0 if H=0, and S=T^{−H} otherwise. The normalised quantity W/p^γ lies between C l and u(C+S)/(1−T^{−b}). Also U/p^M lies in [l^{aᵢ/g},u^{aᵢ/g}] and V/p^M lies in [1,(1−T^{−b})^{−1/g}]. With d₋=min(1,l^{aᵢ/g}) and d₊=max(u^{aᵢ/g},(1−T^{−b})^{−1/g}), put
     
    G₋=Cl/(g d₊^{g−1}),    G₊=u(C+S)/[(1−T^{−b})g d₋^{g−1}].

Then G₋p^s≤V−U≤G₊p^s, s=M−β+γ≤0; both constants converge to C/g. For b≥M select T with G₊T^s<2 and (1/g)T^{M−b}/(1−T^{−b})<2. These bound both nonnegative differences V−U and V−p^M by 2, so |U−p^M|<2 even in the β=2 parity boundary. For b<M let z=M−b(h+1)<0 and c=cₕ₊₁. By decreasing coefficients

    c p^z ≤ V−Q(p) ≤ c p^z/(1−T^{−b}).

Take T so G₊T^s+cT^z/(1−T^{−b}) is less than the reciprocal of the common coefficient denominator of Q. To prove the two positive differences unequal at all p≥T, additionally impose G₊T^{s−z}<c if s<z; c T^{z−s}/(1−T^{−b})<G₋ if z<s; c/(1−T^{−b})<G₋ if z=s. Each respective test eventually succeeds, the last because c<1/g≤C/g. All quantities are rational powers of positive integers or real algebraic numbers computable to certified intervals. Doubling T until the strict inequalities hold (or dovetailing T and interval precision) gives the asserted finite algorithm, then N≤(3/2)T^β. No asymptotic oracle is needed.

Finally if min C≥N^ε for a fixed ε>0 then every exponent in (1) is ≤1/ε. The k−2 pairwise coprime pure exponents are distinct integers ≥2, so k≤2+1/ε. Only finitely many signatures remain. The two complementary arguments above bound them all effectively, establishing the unrestricted-cardinality assertion **within the exactly-one-mixed-row class**. Multiple mixed rows remain open.

[Bożek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09900) gives the original graph and computed components; [Kelanemer's 2026 preprint record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7274460) describes exponent matrices for terminal components under a global counterexample hypothesis, but the full text could not be checked in this comparison. The stated result is a local effective height algorithm for actual factor-closed components, whether the center is represented or not. The [exact checker](check_eac_one_mixed_projector.py) tests the pivot lift at the formal origin and independently at a non-origin unit lift, and exercises the residual exponent comparisons. The height theorem does not depend on these controls.
