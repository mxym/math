# A two-thirds least-product bound in every doubly singular six-label component

Let \(p<q\) be the two least labels of an actual Bożek six-label EAC at even \(N\), \(A\) its complementary-factor exponent matrix, and \(B=I-A\). Then
\[
 \det A=\det B=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad pq\le N^{2/3+o(1)}.
\]
In fact the proof of the mixed-arity branch below uses only singularity of \(A\). For \(\operatorname{corank} B=1\), [the supported-minor lemma](EACSupportedMinorFive.md) therefore bounds the centers by \(X^{2/3+o(1)}\) (the invertible-\(A\) branch still has the stronger \(X^{1/2+o(1)}\)). For \(\operatorname{corank} B=2\), two least labels alone are **not** a general supported-minor set and the extra label that moves the frozen-seed tangent need not be \(q\). No smaller exponent for the full six-label class is claimed here, nor any bound on ordinary Goldbach exceptions.

## 1. Rigorous reduction to an auxiliary pair graph

Fix any \(\alpha>2/3\), and suppose \(pq>N^\alpha\) at arbitrarily large \(N\) in the indicated class. We orient rows \(x\to y\) for odd prime factors \(y\mid N-x\); all labels are distinct, coprime to \(N\), and occur as factors by strong connectivity. There are no self-factors. The \(p\)-row is composite with all its factors \(\ge q\), hence \(q<\sqrt N\) and \(p>N^{\alpha-1/2}\). Every label is at most \(M\le N/p\), where \(M\) is the largest actual label, so \(M/N\to0\). All products are in \((2N/3,N)\) (each factor label has another factor at least 3).

All-semiprime rows were handled in [the separate square-root paper proof](EACSixSemiprimeSquareRoot.md), which gives \(pq\ll\sqrt N\). Next, suppose the only row of arity \(>2\) is a single \(p^3\). Then \(p<N^{1/3}\), so \(q>N^{\alpha-1/3}\). [The lone-cube paper exclusion](EACSixLoneCubePaper.md) applies as soon as \(q>N^\kappa\) for any fixed \(\kappa>1/3\), not only the original \(\kappa=5/12\). Here are the only changes to that proof: \(p\asymp N^{1/3}\), high companions \(N/p\) cannot pair with any factor \(\ge q\), and in the lane with no high companions \(M<N/q<N^{1-\kappa}\). In the 3-vertex outside cofactor case its offset-product error is \(O(1+M^2/N)=O(N^{1-2\kappa})\), smaller than both \(\sqrt N\) and the star center \(s\gg N^\kappa\) (since \(\kappa>1/3\)). It is still smaller than \(s-p\) since \(p<N^{1/3}\). The two-vertex outside case and the giant-case proof require only these comparisons. Two \(p^3\)-rows cannot occur since row products are distinct. Thus we may assume there is either a triple row with a non-\(p\) factor or a row of arity at least four.

In the former case \(p^2q<N\) gives \(p<N^{1-\alpha}\). In the latter \(p^4<N\) gives \(p<N^{1/4}\), which also implies \(p<N^{1-\alpha}\) for \(\alpha\le3/4\); it suffices to prove the theorem for \(2/3<\alpha<3/4\). In both cases set
\[
 \lambda=2\alpha-1,\qquad \mu=2-2\alpha=1-\lambda.
\]
Then \(q>N^\lambda\) and \(p^2>N^\lambda\). Since \(p^3\le N^{3(1-\alpha)}=o(N)\), neither \(p^2\) nor \(p^3\) can be a row. Arity at least six is impossible because \(p^6>N\). At least three non-\(p\) factors are impossible by \(q^3>N\). Two non-\(p\) factors plus \(p\) are impossible by \(pq^2>N^{3\alpha-1}>N\). One non-\(p\) factor accompanied by at least three copies of \(p\) is similarly impossible by \(p^3q>N^{3\alpha-1}>N\). The exhaustive row list includes the pure \(p^5\) type and the four types below. First exclude \(p^5\) from the singular-\(A\) class.

If it occurs, it is unique and no other pure power row can occur, since the ratio between distinct powers of \(p\) is at least 3, yet all complements are in \((2N/3,N)\). Write \(N-r=p^5\); \(p\asymp N^{1/5}\), \(p<N^{1/5}\), \(q>N^{\alpha-1/5}\), and \(q/p^2\to\infty\).

In the fifth-power case declare all companions of \(p\) or \(p^2\) to be highs (two possible scales). Each is \(\sim N/p\) or \(\sim N/p^2\) respectively. It cannot occur in an unscaled pair of non-\(p\) primes since \(q/p^2\to\infty\); nor can it occur at the other scaled level, since the ratios of the two levels differ by \(p\). Each high occurs in exactly one product of the form \(p^eH\), \(e=1,2\). Every other true factor except \(p\) lies in a remaining unscaled pair and is \(<N/q\), so each high dominates all nonhigh labels by a diverging factor. If there are \(h\) highs there remain \(5-h\) genuine non-\(p\), nonhigh factor labels and exactly \(5-h\) unscaled pair rows, without isolates. The pure row \(5e_p\) of \(A\) forces \(v_p=0\) in its right kernel, and each high-pair row then forces \(v_H=0\). Therefore singularity of \(A\) is exactly the existence of a bipartite component of this residual signless-incidence graph. There may be no graph vertices, in which case \(A\) is invertible.

If \(h\ge2\), the residual has at most three vertices and equally many edges; no bipartite component is possible by the degree count in the lone-cube proof. If \(h=1\), it has four vertices; only \(C_4\) or an edge disjoint from a two-vertex component with two loops and their link can supply both balance and singularity. The equal-product relation excludes the sole high as an input to its dependency rows, because the high dominates all remaining inputs while still being \(o(N)\). Explicitly expansion of the equal products yields a signed linear combination of the input labels of size \(\gg H\) if that coefficient is nonzero, but the signed difference of input products divided by \(N\) is \(O(H^2/N)=o(H)\). In \(C_4\) this rules out all high inputs there, and the largest residual label divides the difference of two different such inputs smaller than itself, impossible. In the two-loop case two of the three dependency inputs are \(\le\sqrt N\) (two square factors, the lesser disjoint-edge factor, and \(p\) are all small, leaving at most one large eligible input). The linked products differ by \(> \sqrt N\), contradiction. This is the same four-vertex argument as in the lone-cube proof and does not use which high scale occurred.

If \(h=0\), there is one pure \(p^5\)-row, five unscaled pairs on the other five labels, and no occurrences of \(p\) except in its pure row. Use the no-high maximal-star portion of the lone-cube proof. Its only inputs regarding the pure row were: it is unique, \(p\le N^{1/3}\), and \(M<N/q<N^{1-\kappa}\) for some fixed \(\kappa>1/3\). These all hold here since \(q>N^{\alpha-1/5}\). To review the ranges: the three-vertex star leaves are separated by \(O(M^2/N)=o(\sqrt N)\), contradicting the need for three outside products from two outside factor labels. The two-vertex star leaves a three-vertex nonbipartite residual; two of its labels as input would force an impossible equal-sum pair from three large primes spaced by 2. The pure row and the star row thus consume two residual inputs, so \(p\) and its star center \(s\) both feed outside products. Their factor sums agree by parity and the \(O(M^2/N)=o(\sqrt N)\) error. But then \(s-p=O(M^2/N)\), inconsistent with \(s\gg N^\kappa\), \(p\le N^{1/3}\), and \(1-2\kappa<\kappa\). This proves the fifth-power lane is impossible with singular \(A\).


The remaining row types are
\[
 pH,\qquad xy\;(x,y\ne p),\qquad p^2x\;(x\ne p),\qquad p^4.
\]
The first kind defines a high label \(H\). Such a label satisfies \(H\sim N/p\), and \(Hq>N\) since \(q/p\ge N^{3\alpha-2}\); moreover \(Hp^2>N\). Consequently high factors occur only in their unique \(pH\)-rows. Let \(h\) be their number. The remaining \(5-h\) true non-\(p\) factors are called middles. Introduce the **virtual factor** \(b=p^2\); it is an odd integer distinct from every prime label and is **never a row input**. Then all rows other than the \(h\) high-pair rows are exactly an edge of the auxiliary graph on the middles and \(b\), with \(xy\) an edge, \(p^2x\) the edge \(bx\), and \(p^4\) the loop \(bb\). These pairs are distinct and the graph has exactly \(6-h\) vertices and \(6-h\) edges. Every true middle appears as a factor, and \(b\) appears because this is the genuinely mixed branch, so no isolates. Every auxiliary factor is in \([N^\lambda,N^\mu]\): its other factor on any incident edge is at least \(N^\lambda\). Every actual nonhigh input other than \(p\) is such a middle.

Here is the rank transfer. A right kernel vector \(v\) of \(A\) obeys \(v_H=-v_p\) for each high-pair row. Set the auxiliary coordinate at \(b\) to \(2v_p\); the other coordinates are \(v_x\) at true middles. The equation for each remaining row becomes \(v_u+v_v=0\) on its auxiliary edge \((u,v)\), including \(2v_b=0\) for a loop at \(b\). Conversely every signless-incidence kernel vector on the auxiliary graph uniquely lifts to a vector in \(\ker A\), by \(v_p=v_b/2\) and the prescribed high values. Thus \(\det A=0\) iff the auxiliary graph has a bipartite component. Given a left null vector \(c\) of \(A\), each high \(H\) occurs in only one product row, so that row's coefficient is zero. The remaining coefficients give exactly a signless-incidence relation on auxiliary rows (the \(p\)-coordinate is twice the \(b\)-coordinate). Equivalently, any auxiliary relation lifts by zero coefficients on the high-pair rows. In particular \(\sum_i c_i=0\) follows from auxiliary row sums 2, **not** from original mixed row sums. Therefore
\[
 \sum_i c_i\log(1-x_i/N)=0.                                      \tag{1}
\]
Passing to one fixed chart along an unbounded sequence fixes an integer \(c\); all arities are at most four. If \(h>0\), multiplication of (1) by \(p\) gives \(\sum_{i:\ x_i\ {\rm high}}c_i=0\) since \(H\sim N/p\), all low input sizes are \(O(N^\mu)=o(N/p)\) (as \(\mu<\alpha\)), and \(M/N\to0\). Each coordinate \(c_i\) is attached to an actual input, not to the factors indexing the row.

## 2. No high labels: a maximal star and two residue groups

In this case the auxiliary graph has six vertices and six edges. Put \(L=\max(b,M)\) (the greatest auxiliary factor; \(p\) is smaller than every auxiliary factor), so \(M\le L\le N^\mu\). Every auxiliary factor \(y>M/2\) can occur in at most one product: otherwise it divides the nonzero even difference of two distinct odd input labels, of magnitude less than \(M\), impossible. Thus \(L\) appears once, and it is not a loop, since any other product is at most \(L(L-2)\), too far from \(L^2\). Let \(s\) be its mate. If another edge pairs \(s\) with \(u\), then \(u>2L/3>M/2\) by comparing products; such a mate occurs only once, excluding a loop at \(s\) as well. Therefore the component of \(L\) is a star \(S\) whose center \(s<\sqrt N\) (since \(s<L,\ sL<N\)) and \(s>2N/(3L)\).

The outside graph \(U\) has \(n=6-|S|\) vertices and \(n+1\) edges, \(2\le n\le4\). Every one of its components is nonbipartite: a spanning bipartite four-vertex graph has at most four edges, a bipartite size-three component leaves at most one loop and yields at most three, and a bipartite size-two component uses only one edge and leaves at most three edges on the other two vertices, hence cannot give \(n+1\). Thus every outside factor is \(\sqrt N+O(M/\sqrt N)\) by alternating \(\log(u/\sqrt N)+\log(v/\sqrt N)=O(M/N)\) around a bounded odd closed walk. With \(T=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor\) their outside products expand as \(T^2+T(i+j)+ij\) with \(i,j=O(1+M/\sqrt N)\). The difference of two offset products is \(O(1+M^2/N)=O(N^{3-4\alpha})=o(\sqrt N)\).

Group the actual inputs of outside products into two classes: (i) \(p\), the star center if actual, and the actual outside labels, all \(\le(1+o(1))\sqrt N\); (ii) the actual star leaves, having diameter at most \(M/s=O(ML/N)=o(\sqrt N)\). This exhausts the inputs because \(b\) is only a factor, never an input. Within class (i), any two assigned products have input difference at most \((1+o(1))\sqrt N\); within (ii) it is \(o(\sqrt N)\). Their factor sums must therefore agree within each class: the sums differ by an even integer, so a nonzero difference would contribute \(2T\) or more in magnitude, against only \(o(\sqrt N)\) offset error.

For \(n=4\), each sum admits at most two unordered pairs (including loops), so two classes cannot give five products. For \(n=3\), each class would have to realize two distinct pairs with equal sum; the only such pair of pairs on three distinct values is the middle square and the product of extremes, and using it twice would repeat products. For \(n=2\) the three possible pairs have distinct sums, so again two classes cannot suffice. This eliminates the no-high lane even without singularity.

## 3. Two or more highs: at most four auxiliary vertices

If \(h\ge3\), the graph has at most three vertices with equal numbers of vertices and edges. It cannot contain a bipartite component: a leaf-edge component gives one edge and at most one remaining loop, while a connected bipartite triple has at most two edges. This is contrary to \(\det A=0\). Thus here \(h=2\), the auxiliary graph has four vertices, and is singular only if it is \(C_4\) or the disjoint union of a single edge with a two-vertex component carrying both loops and their edge. The same elementary count as in the lone-cube proof shows no other disconnected case is missed.

In the two-loop case the three linked rows have relation \((1,-2,1)\). Because the high coefficients sum to zero, and at most two high input labels exist, none can occur in those three input positions. The two loop factors are \(<\sqrt N\), and at least one of the disjoint pair factors is \(\le\sqrt N\). After ignoring the virtual label and including \(p\), at most one nonhigh actual input is above \(\sqrt N\). Among the linked rows two inputs are consequently \(\le\sqrt N\), impossible since their corresponding products differ by at least twice the lesser loop factor, \(>\sqrt N\).

In the four-cycle the two high coefficients of the cycle relation must sum to zero, and neither high can feed a star row in the viable case: if no high is a cycle input, both highs feed the \(pH\)-rows and are forced into a forbidden mutual pair. Hence both highs are cycle inputs and the two other cycle inputs are \(p\) and a true middle \(z\); the other two true middles \(r,s\) are the star inputs. The four auxiliary factor vertices comprise exactly these three true middles \(r,s,z\) and the virtual \(b=p^2\). Write the two shores of the cycle as \(a<a'\), \(d<d'\). Its largest product \(a'd'=N-p\) is assigned to the least input \(p\); its second largest product is assigned to \(z\), because the highs dominate all middle labels. By interchanging the shores we may take \(N-z=ad'\). The remaining products \(a'd,ad\) are the high-input rows. These determine exactly
\[
 z-p=(a'-a)d',\qquad |H_1-H_2|=(a'-a)d,\qquad
 |r-s|=p\,|H_1-H_2|.
\]
All four factors are odd, even the virtual \(b\), so \(a'-a\ge2\), and hence \(z>2d'\). Since \(z\) is one of the four auxiliary vertices, it must be \(a\) or \(a'\), not \(d\) or \(d'\). The two factors on each shore have ratio \(1+o(1)\), by comparing two products sharing an opposite factor, since every row value is \(N(1+o(1))\). Therefore \(\max(a',d')\le(1+o(1))z\). But \(d/d'\to1\) and \(p/z\to0\) imply \(|H_1-H_2|\sim z\). The last displayed identity would give \(|r-s|\sim pz\), impossible because \(r,s\) are among the same four factor labels and \(p\to\infty\).


## 4. Exactly one high: three- and four-row dependent supports

The lone high coefficient in any signless-incidence relation is zero. The high input cannot itself give the high-pair row, so it is one of the five auxiliary rows, but no relation uses it. Choose a nonzero relation among the other four rows. Its support has size 3 or 4 (distinct incidence rows of equal sum cannot be proportional); its support graph is connected since two disconnected dependent components would each require at least three rows. A factor incident to only one nonloop edge and no loop cannot occur among the support factors, because its equation would force that row coefficient zero.

**Three rows.** A relation among three distinct incidence rows must be two loops and their mixed edge: one row is a convex combination of the other two, and each of those then has support within the two endpoints of that row, leaving exactly the two loops. Both loop factors are \(\sqrt N(1+o(1))\), so the three products differ by at least \((2-o(1))\sqrt N\). Two remaining auxiliary rows must cover the three other factor vertices. There are exactly three ways to do this: a path of length two on those three vertices; a loop at one and disjoint edge on the other two; or a disjoint edge and a connection from the remaining vertex to a loop-support vertex. Indeed one of the two rows must contain two different outside vertices, and the other must contain the third.

Call an input or factor large if it exceeds \(3\sqrt N/2\). A loop factor or one joined to one of the two loop-support factors is not large. Both factors of an edge with product \(<N\) cannot be large. In the loop/edge and attachment/edge cases there is therefore at most one large outside factor. In the path case the only possible two large factors are its two leaves \(u,v\), and their common mate \(t\ge N^\lambda\) gives \(|u-v|\le M/t\le N^{5/2-3\alpha}=o(\sqrt N)\), irrespective of which of the two path rows has the high input. These are the only possible large actual inputs among the three support rows (the high is absent, \(p\) is small, and the virtual factor is not an input). If at most one is large, two inputs differ by less than \(3\sqrt N/2\); if two are large, their difference is \(o(\sqrt N)\). Both contradict the product gaps.

**Four rows, nonbipartite support.** A connected nonbipartite signless-incidence matrix has rank equal to the number of used factor vertices; four dependent rows then use at most three factor vertices, necessarily exactly three since on two vertices there are at most three possible products. The single remaining auxiliary row must be the disjoint edge on the other two vertices, so at most one of those is above \(3\sqrt N/2\). The supported rows use nonhigh inputs bounded by \(N^\mu\), so the alternating odd-closed-walk argument within the connected support puts its three factor labels at \(\sqrt N+O(N^{\mu-1/2})\). Combined with the fact that \(p\) is small and the high input is absent, at least three of the four inputs are \(\le3\sqrt N/2\). Their factorizations have the same sum: the product offset error is \(O(1+N^{2\mu-1})=o(\sqrt N)\), while factor sums are even integers. Three distinct pairs cannot share a sum on three factor values. Contradiction.

**Four rows, bipartite support.** A connected leafless simple bipartite graph with four edges is \(C_4\). Its shores have values \(a<a'\), \(d<d'\), all odd. All four support inputs are the four nonhigh nonstar inputs, including \(p\) (which is not permitted to feed its own star). Thus the maximum cycle product is \(a'd'=N-p\). The rows with products \(a'd\) and \(ad'\) have inputs \(p+a'(d'-d)>2a'\) and \(p+d'(a'-a)>2d'\), respectively. The input \(z\) of the least cycle product \(ad\) is larger than both, so \(z>2\max(a',d')\). As \(z\) is an actual low non-\(p\) label, it must be the fifth auxiliary factor outside \(C_4\), and it can occur as factor only in the one remaining row, whose input is the lone high \(H\). If that remaining row is a loop, \(z^2=N-H<N\) conflicts with \(z>2\max(a',d')\ge2\sqrt{N-p}\). Otherwise it joins \(z\) to a cycle vertex \(u\); for any factor \(v\) opposite \(u\) in the cycle, \(uv=N-r>N-H=uz\) since its input \(r\) is nonhigh. This forces \(z<v\), again contradicting its size. This finishes the last lane.

For every fixed \(\alpha>2/3\) the above forbids all unbounded countersequences. Hence \(pq\le N^{2/3+o(1)}\) at actual doubly singular six-label centers. The supported-minor lemma applies to the two least labels only for corank one; corank two requires a distinct counting argument. The homogeneous case and the lone-cube obstruction are cited separate paper proofs; no finite enumeration is used in this mixed-row proof.
