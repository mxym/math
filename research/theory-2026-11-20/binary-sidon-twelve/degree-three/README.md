# Exact maximum for binary Sidon sets on plane cubics over GF(64)

Let K=GF(64) and consider K² with **additive** group structure. Thus Sidon means that all unordered sums of two distinct points differ. This is not the group law of a projective cubic.

**Theorem.** For every nonzero polynomial f in K[X,Y] of total degree at most three, a Sidon subset of its affine zero locus has size at most 66. Equality is possible. In fact every 66-point example, up to an invertible K-affine transformation, consists of an affine ellipse and its nucleus. In particular *no geometrically irreducible cubic contains a 66-point example*, even if its equation has no rational flex.

For any identification of a binary 12-space with K² this is one necessary condition on a hypothetical Sidon 67-set: no nonzero cubic or lower-degree polynomial vanishes on it. This is not an unrestricted upper bound of 66, or a classification of all size-66 Sidon sets.

## 1. Reducible curves and conics

A K-line contains at most 9 Sidon points. If it contained 10, translate one to zero. The remaining 9 vectors in a 6-dimensional binary space, used as parity check columns, yield a binary code of dimension at least 3 and minimum distance at least 5. No such length-9 code exists: the binary Griesmer bound is 5+3+2=10 (one may first pass to a dimension-3 subcode). In elementary terms, a minimum word of weight d leaves a residual code of dimension two and distance at least ceil(d/2) on its complement: in each nonzero residual coset one of the two words has at most floor(d/2) support in the removed positions. Repeat to obtain the stated bound.

Three lines therefore cannot do it. A conic plus a line containing a Sidon 66-set would have to supply at least 57 points on the conic. A singular or non-absolutely irreducible conic is a repeated line, two lines, or has at most their one common rational point. For a nonsingular conic, write the degree-two part as Q. If its cross coefficient is zero, Q is a square of a linear form over K; a nonsingular affine zero set is then the graph of an additive map up to affine coordinates, itself a coset of a binary 6-space. Otherwise a translation removes the affine-linear terms because Q has nondegenerate polar form. Up to a scalar and invertible K-linear change, its nonzero level is either xy=1 or x²+xy+32y²=1: a split or anisotropic binary quadratic form. Indeed a nonsplit Q is a scalar times the norm of a K-linear isomorphism K²→GF(4096) by factoring Q over the quadratic extension into conjugate lines; the norm is onto K*. A smooth conic itself has at most 65 rational points. Lower-degree polynomials or a constant give no additional case.

For the 63-point hyperbola every external point is the sum of at least 8 unordered triples of distinct conic points. For the 65-point ellipse every external point other than its nucleus is the sum of at least 9 such triples, while the nucleus has no such triple. Compute the exact distributions as follows, with rows (number of triples : number of external points).
* Hyperbola: 8:882, 9:756, 10:882, 11:1134, 12:378, 21:1.
* Ellipse: 0:1, 9:910, 10:780, 11:780, 12:1170, 13:390.

The verifier constructs these conics over K=GF(2)[t]/(t⁶+t+1) in the polynomial bit basis; 32 has trace one, and the ellipse is the norm-one equation. It directly checks the conics are Sidon, the sole 0-triple point of the ellipse is its nucleus 0, and that the ellipse plus 0 has all 2145 distinct pair sums. Distinct triples giving the same sum are disjoint: sharing one point gives the same pair sum and hence the same pair by Sidon. Keeping at least 57 conic points deletes at most 6 points of the hyperbola, or 8 of the ellipse. Thus each indicated external point still forms a forbidden four with the retained conic. The only possibility at size 66 is the whole ellipse and its nucleus. This set is on a cubic: multiply its defining quadratic by any linear equation through its nucleus.

It remains to treat cubic polynomials irreducible over K. If a cubic becomes reducible over the algebraic closure, its factors form a single Galois orbit (the field is perfect). Since 3 is prime they are three conjugate lines; each K-rational point belongs to all three, so there is at most one. Multiplicities in the reducible cases do not enlarge a zero set.

## 2. Geometrically irreducible singular cubics

The singular point is unique and hence K-rational: two double points would force their joining line to be a component by Bézout. Put the singularity at [0:0:1]; the equation reads Z·Q(X,Y)+C(X,Y)=0 with Q homogeneous quadratic and C cubic. The multiplicity is exactly two since a binary cubic factors into lines over the closure. For each direction (X:Y) there is exactly one further point if Q(X,Y) is nonzero, none otherwise (Q and C have no common projective zero by absolute irreducibility). A split node or cusp therefore has at most 65 rational points in the projective plane. When Q is anisotropic, use the same norm normal form Q=X²+XY+32Y². Replacing Z by Z plus a binary linear form reduces C modulo Q to Y²(aX+bY) by ordinary monic division in X. The remainder is not zero, and rescaling Z allows one representative for every projective (a:b). This covers 65 models, not necessarily inequivalent.

Each model has exactly 66 projective rational points. Enumerate its node and the 65 directions, taking Z=C/Q. A second construction enumerates the full projective plane and tests the implicit equation instead. The resulting projective sets agree. Section 4 tests every possible line at infinity and proves none yields an affine Sidon 66-set (87,360 eligible model/charts in this branch). Thus the potentially troublesome isolated rational node cannot add an extra point to a 65-point parametrization and produce a counterexample.

## 3. Geometrically smooth cubics, including embeddings without a rational flex

A cubic with at least 66 rational points certainly has a rational point O. As an abstract genus-one curve with origin O, take a generalized Weierstrass model

  y²+(a x+c)y=x³+d x²+b x+f .

This is an abstract K-isomorphism, not the assertion that a plane cubic with no rational flex admits this equation by a projectivity. We restore all embeddings below. Standard facts used are the Weierstrass representation and the identification Pic⁰(E)≅E sending [T]−[O] to T; see J. Silverman, *The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves*, III §§1,3. For the same elliptic-curve approach to projective cubic classes see R. Schoof, [Nonsingular plane cubic curves over finite fields](https://doi.org/10.1016/0097-3165(87)90003-3), JCTA 46 (1987). We do not rely on a table of cubic classes.

* If a≠0 use x↦u²x, y↦u³y to set a=1. An x-translation removes c, and a constant y-translation removes b. Then y↦y+s x changes d by s²+s, leaving d to be chosen as 0 or 32. The nonsingular models have f≠0. One f per orbit of squaring suffices: squaring all coordinates is binary linear and the trace of d is unchanged, so one may restore d by y↦y+s x. This is a field semilinear equivalence, not necessarily an isomorphism over the identity of K; it preserves the question over all charts and Sidon structure.
* If a=0, smoothness forces c≠0 (otherwise both partials vanish at a point over the algebraic closure). The same scaling sets c to one of the three cube classes 1,2,4, where 2 is primitive. A change y↦y+s x removes d. Translating x by r=s² and then adding s x to y to restore d=0 changes b by s⁴+c s; constant changes of f are harmless at this step. Choose b in each additive coset of this image (four cosets for c=1, one for c=2 and for c=4). Finally y↦y+t varies f by t²+ct, of index two, so choose either f=0 or f=32c².

This gives 38 possibly redundant coefficient tuples, with no completeness assumption about a database. The verifier counts rational points of each model by testing all 4096 affine pairs and adding the point at infinity. Any model with fewer than 66 projective points is discarded.

Every plane embedding of the original smooth cubic is by its complete degree-three hyperplane linear system; Riemann–Roch gives dimension 3. Its divisor class is 2[O]+[T] for a unique T∈E(K) because subtracting 2[O] leaves a rational degree-one class, whose one-dimensional space defines a unique rational point. Translation on E shifts T by a member of 3E(K); pulling back the two line bundles and choosing bases then gives projectively equivalent images. We need only cover T modulo 3E(K), with no assertion that these give inequivalent embeddings. The verifier computes the full rational addition law on each surviving model using the chord formulas and covers every point by the listed cosets.

For T=O we use the Weierstrass model itself. Otherwise set T=(u,v), −T=(u,w), where w=v+au+c. As a basis of L(2[O]+[T]) take 1,x and z=(y+w)/(x+u). The function z has a simple pole at O, since x,y have pole orders 2,3, and at T. For distinct T and −T the numerator cancels the denominator's simple zero at −T; when T=−T the denominator has a double zero there and the numerator a simple zero (the curve is smooth with vertical tangent). They have no other poles. Independence follows by comparing their poles at T and O. The map on affine points is (x,z); O goes to [1:0:0] and T to [0:1:0]. At −T distinct from T, the finite slope is (x²+b+a y)/(a x+c) by differentiating the Weierstrass equation. As an independent check, substitute y=(x+u)z+w into the cubic, cancel x+u, and get the implicit affine equation

  (x+u)z²+(a x+c)z+x²+(d+u)x+b+aw+ud+u²=0 .

Its projective points are enumerated again from this equation. Including the flex and nonflex cases yields 40 possibly redundant surviving models (24 nonzero T). Their projective point sets are passed to the two chart certificates independently.

## 4. All affine charts and the counting certificate

A projectivity carries a chosen line at infinity to some line H, and on that affine chart two choices of remaining coordinates differ by an invertible K-affine map. It therefore suffices to enumerate the 4161 projective linear forms H up to scalar for *each* model, discard its points on H, and divide two complementary independent coordinates by H. Frobenius in the prior semilinear reduction also preserves all binary sums. The two checkers take the first or the last nonzero coordinate of H as pivot, respectively.

Given a chart supporting h≥66 points, form the 4-uniform hypergraph of all four-point zero sums on those h points. Write e for the number of edges, d_i for the degrees in descending order. A Sidon 66-subset must delete h−66 vertices meeting every edge, which would imply d_1+...+d_(h−66)≥e. On all 148,673 eligible smooth charts the opposite strict inequality holds, with minimum deficit 18. On all 87,360 eligible nodal charts the minimum deficit is 150. Counts refer to models and charts, not inequivalent curves; redundant cover is intentional.

First implementation: let m_s count pairs of difference s. For every pair {i,j}, add m_s−1 to the counters of i and j. Each four-point zero sum is counted in its three complementary pair partitions, so the resulting counters equal 3d_i. The sum of m_s−1 over all pairs is 6e. These integer scalings give the claimed strict comparisons. Independently, the second program loops over i>j>k, tests by value lookup whether there is an l<k giving zero XOR, and increments exactly the four vertex degrees once. The two programs must return the same number of charts and the same exact minimum deficit *for each model*, not merely the same global yes/no. Together with Sections 1–3 this proves the theorem.

## Replay, references, and scope

Run `python3 -O run.py` (Python 3.10+, GNU-compatible C++17 on x86-64 Linux; set CXX if required). No package or network access is required. Every Python guard uses explicit exceptions, and the C++ sources reject NDEBUG. The sharp positive control is direct, not inferred from a zero-only search. The displayed bounds use only integer arithmetic.

The ellipse+nucleus example and completeness among sets containing the whole conic are due to [Nagy, JCTA 212 (2025), 106001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcta.2024.106001). The point here is to cover all polynomials of degree at most three and recover the unique extremal set even without prescribing a conic. As of 2026-11-21 we also checked results on [algebraic curves as Sidon sets in generalized Jacobians](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04917), [plane cubic classification](https://doi.org/10.1016/0097-3165(87)90003-3), and 2026H2 literature on APN and additive sets. Neither an elliptic curve's own group nor its Jacobian is the additive group used here. This packet claims the theorem, not universal priority.
