# Independent finite-case review

**Verdict: PASS.** For weights with at most two distinct nonzero absolute values, the proposed bound $K\rho^2\le1$ holds for every finite multiplicity and every real ratio of the two nonzero magnitudes. Equality occurs only when the vector is all zero, including the empty case. This is not a proof for arbitrary weights, biased bits, or a uniformly chosen subset of fixed size. The frozen source reviewed has SHA-256 aa9fc65adf6472d0dcce8b581be2e3f2b8b2dbc1f413adba2f945a2fab0b20c7. The following is internal AI review, not external peer review. The finite checks are illustrative, not the proof.

1. **Normalization and injectivity.** For a fair bit $U$, $-aU=a(1-U)-a$; complementing any chosen bits preserves their joint independence. Deleting zeros changes neither the law nor its support, and rescaling preserves both $K$ and $\rho$. If the two magnitudes are incommensurable, $a(i-i')=b(j'-j)$ forces both differences to vanish. For a commensurable pair, coprimality after rescaling implies its kernel in $\mathbb Z^2$ is generated by $(b,-a)$. A nonzero displacement in the rectangle therefore requires $n\ge b$ and $m\ge a$, with the multiplicities assigned respectively to $a,b$. In all injective cases the density factors exactly and the maximal mass is $c_nc_m$.
2. **Counting even at the rectangle's boundary.** Intersecting a coset of the kernel with $[0,n]\times[0,m]$ gives a consecutive interval in its integer parameter. It has one first point in the direction $(b,-a)$. Points lacking a predecessor are exactly those with $i<b$ or $j>m-a$. In the overlapping case their union has cardinality $b(m+1)+a(n+1)-ab$. This proof also works at $n=b$, $m=a$, when exactly two opposite corners coincide and there is just one lost point. In a fixed sum fiber $y$ lies in one residue class mod $a$ (vacuous if $a=1$), and each choice of $y$ determines *at most* one $x$. Bounding that $x$ mass by $c_n$ and summing over the entire residue class gives $\rho\le c_nR_m(a)$; exchange $a,b$ and $x,y$ for the other bound.
3. **Independent certificate of the three binomial constants.** Let $z_s=\binom{2s}s/4^s$. For every $s\ge0$, $z_s^2\le 1/(3s+1)$. Indeed the initial value is exact and $z_{s+1}/z_s=(2s+1)/(2s+2)$. The induction step follows because
   $$(3s+1)(2s+2)^2-(3s+4)(2s+1)^2=s\ge0.$$
   Since $c_{2s-1}=c_{2s}=z_s$, it suffices to bound even indices $2s\ge2$, where $(2s+1)/(3s+1)\le3/4$, $(2s+1/2)/(3s+1)<2/3$, and $(2s+2)/(3s+1)\le1$. Thus the three manuscript bounds hold without any limiting approximation. Independently checking the manuscript's Wallis/Stirling version gives the stated difference $+1$ and the limit $2/\pi$.
4. **Residues and endpoint collisions.** Convolution with a fair bit averages the cyclic probability vector with a permutation of itself, so its largest entry cannot increase. At $j=d$, only 0 and $d$ coalesce; their combined coefficient 2 is at most the central coefficient, including equality for $d=2$. At $j=2d$, split the bits into halves. Their total is equal in law to $d+B-B'$ where $B,B'$ are independent $\mathrm{Bin}(d,1/2)$ variables. Modulo $d$ the difference distribution is the autocorrelation of their cyclic probability vector, maximal at zero by Cauchy–Schwarz even if there are ties. At residue zero precisely $0,d,2d$ contribute, giving $R_{2d}(d)=(2+\binom{2d}{d})/4^d$. At $d=2$ this equals $1/2$ and $dR_{2d}(d)^2=1/2$. For $d\ge3$ the induction above gives $\sqrt d\,c_{2d}<1/\sqrt3<3/5$; the extra $2\sqrt d/4^d$ is at most its value at 3, less than $1/10$. Hence the scaled mass is less than $7/10<1/\sqrt2$.
5. **Final split and strictness.** With $a,b\ge2$, multiply the respective squared fiber bounds by $a(n+1)$ and $b(m+1)$. Since $R_m(a)\le c_a$ and $R_n(b)\le c_b$ in the overlap range, each gives less than $1/2$, and together they bound $K\rho^2$. With $a=1$ the integer support count is $n+bm+1$. When $b\le n\le2b-1$, $K\le b(m+2)$, so $K\rho^2\le (m+2)c_m^2\,b c_b^2<2/3$. When $n\ge2b$ use $\rho\le c_n$ as well as $\rho\le c_mR_n(b)$. The allocated terms are $n+1/2$ and $bm+1/2$: multiplying by $\rho^2$ gives first less than $2/3$ and second at most $(m+1/2)c_m^2\,bR_n(b)^2<1/3$. The inequality $bm+1/2\le b(m+1/2)$ is in the correct direction. The finite regimes meet exactly at $n=2b$. One nonzero magnitude uses the binomial bound; the all-zero case gives $K=\rho=1$.
