**First moment and quantifiers.** Every EF allocation has all bundles nonempty if $m>0$, since the individual costs are strictly positive a.s. At $m=n$ all rows must hold their least expensive single; expectation $n!/n^n$ is exact. If there are $r$ nonsingletons, the $n-r=s$ remaining agents own specific singletons in each chosen assignment. Necessary ordinal events have respective probabilities $1/s$ and $1/\binom{s+b_i}{b_i}$, as each owner must be at least as satisfied with its full bundle as with the cheapest singleton. These row events are independent for one assignment; no independence between competing assignments is needed. The factorials from labeling cancel as in (A1). Precisely $1\le r\le m-n$ and the ordered positive composition of the excess has $\binom{m-n-1}{r-1}$ choices. I independently recomputed $J_a,J_{aa},J_u$, including the $+(1+u)/(1-a)$ in $J_a$. The unique interior maximizer, continuity to $u=0$ after $a=vu$, $J_*(0)=-1$, and the uniform additive Stirling error $O(\log n)$ give exponentially small probabilities on compact fixed-gap intervals. All random data used here are ordinal, so a distribution changing with $n$ causes no obstruction.

**Ownership and local weak limits.** Whenever a certified row-prefix item is the earliest singleton for that row, every nonsingleton costs more and every other singleton costs more than that item. This forces ownership by the row. At a later request for a singleton, if any earlier prefix position is singleton, take the earliest: it must belong both to the current row and to a distinct previous requester by induction, which is impossible. Thus a twice-requested item cannot be a singleton. Counting all compulsory nonsingleton items gives at most $m-s=m-n+r\le2(m-n)$; strict excess after dividing by $m$, not $n$, yields (B1)'s comparison.

For fixed $K,t$, row lengths are independent of the random order permutation; they are not independent marks on different ranks of that row. At each newly exposed item, type $(j,\ell)$ is exclusive within one row and has conditional probability $\pi_j^{(m)}/m+O_{B,K}(m^{-2})$ on all bounded prior avoidance histories. Avoidance conditioning factorizes across distinct unexposed rows. Hence fresh counts at a queried item have independent joint Poisson limits of means $\pi_j/c$ for each available rank. Revealing rows already found requires following their other uniformly sampled distinct labels, and accidental identifications in any bounded one- or two-root neighborhood are $O_{B,K}(1/m)$. Bound the size of an unbounded exploration to depth $2t$ by the sum of numbers of simple paths. A length-$2h$ path uses $h$ different rows, each retaining two prescribed labels with probability at most $K(K-1)/(m(m-1))$, and there are at most $n^hm^h$ label sequences; one more step contributes at most a factor $nK/m$. This gives uniform tightness and permits taking the cap off **after** the market limit. The requests on distinct child branches in the limiting tree are independent when their common parent is removed by the “other row” rule. Thus the root count is Poisson with the stated recursion, and two distinct roots converge independently, enough for concentration of their empirical obstruction. No unproved independence assertion for finite unbounded neighborhoods or infinite depth is needed.

**Prefix marks.** A direct independent calculation for uniform costs, conditional on the value of the $\ell$-th order statistic, gives the same answer: the $N=\ell-1$ smaller observations, scaled by that value and disregarding order, are iid uniform. The two least sum to more than 1 if all exceed $1/2$, or exactly one $v<1/2$ is drawn and each of the others is $>1-v$. For $\ell\ge3$ the total chance is $2^{-N}+N\int_0^{1/2}v^{N-1}\,dv=2^{1-N}$. Ranks 1 and 2 are always safe. For positive-on-every-neighborhood, atomless $F$, its generalized inverse has $F(Q(1/m))=1/m$ and $Q(1/m)\downarrow0$ by continuity and the origin condition. The regular-variation comparisons at the *two fixed multipliers* $y\pm\delta$ suffice for the sequential inversion at any $y_m\to y>0$; plateaus or lack of a density cause no problem. The exponential-order coupling is valid jointly for the first fixed $K$, the random order independent of the ordered values, and the strict sum comparison has limiting tie probability zero. The convergence here is only for each fixed law, as claimed.
