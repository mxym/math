# Rigidity of the single-deletion incidence relation

The matrix here has *only* inputs of length n and outputs of length n−1. There are no other observed ranks, no probabilities, and no within-rank Hamming/substitution edges. It is not the full word poset to which Erdős–Sziklai–Torney's Theorem 2 applies. Unique reconstruction of a row from all its labeled neighbors, on its own, does not prove rigidity under independent relabeling.

**Theorem.** Let q=|Σ|≥2. Permute inputs g:Σⁿ→Σⁿ and outputs b:Σⁿ⁻¹→Σⁿ⁻¹ independently, preserving the unweighted incidence y≼x in both directions. For n≥3 the only such pairs are a simultaneous uniform alphabet permutation and an optional simultaneous reversal; the group is S_q × C₂. For n=2 one can reverse mixed words separately for each unordered letter pair, giving (C₂)^{q choose 2} ⋊ S_q. In particular the binary answer is four for every n≥2. At n=1 the sole output is empty and g may be any alphabet permutation; an exactly-one-deletion experiment is not defined at n=0. The input group alone is the same as the pair group. These groups are also exact for the equally likely one-deletion channel since every displayed generator preserves deletion multiplicity. No claim is made for an arbitrary fixed number of deletions.

**1. Reading the counts.** A top word has exactly as many distinct neighbors as it has constant-letter runs. Indeed deleting positions i<j gives the same result precisely when the segment from i to j is constant (compare output coordinates i through j−1). Therefore the top constants aⁿ for a∈Σ are intrinsic, and each has sole neighbor aⁿ⁻¹. For n≥2 these neighbors are distinct. Normalize by the inverse simultaneous alphabet permutation to fix constants on both sides.

Join two distinct vertices on either side if they have a common neighbor on the opposite side. With m=n (top) or m=n−1 (bottom), two words now are adjacent exactly when their longest common subsequence (LCS) has length m−1. On the top this is immediate. On the bottom, deleting two different indices of a length-(m+1) common superword leaves a common word of length m−1 once both are deleted. Conversely a common subsequence of length m−1 gives a common superword of length m+1 by merging the two exceptional letters into their respective gaps (choose either order if the same gap). The graph distance on either side is
     
    dist(u,v)=m−LCS(u,v).

Each move deletes a symbol and inserts one, so after t moves at least m−t original occurrences survive in their original order; this lower bounds the distance. Conversely mark copies of a common subsequence of length L in the source and the target. When L<m, delete any unmarked source letter, insert one unmarked target letter at a position consistent with the marked target order, and mark this new letter, increasing the size of the marked common word by one. Subsequent moves do not delete marks. This yields at most m−L moves (an unchanged word need not be counted as a move). This is the familiar fixed-length Levenshtein distance; see [Bar-Lev–Etzion–Yaakobi, IEEE Trans. IT 69 (2023), introduction](https://yaakobi.net.technion.ac.il/files/2024/02/77_Levenshtein_Ball_IT23.pdf). Each co-neighbor graph is carried to itself by its respective permutation; distance to aᵐ is m minus the number of copies of a. Hence all letter counts on each side are preserved after normalization.

**2. Binary rigidity, n≥2.** Write the alphabet {0,1}. Among words with exactly one 1 on each side, the incidences form the path

    T₁—B₁—T₂—B₂— … —B_{n−1}—Tₙ,

where the subscript gives the location of the 1. Indeed an input T_j can yield B_j or B_{j−1} by deleting a zero, whenever the subscript is in range. The permutations preserve this path and are therefore either jointly the identity here or its reflection. Compose with simultaneous word reversal if necessary to fix the path pointwise. This includes n=2: the path has three vertices, and reflection swaps the two distinct top words while fixing the single bottom word.

Inductively suppose all bottom words with k−1 ones are fixed, for k≥2. For a top word x with k ones, write z=n−k and represent x by the multiset M of the numbers of zeros preceding its individual ones (each value belongs to {0,…,z}); with z fixed this is a bijection. The bottom neighbors having k−1 ones are exactly the multiset values M−{j}, for each distinct j in the support of M, with repeats ignored. This *set* determines M. If it has one member then M has just one occupied value and k−1≥1 reveals that value. If it has at least two members, at each j the maximum multiplicity across all members is exactly its multiplicity in M, since some deletion removes a different element. Thus all top words with k ones are fixed.

Next let a bottom word containing k ones have its n−1−k zeros distributed in the k+1 gaps around the ones, with counts v=(v₀,…,v_k). Its top neighbors having k ones, all now fixed, have gap vectors exactly v+e_j, 0≤j≤k. As k≥1, the coordinatewise minimum recovers v. All bottom words of weight k are consequently fixed whenever that layer exists. Starting at k=2 and ending at k=n proves rigidity (all weight-zero words and both single-one layers were already fixed). Count preservation justifies using the indicated portions of each observed neighborhood at every step. No positional probability or multiplicity is used.

**3. More letters.** Fix a letter pair {a,b}. Count preservation restricts the normalized maps to its binary two-layer incidence, so the restricted top action is either the identity or reversal by step 2, with one sign s_ab. For any top word x let u be its projection to {a,b}, retaining these letters in order. For every full-length binary test word y over {a,b}, LCS(x,y)=|u| exactly when u≼y. The family of such y determines u uniquely: if u is not contained in w, greedily match the longest possible prefix of u in w and append to w as many copies of a letter different from the next unmatched letter as needed to reach length n. This produces a binary test word that contains w and avoids u. Different words either disagree in one direction of containment or vice versa. Since |u| is fixed by letter counts and g preserves LCS, the projection of gx to each pair is the original projection or its reversal with sign s_ab. Indeed, applying g to the family of qualifying tests for x gives the family for gx, and reversing a test reverses the candidate subsequence along with it.

If three distinct letters are available and n≥3, test all six words made of three nonempty homogeneous blocks with these letters, in all orders; assign n−2 copies to one specified letter and one to each other. Projecting pairwise reveals the total ordering of their blocks. For the image to exist all three transformed comparisons must be consistent; in particular the order of the first occurrence of the three letters cannot contain a directed cycle. If exactly one edge comparison is reversed, order those two endpoint letters first and last, with the third between, giving a cycle after the flip. If two edges are reversed, apply simultaneous reversal to all three comparisons and reduce to the one-edge case. Thus every triangle of letter pairs has uniform sign. All pairs have the same sign because any two intersecting pairs lie in a triangle and disjoint pairs can be joined by an intersecting pair. This includes q=3 and needs no triangle when q=2.

Finally, all pair projections with the letter counts uniquely specify x: label the occurrences of each letter in their internal order. Comparing the labeled occurrences of any two distinct letters is read from the corresponding projection, and occurrences of one letter are already ordered. Hence all occurrences have a prescribed total order. The normalized g is therefore a single identity or reversal. The corresponding b is unique, since the sets of input superwords of length n separate bottom labels by the same greedy argument (use any alternative letter). Conversely all claimed symmetries preserve incidence and, for uniform single deletion, preserve the number of positions giving each output by the matching index bijection.

**4. Small n.** At n=2, fixing all alphabet letters fixes the repeated top words and permits just an independent swap of ab and ba for each unordered pair because these two have the identical pair of lower neighbors {a,b}; those are all the unrestricted choices. The binary group is then C₂×C₂ (its alphabet transposition and the sole pair reversal commute). At n=1 every permutation of the q inputs is possible, the single output fixed. The two parts have different cardinalities for q≥2, and we only assert color-preserving actions in the first place.

**Optional simple consequence, separate from the main classification.** If exactly one index i is deleted with strictly positive probabilities p_i independent of the input symbols, summing to one, the support is as above. For n≥3 all alphabet permutations still preserve the law, while reversal does so precisely when p_i=p_{n+1−i} for all i. Necessity: the word with one b at i and the rest a, a≠b, yields aⁿ⁻¹ with probability p_i; under reversal the corresponding probability is p_{n+1−i}. Palindromic weights suffice by reversing the deleted index. The same statement holds for binary n=2. This does not cover arbitrary zero probabilities.
