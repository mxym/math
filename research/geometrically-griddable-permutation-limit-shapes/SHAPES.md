# The full-sequence scaling limit for geometrically griddable permutation classes

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal C$ be an infinite hereditary subclass of any fixed geometric grid class. A uniformly chosen permutation of length $n$ from $\mathcal C$, with its grid lines forgotten, converges in distribution as a permuton, along the full sequence of lengths. Its law is a finite mixture: choose a finite directed route as specified below, cut $[0,1]$ in successive durations of law $\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,\ldots,1)$, and on each interval use the fixed vector of cell frequencies given by a primitive transfer matrix. Apply the oriented grid-coordinate pushforward to this profile. The result is supported on a bounded number of straight segments, uniform on each segment piece with its prescribed mass, conditionally on the route and durations. The limit need not be deterministic (for instance the union of the increasing and decreasing classes). As a byproduct $|\mathcal C_n|\sim c\rho^n n^{d-1}$ for some $c>0$, $d\ge1$ and $\rho\ge1$.

## A canonical finite path presentation with primitive components

By the refinement of Albert–Atkinson–Bouvel–Ruškuc–Vatter (AABRV), we may work with a finite partial multiplication matrix: assign a sign to each row and each column such that their product is the cell sign. The alphabet $A$ consists of its nonempty cells. A word codes a point in the designated cell for each letter, moving successively away from the oriented corner. Along each shared column/row the position/value order is therefore forward word time for positive direction, reverse for negative. The map $\phi$ to ungridded permutations respects deletion of letters. Two words encode the same *gridded* permutation exactly when they differ by commutations of letters in distinct rows and columns (AABRV Prop. 7.1).

Fix a total order on $A$, and choose the lexicographically least word in each trace. Its finite scanner has blocked-letter set $R$ (initially empty). Reading $a\in R$ is forbidden. When an allowed $a$ is read, remove all blocks on letters dependent on $a$ (including $a$ itself); for each independent $b$ retain its old block and additionally block it if $a>b$. Indeed a nonleast word has a factor $bwa$ with $b>a$ and $a$ commuting with $b$ and every letter of $w$: at the first nonleast position choose a smaller available letter in the remaining labeled dependence poset; it has no predecessor among the intervening letters of the given representative. Conversely such a factor admits a lex-improving move. The update detects precisely those obstructions, and, crucially, after a valid $a$-transition, the next $a$ is allowed and fixes the new $R$.

We still need one gridding per **ungridded** permutation. Use AABRV's canonical gridding from their proof of Theorem 8.1 (lexicographically maximize the column totals, then the row totals). Their auxiliary language $J$ on marked/unmarked copies of $A$ is downward closed under subsequences. For any word coding a permutation of $\mathcal C$, its gridding is noncanonical iff the word admits a marking in $J$ with at least one marked position. More precisely, for an alternative strictly preferred gridding the entries newly in the first improved column (or row) are marked; some such entry must exist. In a subword retaining a marked entry the original comparison at that column (or row) persists; with none retained the identical comparison witnesses the unmarked subword. Thus their downward-closure statement does not require turning an arbitrarily marked letter into an unmarked one. We also enforce $w\in\phi^{-1}(\mathcal C)$, itself downward closed.

Here is the extra structure needed to avoid periodic main terms. For each $a$, let $D_a$ be the set of word pairs $(u,v)$ such that $uav$ has a marking in $J$ with the indicated $a$ marked; other positions may also be marked. This is a downset under separate subsequence deletion in the two words, keeping the distinguished position. By the finite-product form of Higman's lemma, its complement is the upward closure of finitely many pairs of words (possibly none). The complement of $\phi^{-1}(\mathcal C)$ similarly has a finite word basis. Choose $k$ dominating every individual word length in these finitely many bases; write $T(w)$ for its set of subsequences of length at most $k$, including the empty word. The truth of $(u,v)\in D_a$ depends only on $T(u),T(v)$; no synchronization of independent embeddings is necessary in two separate strings. Class membership depends only on $T(w)$. Appending/prepending $a$ induces well-defined updates $P\mapsto P\cdot a$ and $S\mapsto a\cdot S$ on types, by appending/prepending to subsequences of length $<k$ and adjoining the old type.

Use states $(P,S,R)$ at cuts, the types of the prefix and remaining suffix and the scanner state. Draw a labeled edge $a$ from $(P,S,R)$ to $(P',S',R')$ iff $P'=P\cdot a$, $S=a\cdot S'$, the scanner allows that step, and the local witness $(P,S')$ is NOT in the type relation for $D_a$. Initial states require empty prefix type and empty scanner; terminal states require empty suffix type and final prefix type consistent with $\mathcal C$. Unproductive states may be removed. From a word on an accepting path, all types are forced forward/backward from the endpoints, and all scanner states are forced forward, so accepting paths are unambiguous. Conversely any canonical word has one such path. Thus accepting paths and ungridded permutations are in bijection length by length.

On any internal edge of a strongly connected component, $P=P'$ and $S=S'$ by monotonicity ($P$ only grows, $S$ only shrinks). At its target the very same letter makes a self-loop: both type tests and the local obstruction test are unchanged, and the scanner update with the same letter fixes the new state as proved above. This loop also lies on accepting paths whenever the state does. Hence every component with a cycle is primitive. The point is not that every regular language has such a presentation.

## Routes, bridges and times

For completeness give the finite transfer argument. Partition accepting paths by their initial/terminal states, their sequence of visited components in the acyclic condensation, and the particular labeled edges passing between successive components. These finite routes fix all component entry/exit vertices $u_i,v_i$. Let $s$ be the number of crossing edges, $A_i$ the nonnegative integer matrix counting internal edges with multiplicity. For fixed lengths $\ell_i$ inside the components, the count is $\prod_i (A_i^{\ell_i})_{u_i v_i}$ with $\sum\ell_i=n-s$, including the identity matrix at length zero. A singleton without a loop contributes only at zero. Routes containing no cycle are bounded-length.

For a cyclic route let $r=\max_i\rho(A_i)>0$, with $d$ components attaining it. Their normalized counts $r^{-j}(A_i^j)_{u_i v_i}$ are bounded and converge to $c_i=v_i^R(u_i)v_i^L(v_i)>0$ by primitivity and left/right normalization $(v_i^L)^Tv_i^R=1$. For each smaller component the corresponding normalized count sequence is summable, geometrically in its tail (the acyclic singleton contributes $1$). Write $z_i$ for its sum. Each $z_i>0$. Then the route count is asymptotic to
$$K r^n n^{d-1},\qquad K=\frac{r^{-s}}{(d-1)!}\prod_{i:\rho(A_i)=r}c_i\prod_{i:\rho(A_i)<r}z_i.$$
Indeed fix all subcritical lengths and distribute the remaining length among the $d$ maximal components. On a uniform weak composition, their normalized lengths approach the uniform distribution on the simplex by Riemann sums (for $d=1$ the sole duration tends to one); each unnormalized part tends to infinity in probability. Their product weights are bounded and converge in probability to $\prod c_i$. The number of such compositions is $\binom{n-s-\sum_{sub}\ell_i+d-1}{d-1}$ when feasible, bounded uniformly by a constant times $n^{d-1}$. Dominated convergence using the summable subcritical weights proves the count formula, as well as the same formula multiplied by the uniform-simplex average of any bounded continuous function of the normalized critical lengths. The subcritical total length is tight by the same summable tail bound.

Globally only routes of highest $r$, and among those highest $d$, contribute in the limit; their limiting probabilities are proportional to their positive $K$. This yields the asserted count asymptotic and full-sequence Dirichlet durations. No periodic residue classes are involved.

On a long internal path with fixed entry/exit vertices in a primitive component of growth $r$, put transition probability $v^R(y)/(r v^R(x))$ on each individual internal labeled edge $x\to y$. It is stochastic and its stationary vertex probabilities are $v^L(x)v^R(x)$. The resulting Markov chain, conditioned to end at the prescribed vertex after $j$ steps, is uniform on those paths by telescoping, and the probability of the conditioning is bounded away from zero for large $j$. By the finite-state ergodic theorem, cumulative counts $N_a(m)$ along the unconditioned chain satisfy
$$\max_{m\le j}|N_a(m)-m f_a|/j\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely},\qquad
f_a=\frac1r\sum_{e:x\to y\text{ labeled }a}v^L(x)v^R(y).$$
The maximal assertion follows by separating finitely many small $m$ from the eventual pointwise law $N_a(m)/m\to f_a$. This convergence holds in probability after terminal conditioning. Conditional on route and lengths the component paths are uniform independently. There are finitely many, each critical length tends to infinity in probability, and the crossing and subcritical lengths are $o_p(n)$. Hence the vector of cumulative cell frequencies divided by $n$ converges in law uniformly on time $[0,1]$ to the integral of the stated successive constant profiles.

## From a cell-word profile to a permuton

For a profile $p_a(t)$ write $C_i(t)=\int_0^t\sum_{a\text{ in column }i}p_a(z)dz$ and define $R_j(t)$ similarly for row $j$. Send mass $p_{ij}(t)dt$ to the point with $x$ coordinate $\sum_{h<i} C_h(1)+C_i(t)$ for positive column direction and $\sum_{h<i}C_h(1)+C_i(1)-C_i(t)$ for negative direction; do the corresponding row operation for $y$. The two marginals are uniform, even when some strips carry zero mass: a strip emits $dC_i(t)$ and its cumulative transform is Lebesgue on that strip. Continuous convergence of cumulative letter masses uniformly on $[0,1]$ implies weak convergence of this pushforward: each strip curve converges uniformly, and each letter measure converges weakly by its continuous cumulative distribution. Finite words on intervals of length $1/n$ give their empirical permutons to within $O(1/n)$ in coordinate transport, since the correct mass occupies each rank square. The continuous mapping theorem proves the claim. A constant profile sends each letter to a straight segment with uniform conditional mass, a positive-mass cell having nonzero speeds in both coordinates. Zero-duration phases and zero-frequency letters contribute nothing.

A limited number of components or a section of a regular language, without the canonical unambiguity and primitive-component checks, would not by itself imply this result. The Perron laws and uniform weak-composition limits are standard; the application turns on the finite marked-witness test of the canonical encoding.
