# Permuton limits of first-order position interpretations of regular words

## Statement

Let a trimmed finite deterministic automaton accept a language with arbitrarily long words, and suppose that every strongly connected component of maximal spectral radius is primitive. The adjacency matrices count directed edges with their label multiplicities. Let two fixed first-order formulas of finite linearly ordered, letter-labelled words, each with two free point variables, specify strict total orders on the positions of each accepted word. For a uniform accepted length-$n$ word take the permutation specified by its two orders. Then its permuton converges **in law along all lengths**. The limit is described by the following finite mixture:

* Choose one of finitely many accepting component routes and finite boundary contexts according to fixed probabilities.
* On a route with $d$ dominant phases choose $\Theta$ independently of the context given the route, uniformly on the $(d-1)$-simplex (with $\Theta=1$ if $d=1$).
* For phase $i$ there are finitely many local types $a$ with fixed probabilities $f_{ia}$. Conditional on the choices above, the permuton is the sum, with weights $\Theta_i f_{ia}$, of uniform parametrized straight segments. The coordinates of each endpoint are fixed linear functions of $\Theta$ given the context, explicitly (3) below.

Thus even the number of segments is bounded by a fixed constant. This is not a statement of a deterministic shape, a discrete distribution of permutons, or a geometrically griddable class. In particular it holds for the uniform permutation in **every infinite small permutation class** (upper growth strictly smaller than $\kappa$, the real root of $x^3-2x^2-1$). For the regular-language theorem the distribution on output permutations is with the multiplicities of their input words; injectivity is needed only for the small-class application.

## 1. Route law and conditioned walks

A route specifies the accepting state, the successive SCCs, the entry and exit vertex in each, and the intervening crossing edges. These give a finite disjoint partition by determinism. Denote the largest productive spectral radius by $r\ge1$. Along a route meeting radius $r$, let $d$ be the number of such components. For fixed lengths $j$ the internal paths in the components are independent uniform paths between their specified endpoints. Their counts are $(M^j)_{uv}$. In a primitive radius-$r$ component these entries divided by $r^j$ are bounded and converge to strictly positive constants. In a subcritical component they are summable after division by $r^j$, indeed exponentially bounded (including the trivial acyclic case). Crossing edges have fixed total length.

For clarity, fix the entire paths in the subcritical components and sum the product over compositions of the remaining length into $d$ phase lengths. The normalized number, divided by $n^{d-1}r^n$, tends to the product of the Perron constants divided by $(d-1)!$, times $r^{-b}$, where $b$ is the total fixed length including crossings. The same Riemann sum with a continuous test function of the length fractions gives its uniform-simplex average: bounded entries near the boundary are negligible (for $d=1$ the sole length tends to infinity). The summable path majorant from the subcritical radii permits summing over all choices of subcritical paths. Routes without $r$ are exponentially negligible; among the others only the largest $d$ survives. Thus route probabilities converge, normalized phase lengths converge to the stated Dirichlet law independently of the tight subcritical paths conditional on the selected route, and every phase length tends to infinity in probability. This also gives eventual nonemptiness. This route count is standard Perron transfer, not a separate novelty claim.

In component $C$ take a positive right Perron vector $u$. The chain assigning each edge $x\to y$ probability $u_y/(ru_x)$ is primitive and has positive invariant distribution $\pi$. Conditional on going from the entry $b$ to the exit $c$ in $j$ steps it is exactly the uniform bridge. The probability of this endpoint condition is bounded below for all sufficiently large $j$. In a long bridge a fixed initial and a fixed terminal window converge jointly to independent windows, one grown from $b$ forward and the other from $c$ backwards with stationary reversed edge probabilities $\pi(x)P(x\xrightarrow{e}y)/\pi(y)$. Indeed the intervening transition probability tends to the stationary mass of the initial vertex of the terminal window, while the denominator tends to $\pi(c)$. These limits are uniform in the finitely many entry-exit choices.

## 2. A buffer that erases intervening two-mark context

Put two flags on the letter alphabet. For each comparison, the marked words carrying exactly one occurrence of each flag in distinct positions for which the first point precedes the second in this output order form an FO language (quantify the unique marked positions). By the first-order/aperiodic monoid theorem there is a common finite aperiodic monoid $M$ and a morphism recognizing both tests. Write $\eta$ for unflagged products, and $a^{[h]}$ for the image of a letter $a$ marked with flag $h$. Only the two comparisons, not the input language, need be aperiodic.

In each dominant component fix a vertex $v$. Unmarked products of its internal closed walks form a submonoid $S$ of $M$. Choose an idempotent $e$ in its minimal nonempty two-sided ideal (take an idempotent power there). Then
$$ eSe=\{e\}. \tag{1} $$
Indeed for $t\in eSe$, minimality gives $e=xty$ for $x,y\in S$. Consequently $(exe)t(eye)=e$ in the finite monoid $eSe$. A product equal to its identity has only invertible factors in a finite monoid, and aperiodicity forces every unit to be the identity. Fix a nonempty edge walk $B:v\to v$ realizing $e$: if only an empty representative were known, $e$ would be the identity of $S$ and (1) would give $S=\{e\}$, so any positive-length closed walk suffices. Distinct nonoverlapping occurrences of this edge word enclose a $v$-to-$v$ walk, so $B(\cdots)B$ has unmarked product $e$ whenever both occurrences are included. An occurrence is always looked for *entirely inside the current component segment*. It need not be FO recognizable from the input labels.

There are constants $m,\epsilon>0$ so that from any vertex of $C$ a full $B$ occurs within the next $m$ edges with probability at least $\epsilon$ (reach $v$, then take $B$). Thus the probability that a window of $h$ edges misses $B$ is at most $(1-\epsilon)^{\lfloor h/m\rfloor}$. The bridge bound above applies by conditioning from the entry, without assuming independence of windows. Take $h=\lceil K\log(n+2)\rceil$ with $K$ large for all the components. By a union bound, with probability tending to one, every $h$-window fully inside every long dominant segment contains a complete $B$. We may assume its length is $>10h$ for each, by the route law. Prefix and suffix notations below are all words of **edges**, evaluated using their input letters.

For each dominant segment let $A$ be the product before its first complete occurrence of $B$; let $D$ be the product after its last. For arbitrary distinct occurrences ties by starting or ending index cannot arise because the edge word has fixed length. Except on the negligible event just described, these first and last occurrences are disjoint and the entire unmarked product is $AeD$. The finite global context consists of all pairs $(A_i,D_i)$, the route data, and unmarked monoid products of subcritical paths. Defaults may be set arbitrarily if any buffer is missing. Conditional on the limiting route, this context has a full-sequence limiting distribution independent of $\Theta$. To see this, first compute first/last products using at most $H$ boundary edges at each end (default if no complete buffer seen there). For fixed $H$ the joint window law in §1 converges whenever the lengths tend to infinity; the limit is constant in the length fractions and independent between distinct critical paths, given route and subcritical paths. The probability of a truncation failure is $O((1-\epsilon)^{\lfloor H/m\rfloor})$ uniformly for large lengths, also at the terminal end by the same window/bridge bound. Send $H\to\infty$ after $n\to\infty$. This establishes the asserted joint convergence. There is no assumption that boundary counts or terminal distributions are unconditioned iid.

## 3. Local types and the marked comparison table

At a position $p$ in a long phase, take the last complete $B$ strictly before it and the first complete $B$ strictly after it. Let $L_p$ be the unmarked product between the end of the former and $p$, and $R_p$ the unmarked product between $p$ and the start of the latter, excluding $p$ and both occurrences. Its finite type consists of $(L_p,\text{letter}_p,R_p)$; use an arbitrary default if either buffer is missing.

In the two-sided stationary chain these types are stationary ergodic, the required occurrences existing almost surely. Let $f_{ia}$ denote the probability of type $a$ in phase $i$. If $N_a(t)$ counts type $a$ in the first $t$ positions of a length-$j$ path then
$$ \max_{0\le t\le j}|N_a(t)-t f_{ia}|/j\ longrightarrow\ 0 \quad\text{in probability}. \tag{2} $$
For the two-sided chain this follows almost surely by the ergodic theorem (uniformity follows by applying the ordinary bound to all sufficiently large $t$ and separately bounding the finitely many smaller $t$). Conditioning on its initial vertex costs only a constant; conditioning further on its vertex at $j$ costs another bounded factor. The stationary types and segment-internal types agree except in the initial/terminal buffer neighborhoods, of size $O(h)$ with high probability. Alternatively the two boundary waiting times are uniformly tight under the long bridge. The latter observation makes (2) immediate as $j\to\infty$ with no dependence on the global $n$. There are finitely many components/types. In particular (2) with its **deterministic** limits holds jointly with the random finite boundary context; independence of finite-word counts and boundary information is not being asserted.

Call points more than $3h$ from either end of their dominant component good. In a good phase, a single flag $g$ at good $p$ gives the exact monoid product
$$ A e L_p\, a_p^{[g]}\,R_p e D. $$
For good $p<q$ in the same phase with word distance $>4h$, flags $g,g'$ respectively give
$$ A e L_p\,a_p^{[g]}\,R_p e L_q\,a_q^{[g']}\,R_q e D. $$
Here the last buffer before $p$ is disjoint from the first of the segment; the first after $p$ is disjoint from the last before $q$; and likewise at the terminal end. Each intervening sandwich collapses by (1). There are no boundary-crossing flags. For flags in different phases use both single-mark products with the other factors unmarked. Hence, for either output order, comparison on good separated pairs is exactly a lookup on the finite global context, the two phase/type labels, and word orientation within a single phase. For any good point the number of other points for which the table need not apply is $O(h)$ times a fixed constant plus the total subcritical path and crossing length, which is tight.

## 4. From comparisons to a limiting random measure

Given the context, let $I^{(l)}_{kb,ia}$ indicate that a position of type $b$ in phase $k\ne i$ precedes a position of type $a$ in phase $i$ in order $l$. For two positions in phase $i$, use $J^{(l),-}_{ib,ia}$ and $J^{(l),+}_{ib,ia}$ for the same question when the type-$b$ position is earlier or later in the word. These values are just the marked tests above; their values on impossible combinations are immaterial. Define
$$ X^{(l)}_{ia}(s)=
\sum_{k\ne i}\Theta_k\sum_b f_{kb} I^{(l)}_{kb,ia}
+\Theta_i\sum_b f_{ib}\{s J^{(l),-}_{ib,ia}+(1-s)J^{(l),+}_{ib,ia}\},\quad 0\le s\le1. \tag{3} $$
With the actual finite length fractions in place of $\Theta$, (2) and the table imply that the normalized rank of **every** good position $p$ differs by $o_{\mathbb P}(1)$ from (3) at $s=p/j_i$ (here $p$ counts within its phase). The error is uniform: use the finitely many prefix/suffix count errors in (2), adding only the exceptional pairs per position and the $1/n$ rank convention. The fraction of nongood points tends to zero. The empirical measure of the within-phase times $p/j_i$ at positions of type $a$, divided by $j_i$, tends to $f_{ia}$ times Lebesgue measure by (2). The affine maps in (3) have uniformly bounded slopes; continuous test functions on the square are uniformly continuous. Piecewise-constant approximation in $s$ on a fixed fine mesh therefore shows that the empirical permuton differs weakly in probability (in any metric metrizing weak convergence) from
$$ \sum_{i,a}\Theta_i f_{ia}\, (X^{(1)}_{ia},X^{(2)}_{ia})_*(\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]}) \tag{4} $$
evaluated with the actual context and actual fractions. For finite $n$ the fractions can sum to $1-o_{\mathbb P}(1)$; one may renormalize or assign missing mass at the origin without affecting the result. On the finite context space (4) depends continuously on the limiting fractions. The joint convergence of phase times and contexts proves the theorem. Since both output ranks are permutations of all $n$ positions, each marginal of every limiting measure is uniform almost surely. Consequently the positive-weight terms in (4) cannot have a constant coordinate: their marginal atom could not cancel against positive measures. Using tiny uniform squares instead of atoms for the finite empirical permuton does not change the limit.

## 5. Application and precise dependencies

Markström and Räty, *Logical isomorphism and logical limit laws for small permutation classes and regular languages*, arXiv:2610.22218v2: Theorem 36 explicitly preserves free position variables and first-order logic from permutation to word on the valid encoding; apply it to the two order relations, taking their strict versions. Theorem 37 and §6 (proof of Theorem 1) restrict the length-preserving map to a canonical regular language bijecting onto the ungridded class. Theorem 62 gives primitive dominant components of exactly its finite deterministic automaton after trimming. These are the **proved forward** statements; no sketched reverse interpretation, alternative language, or unproved assertion of logical isomorphism is needed. Thus the uniform measure on words pushes to the uniform measure on the underlying class at each $n$. The underlying structure and logical coding of small classes is prior work, not supplied by this proof. Our assertion describes the full geometric probability law, not just limits of logical sentence probabilities or a formula for an individual expected pattern density.
