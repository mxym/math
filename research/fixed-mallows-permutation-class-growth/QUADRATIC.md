# The identity-forbidden case: quadratic rate and unique minimum-inversion shape

Suppose $B$ is finite and contains a nonempty identity. A layered permutation is a direct sum of decreasing blocks. Put
$$d+1=\min\{\text{number of maximal decreasing blocks of }p:p\in B\text{ is layered}\}.$$
If $d=0$, both an identity and a decreasing pattern are forbidden; the class is eventually empty by Erdős–Szekeres. Otherwise put $\mu_d$ equal to the uniform permuton supported on $d$ descending diagonal segments: if $x$ is uniform in $[j/d,(j+1)/d]$ then $y=(2j+1)/d-x$. For fixed $q\in(0,1)$,
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-2}\log Z_n(q)=\frac{\log q}{2d}.$$
Under the probability proportional to $q^{\operatorname{inv}}$ on $\operatorname{Av}_n(B)$, the probability of being outside any fixed weak neighborhood of $\mu_d$ is at most $e^{-c n^2}$ for all large $n$, with $c>0$ depending on the neighborhood, $B$ and $q$. These are leading-order results. The proof is an application of the Erdős–Stone theorem and Erdős–Simonovits stability, not a new graph stability theorem.

**Finite extremal reduction.** Associate to $\pi$ the graph on its points with an edge for a strictly increasing pair in position order. Its nonedges are exactly inversions. Fix a forbidden identity length $h$; here $h\ge2$. Fix a forbidden layered pattern with exactly $d+1$ nonempty blocks and largest block size $s$. The graph avoids the complete $(d+1)$-partite graph with each part of size $t=s(h-1)$ as a (not necessarily induced) subgraph. Indeed, within each candidate part assign ranks by longest increasing subsequence *within that part*. There are at most $h-1$ ranks, and a common-rank set is decreasing, so one can choose a decreasing subsequence of size $s$ in each part. All pairs in distinct chosen groups are comparable. A point comparable to all points in a nontrivial decreasing group cannot have position between its first and last points, since it would have to be simultaneously above the first and below the last value. If another decreasing group had points both before and after the first group it would have an ascent, by cross comparability. Thus the groups lie in some single linear order both horizontally and vertically (the singleton case is immediate). Their union is the direct sum of $d+1$ copies of the decreasing pattern of size $s$, containing the forbidden pattern. This proves the reduction.

Conversely, every direct sum of at most $d$ decreasing blocks is allowed: any pattern it contains is itself layered with at most $d$ blocks. In particular the $d$ balanced block sizes give an allowed permutation with $n^2/(2d)+O_d(n)$ inversions. For $d\ge2$, the Erdős–Stone extremal edge theorem with the forbidden complete partite graph just identified gives at most $(1-1/d)n^2/2+o(n^2)$ increasing pairs. For $d=1$, the excluded graph is a fixed $K_{t,t}$; the bipartite case of the same theorem gives $o(n^2)$ increasing pairs. Hence the minimum inversion count divided by $n^2$ tends to $1/(2d)$. The contribution of a minimizer lower bounds $Z_n(q)$, and multiplication by $n!$ upper bounds the full sum relative to this contribution. The claimed logarithmic limit follows.

**Uniqueness of nearly extremal plots.** Let $\pi_n$ be any admissible sequence with $\operatorname{inv}(\pi_n)/n^2\to1/(2d)$. For $d\ge2$, the Erdős–Simonovits stability theorem for graphs excluding a fixed graph of chromatic number $d+1$ partitions the $n$ vertices into $d$ sets of sizes $n/d+o(n)$ so that the graph differs from the corresponding complete multipartite graph on $o(n^2)$ pairs. For $d=1$ use all vertices as the sole class, and the preceding edge bound. Take any subsequence and extract a further one on which the $d$ coloured plot measures (assign mass $1/n$ to each point of the given colour at its scaled coordinates) converge weakly. Their limits $\nu_j$ have mass $1/d$, and their sum has uniform coordinate marginals. In particular coordinate ties have zero probability, also for independent samples of specified colours. The pair-count error then implies that an independent pair of the same colour inverts almost surely, whereas an independent pair of distinct colours increases almost surely when read in position order.

Write $(a_j,b_j)$ for the essential horizontal range of colour $j$. For another colour $l$, almost no position of $l$ can lie inside $(a_j,b_j)$: condition on such a point and choose two independent points of colour $j$, one on either side, an event of positive probability. They invert, and the middle point cannot be comparable to both, contrary to the pair laws and Fubini. Thus $(a_j,b_j)$ belongs exclusively to colour $j$ up to null sets and has length exactly $1/d$, by the total uniform horizontal marginal and absence of atoms. The same holds in vertical coordinates. The $d$ horizontal intervals tile $[0,1]$, as do the vertical intervals, and their orders agree because all cross-colour pairs are comparable. Within each square the conditional marginals are uniform and the pair inverts almost surely: for almost every point, the probability of a second point to its left equals that of the second point above it. Hence the sum of its coordinates equals the sum of the endpoints of the common interval. This forces precisely the indicated reflected segment. Every further subsequential limit of the uncoloured plot is therefore $\mu_d$, so the original plots converge weakly to it.

Outside a fixed open weak neighborhood there must consequently be a fixed positive margin above $1/(2d)$ in the normalized inversion count for all sufficiently large admissible $n$; otherwise compactness and near-extremal uniqueness give a contradiction (the empty exterior is harmless). Comparing the total weight there, bounded by $n!$ times the largest such weight, with the single balanced allowed example proves the speed-$n^2$ concentration statement. For instance, the increasing length-three case gives the leading exponent $\log(q)/4$ already established by Pinsky; no priority for this special case or for the extremal graph input is claimed.
