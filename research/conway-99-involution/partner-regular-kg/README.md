# A hypothetical Conway 99-graph has no partner-regular vertex

**Theorem.** Let G be a strongly regular graph with parameters (99,14,1,2). There is no vertex o such that, for each neighbor x of o, all six pairs of outside vertices matched at x are obtained by reversing the sign of their other axis. Equivalently, not all 21 pairs of the seven axes through o extend to 3-by-3 rook grids. No global automorphism, transitivity, or fixed-point hypothesis is made. This excludes one branch; it does not decide the existence of G.

The precise partner-regular split, its elementary local pair model, and the previously open “Case A” appear in [Joe975, Attempt 001, sections 3–4 (July 27, 2026)](https://github.com/Joe975/math-lab/blob/main/problems/conway-99/attempts/001-forced-pair-model-and-partner-regular-split.md). The two realized graphs with parameters (9,4,1,2) and (243,22,1,2) do have partner-regular roots; our argument is particular to the 99 parameters. The only finite step specific to our proof is a binary linear calculation with just 128 Gram signatures. Run `python3 check.py` (Python 3.10+, standard library). It also independently checks the terminal Petersen fact used below.

## 1. A lift of the Kneser graph at a hypothetical partner-regular root

The neighbors of o induce seven disjoint edges, called axes, with endpoints labeled by signs. Every other vertex has exactly two neighbors in the first layer by μ=2, belonging to distinct axes, and every such signed pair labels a unique exterior vertex: the common neighbor other than o. The 84 exterior vertices thus split into 21 four-point cells indexed by the edges e=ab of K₇, one cell for each two axes. For a signed neighbor x of o the twelve cell vertices bearing x are matched in pairs by the triangles through x.

Under partner-regularity these matchings are precisely the flips of the other sign coordinate. The resulting four points in each cell give a square. The square together with o and the four signed neighbors on the two axes is a 3-by-3 rook grid: the indicated grid edges are forced, and no extra edge is possible (two nonadjacent grid points already have two common neighbors, incompatible with adjacency). Conversely all 21 of these grids force the partner matchings. Within each such grid all pairwise common-neighbor allowances are consumed, so each outside vertex meets at most one grid point. The number of edges leaving a grid is 9(14−4)=90, the number of outside points. Thus **each** outside vertex meets exactly one point of that grid. This equality of counts should not be used at different parameters.

In particular there are no edges between distinct cells on overlapping axis pairs: an outside cell point on an overlapping axis already sees one of the first-layer grid points. Between any two disjoint cells all adjacencies form a perfect matching: all four points of the second cell miss the central and first-layer points of the first grid, hence see one square point, and conversely. So between cells we have a four-point lift of K=KG(7,2), the graph on pairs with disjointness as adjacency. Identify each square with F₂² using sign coordinates. All permutations of its four points are affine, since AGL(2,2) has order 24 and acts faithfully. Nothing here presupposes a common global coordinate choice or symmetry of the intercell maps.

## 2. A 12-dimensional symplectic image over F₂

Over the reals, take the seven normalized sign-difference vectors on the first-layer axes and the three normalized nonconstant characters in each four-cell (the two single-sign characters and their product). These 70 vectors are orthonormal and span an adjacency-invariant space of zero-sum vectors: the first-layer matching acts by −1 on sign differences; first/exterior adjacency joins a sign difference to a single-coordinate character and has no constant term; square adjacency acts by 0,0,−2 on the three characters; intercell matchings act by signed character permutations. In this basis adjacency restricts to

    S = [ −I₇     √2 C  ]
        [ √2 Cᵀ   H−2B  ].

Here C has six ones in row a selecting the a-character of each incident cell, with pairwise disjoint row supports. B is the diagonal projector onto the 21 product characters. H has zero diagonal fibre blocks and, between disjoint cells, signed permutation matrices on the three characters; write H₀=H−2B. On this sector the SRG identity is S²+S=12I, giving CH₀=0 and H₀²+H₀=12I−2CᵀC. The image of Cᵀ has dimension 7 and eigenvalue 0 for H₀; on its perpendicular complement of dimension 56 the eigenvalues of H₀ are 3 and −4. Since tr H₀=−42, the multiplicities are 26 and 30 respectively.

Modulo 2, the symmetric matrix H is idempotent of rank 26 (its characteristic polynomial has 26 roots 1 with multiplicity, and it is diagonalizable). On the three coordinates in each fibre the natural dot-product space decomposes as constant line plus the even-weight plane. These spaces are orthogonal; on the plane the form is alternating and nondegenerate. Both summands over all fibres are H-invariant since fibre maps are permutations. H is K adjacency on the 21 constant fibre lines, of binary rank 14: if D is ordinary unsigned vertex/edge incidence for K₇ then K=J+I−DᵀD, DDᵀ=5I+J, so the real eigenvalues are 10 (once), −4 (six times), 1 (fourteen times). Reduce its characteristic polynomial modulo 2 and use idempotence. The remaining binary projector P on the 42-dimensional even-weight space consequently has rank 12. Being self-adjoint and idempotent, it has nondegenerate image W, an alternating space of dimension 12.

For each cell e, let U_e denote the image of its input even-weight plane in W. For inputs x,y from the two planes of e,f the pairing of their images is xᵀPy. Thus U_e is an injective image (test against any disjoint cell) and totally isotropic; on intersecting pairs the mutual pairing vanishes, while on disjoint pairs it is nondegenerate. More particularly, label the three nonzero even-weight input vectors by their *missing* coordinate. Two labels have dot product zero if equal and one otherwise. Therefore for disjoint e,f, the unique label in U_f perpendicular to a given nonzero label in U_e is exactly its image under the corresponding unsigned character permutation. All unsigned matching permutations can be recovered by this rule, up to independent choices of labels in the cells.

## 3. All possible pairings in W: a small exact computation

Use the six cells incident with an axis ★. Their planes are mutually orthogonal; any three are in direct sum. In fact for a relation among U_{★a},U_{★b},U_{★c}, pair with U_{bc} to eliminate the first coefficient, and repeat. They therefore span a six-dimensional Lagrangian L in W. Use a symplectic splitting W=L⊕L* with pairing (x,y)·(x′,y′)=x·y′+x′·y. Such a splitting exists by extending a basis of a Lagrangian to a symplectic basis, also in characteristic two.

Normalize the six planes inside L as follows. Choose the first three as the coordinate 2-spaces in (F₂²)³. The fourth plane is a graph (u,Au,Bu) with A,B invertible since it is direct with any two coordinate planes; make it (u,u,u) by independent coordinate changes. The fifth is (u,Cu,Du): C,D, C+I,D+I,C+D are invertible by the same three-plane tests. The sole fixed-point-free matrices in GL₂(F₂) are the two 3-cycles ω and ω² on the three nonzero vectors. Hence (C,D)=(ω,ω²) up to simultaneous conjugation. The sixth must be (u,ω²u,ωu): the same requirements apply, and sharing C (or D) with the fifth would violate directness with a coordinate plane. No further type is lost. In the checker these planes are written P_a, 0≤a<6.

For e=ab away from ★ its projection to L* lies in Y_e=ann(P_a+P_b) of dimension two and has no kernel: a vector in the kernel lies in L and pairs to zero with every P_c, whereas the pairing with each c outside {a,b} is nondegenerate. Thus the projection is an isomorphism. Fix a basis y_{e0},y_{e1} of Y_e, and write the two lifted basis vectors as (x_{ei},y_{ei}), with unknown six-bit x_{ei}. There are 15·2·6=180 unknown bits. Their pairings

    m(e,i; f,j) = x_{ei}·y_{fj} + x_{fj}·y_{ei}

are homogeneous *linear forms*. Enforce m(e,0;e,1)=0 for each cell and the four vanishing pairings on each intersecting pair of distinct cells. These conditions have binary rank 152, leaving 28 independent bits. On this nullspace the 4·45 forms for disjoint off-star pairs span dimension seven. This is why only 128 assignments, not 2²⁸, need testing: only their values on a basis of those seven forms affect any remaining disjoint Gram block. On star/off-star pairs the pairing is already fixed by P_a and Y_e.

Of the 128 assignments only one has determinant one on every disjoint off-star 2-by-2 block. For that assignment compute the matching permutations by the unique missing-coordinate orthogonality rule above. For **all 105** triangles of the Kneser graph, the product of the three character permutations around the triangle is the identity. These finite statements, including all normalization-independent disjoint-pair determinant tests, are verified by `check.py`. The code generates rather than imports all planes, constraints, signature functions and holonomies; it does not call a numerical or combinatorial solver.

## 4. Affine holonomy forces an impossible Petersen decomposition

A base triangle (e,f,g) is a matching of three disjoint edges of K₇. Its three affine intercell maps compose to a permutation of the four points of e. The preceding calculation says its action on the three linear characters, ignoring signs, is trivial. Thus its linear part is the identity and its affine holonomy is a translation. A translation has either zero fixed points or all four. A fixed point is exactly one triangle in the four-lift of this base triangle. For a disjoint cell pair e,f, the four matching edges require one triangle per edge, by λ=1. None can use the root or a first-layer point (axis pairs are disjoint), or a second point of either cell (the cell-to-cell links are matchings), or an intersecting cell (those lack off-cell edges). Therefore among the three possible third cells, exactly one base triangle must have identity translation, covering all four edges. The selected base triangles would partition the edges of KG(7,2).

Here is a short purely combinatorial contradiction. Each K₇ edge e is a vertex of degree ten in KG(7,2), so five triangles of a decomposition contain it. For an axis h not in e, exactly four of e's ten neighbors contain h, hence four of the five selected triples through e cover h and exactly one omits h. Since every base triangle uses six axes, for each h the triples omitting h are a 1-factorization of K₆ on the other axes: each edge not incident with h occurs exactly once. Take different h,k. Restrict each of their factorizations to the five common axes, deleting the edge incident to k or h in each perfect matching respectively. Each restriction gives a perfect matching of KG(5,2), pairing all ten edges of K₅ into disjoint-edge pairs. The two Petersen perfect matchings must be edge-disjoint, since any shared pair would occur in two decomposition triangles, one omitting h and one omitting k. But no pair of Petersen perfect matchings is edge-disjoint. This is equivalent to its familiar non-3-edge-colorability, or can be checked directly: its six perfect matchings pairwise intersect (also independently verified in the checker). This contradiction proves the theorem.

In prism language, if all seven triangles through o have twelve prism partners, then for each two axes and each sign of one, the prism based at the other axis flips the other sign, so o is partner-regular. Conversely partner matchings make all twelve partners at each rooted triangle. Hence every vertex lies on at least one triangle with fewer than twelve prism partners. This is a local obstruction, not a solution of the 99-graph existence problem.
