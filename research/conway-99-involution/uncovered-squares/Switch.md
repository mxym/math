# The 53-grid endpoint has no exceptional square

Let G be an SRG(99,14,1,2), let n3 count disjoint triangle pairs with precisely two linking edges, g count induced 3-by-3 rook grids, and U count induced squares contained in no triangular prism. Assume n3=1602 and g=53. Then U=0: every square belongs to a prism, and every square that belongs to two prisms belongs to a rook grid. This does not rule out a 53-grid graph or a 54-grid graph.

We use the [grid counting identity](../grid-density-gap/GridFreeDichotomy.md), [nine-point 17-edge gap](../grid-density-gap/README.md), and [finite two-star inequality](README.md), which gives U≤15. Every edge is in a unique triangle, called a line. Center the chart at a vertex x. Its 14 neighbors form seven lines through x (besides x, each has a signed pair). For two lines L,M and signs on the two axes, their signed parents have unique common neighbor other than x, an exterior point in the four-cell C_LM. All 84 are distinct; its signs are the two parents of that exterior point in N(x). It is the opposite vertex of a square through x. A flip joins two points of C_LM differing in just one sign. By the elementary rooted prism correspondence, the number of prism directions of a square through x is its opposite point's flip degree. The four flips form a rook grid on these nine points; conversely if any such square lies in a grid then this is its grid (its two lines through x determine the chart). The nine-point gap excludes exactly three flips. Therefore all nongrid cells have ≤2 flips. The global number of flips is 6P=2(4158−1602)=5112. The 9g=477 gridded cells have four each; the remaining 2079−477=1602 have at most two each. Equality 5112=4·477+2·1602 hence forces exactly two flips in every nongrid cell.

Call an uncovered square minus and a doubly prism-covered square outside any grid plus. In a cell of two opposite flip edges all four associated squares have one prism each. In a cell of two adjacent flip edges the flip degrees are 2,1,1,0, with the degree-two and degree-zero points at opposite sign pairs. The cell has no grid, so these give an intrinsic plus and minus square. At a corner x define its switch by reversing both parent signs on its two lines through x. On occurrences of exceptional squares at x this is a bijection swapping plus and minus, preserving the two lines. In particular there are U plus squares. For a given pair of lines at x there is at most one plus and one minus occurrence.

Suppose U>0. A line is active if some side of an exceptional square lies on it. For an active line L={a,b,c}, write p_ab,p_ac,p_bc and m_ab,m_ac,m_bc for the numbers of plus and minus squares on its respective edges. Switching at a gives p_ab=m_ac and p_ac=m_ab. At b it gives p_ab=m_bc and p_bc=m_ab. At c it gives p_ac=m_bc and p_bc=m_ac. Thus all six equal one integer w_L≥1. Summing plus-side incidences gives 3∑_L w_L=4U≤60. There are at most 20 active lines.

At each point x on an active line put a simple graph on the active lines through x, joining two if their common chart has a plus occurrence. For L through x its degree in this graph is 2w_L: the 2w_L plus squares through x with a side on L are counted once, and no line pair repeats. So every point of X, the union of active lines, lies on at least three active lines. Incidence counting gives |X|≤#active lines≤20. The graph induced by X has minimum degree ≥6. For x of internal degree d, count length-two walks with different endpoints starting at x. There are at least 5d because every intermediate point has at least six neighbors within X. At most one such walk ends at an adjacent vertex and at most two at any nonneighbor. Hence 5d≤d+2(|X|−1−d), or 6d≤38. Consequently X is induced 6-regular; there are exactly three active lines at each of its points and the local simple graph on them is a triangle.

Fix x in X. For any pair of these three lines the four cell points all belong to X. Indeed the diagonally opposite plus and minus points are corners of exceptional squares. At the plus point, each of its two available standard flips forms a triangle with the shared signed parent: it is the unique third point of the line on that edge of the plus square. That line is active, so its whole triple lies in X, including the flip mate. The two mates give exactly the two remaining sign points. For all three pairs we have obtained twelve distinct x-nonneighbors in X, each with both of its global common neighbors with x (the two signed parents) also in X. Combined with its six neighbors and itself this yields 19≤|X|≤20. Every adjacent pair in X has its common neighbor in X, since every internal edge is on one of the three active lines of its endpoint.

For |X|=19 no vertex outside X can meet two vertices of X: each pair has exhausted its common-neighbor allowance. For |X|=20 each vertex of X has at most one other vertex of X forming a pair with any outside common neighbor, namely its sole remaining nonneighbor; thus any outside vertex meets at most two vertices of X. The boundary has size 8|X| by 6-regularity inside. But 8·19>80 in the first case, and 8·20>2·79 in the second. Contradiction.

Hence U=0 and no plus squares occur. In particular all 99 points are covered and all nongrid rooted cells have two disjoint parallel flips. The 54-grid case is not covered by the switching bijection.
