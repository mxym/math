# The four-fixed-grid shells cannot extend

At the possible endpoint n3=1602, the [earlier fully nice line theorem](../nice-triangle/Theorem.md) proves that there is a pure-rich triangle T with all three vertices nice (covered and free of centered H), b_T=3 or 4, and either sign twist on the complementary pair cycle. Both b_T=4 possibilities are impossible. Moreover the eight- and eleven-arc nice-root diagrams are impossible, even locally, leaving only the diagrams with ten, twelve, or fourteen dirty arcs among the five containers. Hence some fully nice T must have b_T=3 (two sign twists). This excludes neither of the three-fixed-grid shells nor the whole graph.

## Two integral cuts

Use the integer row multiplicities n, the homogeneous moment constraints An=0 and four rows per cell, and the 105 inequalities Qn≥16 for a nice root exactly as in the [covered-root certificate](../covered-root/Theorem.md). Label pairs of disjoint cells are ordered by second cell then first in lexicographic cell order. At the eight-arc diagram, the number M of edges from one pair of incident cells (the sum of the first dirty coordinate over the third dirty cell in canonical arc order) is an integer. Rounding exact dual bounds gives M≥2 and M≤1. At the eleven-arc diagram the 18 rows with zero-based indices 10,11,14,15,18,19,29,30,32,33,36,37,39,40,43,44,46,47 of Qn each have upper bound strictly below 17; as integers each is 16. These cuts contradict the original constraints together.

Here is the general replay rule. To certify an integer inequality c·n≥b given Rn≥r, choose scale s>0, signed integer multipliers u of A, and nonnegative integer multipliers a of R. Compute
  L = a·r + 4 sum_h min_(t in h) (sc−Aᵀu−Rᵀa)_(h,t).
Then sc·n≥L, so L>s(b−1) implies c·n≥b. To derive a contradiction append the proved cuts to R and use c=0,L>0. The standalone checker reconstructs all ternary rows and these A,Q coefficients directly; the JSON contains only integer multipliers and claimed lower numerators. No LP infeasibility report is used.

## Four-grid symbols

Since the eight- and eleven-arc possibilities are excluded, at a nice point a pure-rich axis with b=4 can occur only in the ten-arc diagram, five sources pointing to two sinks. This axis is one of the five sources; the four other source axes make clean cells with it and with one another. Suppose T has three nice points and b_T=4. Label its points arms 0,1,2, its four incident grids symbols 0,1,2,3. Each grid consists of T and two points in each arm; label those two by signs ± (consistently along the two parallel fixed triples). Each of the 60 vertices far from T sees exactly one vertex of each of its grids, since a nine-point rook grid is 1-dominating (its indicator satisfies (A−3I)χ=1). Thus its four symbols specify a word in {0,…,5}^4; a letter 2i+s means arm i, sign s. There are exactly two contacts with all twelve points in each arm, hence at most two letters in any one arm. Distinct words/points cannot agree on three symbols, by the common-neighbor bound. In particular 60 words occur, with no repetitions.

For two fixed symbols and specified letters x,y, they occur together once if in the same arm, and twice if in different arms. In the first case the two indicated wing points from distinct grids share just the central point of that arm in the 39-point shell; in the second they share no shell neighbor. Both pairs are nonadjacent and require two common neighbors in total. This also yields 72 occurrences of a pair of symbols together in an arm (six pairs, three arms, four sign combinations). Every word has distribution (2,1,1) or (2,2,0) among the arms. The former contributes one same-arm pair and the latter two, so precisely 12 words have pattern (2,2,0).

Fix a pair h of symbols in one arm i. Their four sign choices give a four-cell of far vertices. At the nice root of arm i this is clean since both axes are sources. For any other symbol c, the cell on T and c is also clean and its four points are the signed letters for c in the other two arms. Between two disjoint clean four-cells the graph is a perfect matching by grid domination. Its permutation P cannot map opposite points to opposite points. Otherwise for v in one cell let w=P(v*) with v* opposite v. The pair v,w is nonadjacent and neither has a signed-parent triangle partner in common with the other (all such partners are the two vertices adjacent on its own square). As in the H motif proof, the root,v,w would be the centers of H. Encoding a square by two bits, P therefore ranges over the permutations of 0,…,3 with P(0) XOR P(3) different from 3. All permutations of a four-set preserve the system of opposite pairs whenever they preserve one member of that system, so this tests exactly the 16 nonisometries.

## The two overlap patterns

Partition the four symbols into complementary pairs h and f. At h in arm i, the two projected letters of the f symbols have balanced arm bits on the four input words: each takes each other arm twice. Hence for each of the two other arms j the number with both f letters in j is the same t_(h,i) in {0,1,2}: two balanced binary coordinates have the same number of 00 and 11. By counting these very words in the reverse direction, t_(h,i)=t_(f,j) whenever i≠j. This bipartite relation on three plus three cells is connected, so a single t applies to the partition h|f. Exactly 6t double-pair words come from the partition (sum over the three h cells and two other arms). Over the three partitions the sum of t equals 12/6=2. Permuting the four symbol names lets us treat just (0,0,2) and (0,1,1).

A table at an arm/pair cell specifies its four words by choosing the two permutations P,Q to the two complementary letters, with the required t. For t=0 there are 48 ordered choices; for t=1 there are 160. If t=2 there are two points at h in arm i sent to the same other arm j with both complementary letters there. They have opposite signs on *both* f symbols (each projection is bijective), so they are opposite on the clean f square. Therefore they cannot be adjacent on the clean h square, and must also be opposite there. Thus the two words at each i,j comprise a bijection of one diagonal pair to another.

* In (0,0,2), put t=2 on 01|23. Choose which diagonal parity of each of its six arm/pair cells goes to which other arm (six bits), then the bijection of each size-two pair for i≠j (six bits). This enumerates all 4,096 possible double-word sets. Relabel the three arms, flip the four symbols' signs, swap symbols in either pair and exchange the two pairs; the explicit action groups these into exactly 21 representatives. Complete each by choosing one t=0 table at each of the other 12 arm/pair cells.
* In (0,1,1), put t=0 on 01|23. The subgroup swapping either pair's symbols, flipping independent global signs and swapping arms 1 and 2 preserves this pattern and the 01 cell at arm 0. It places its 48 possible tables into three orbits of 16 (representative indices 0,2,3). Choose a table for each of the 18 cells. For each t=1 partition and i≠j the two respective tables must specify exactly the same four-sign double word. Count that word from its designated primary pair only; each singleton word belongs to a unique cell.

In both cases the search maintains the residual prescribed two-symbol occurrence counts, and unit capacity on each assignment of any three symbols. A branch cannot choose a table exceeding any remaining capacity. For each required pair count it sums the minima / maxima over the remaining domains; infeasibility or removal of a candidate whose contribution lies outside any achievable interval is sound. For t=1 it additionally removes tables lacking a compatible double-word tag in the opposing domain. Iterate these deletions, branch over every table in a smallest remaining domain, and accept only when all required pair counts hold. These are necessary conditions, not asserted sufficient for a graph. All branches fail (the exact search has no timeout). No symmetry of the putative graph is assumed; the reductions are relabelings of the auxiliary words.
