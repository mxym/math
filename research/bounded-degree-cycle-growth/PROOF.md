# Cycle growth with a fixed maximum degree in 3-connected graphs

All graphs are finite, simple and undirected. Distinct cycles mean distinct edge sets, not distinct lengths or vertex sets. For each fixed integer D≥3, put b=2(D−1)²+1 and γ=(log_b 2)/4. There are constants c_D>0 and n_D such that every n-vertex 3-vertex-connected graph with maximum degree at most D, n≥n_D, contains at least
$$
2^{c_D n^{\gamma}}
$$
cycles. In particular the polynomial upper bound in Knor's final conjecture (1994, §4, last paragraph) fails for all its triples of fixed integers k>2, k<δ<D. This is not a sharp shortness exponent or a proof of an exponential-in-n bound.

Aldred and Thomassen (1997, proof of Theorem 3.1) gave the underlying cycle/chord ordering and ladder strategy for cubic 3-connected graphs. Their published theorem already gives a stronger exponent in that cubic case. We adapt the ear supply to bounded maximum degree and give a full ladder-switching argument using 3-connectivity in the ambient graph, not in a chorded minor.

**1. A linear supply of disjoint ears on a long cycle.** Given any cycle C of length L in a 3-connected graph of maximum degree D, delete its edges but retain its vertices as terminals. Each component containing a terminal then contains at least two. In fact every terminal has degree at least three in G, so has an incident non-cycle edge; a chord leads to another terminal, and any component of G−V(C) has at least three distinct neighbors on C by 3-connectivity (otherwise deletion of those neighbors separates it from the remainder of C).

In each component with t≥2 terminals, root a spanning tree. Repeatedly choose a deepest vertex whose remaining rooted subtree contains at least two terminals. Each child subtree then contains at most one, so this whole subtree contains at most D+1 terminals. Within it take a path between a nearest pair of terminals, and remove the entire subtree. The remainder is a rooted tree or empty; at most one terminal survives when the procedure stops. If p paths were taken then p≥1 and t≤(D+1)p+1≤(D+2)p. The resulting collection has P≥L/(D+2) vertex-disjoint paths between pairs of distinct terminals, with no interior on C and no edge of C. Call these ears; they may include single chords.

**2. Chord order.** Fix an integer s≥3 such that L≥100(D+2)s⁴. Set T=(2s+12)(s+2). Since 2P≥200s⁴≥s(s+2)T, partition the cyclic sequence of 2P distinct terminal endpoints into s consecutive blocks each with at least (s+2)T endpoints. If each block contains both ends of some ear, select one such ear in each block. Independently replace or retain the portion of C within the block between its two ends. These s disjoint choices yield 2^s cycles.

Otherwise, choose a block with all its partners outside. List the block endpoints in cyclic order and record their distinct partners' positions on the complementary arc (in the same cyclic direction). The Erdős–Szekeres monotone subsequence theorem supplies an increasing subsequence of length s+2 or a decreasing one of length T: absence of both bounds the list length by (s+1)(T−1). In both cases label the selected endpoints a_i on the block arc and their partners b_i. The arc portions joining consecutive a's and consecutive b's are two rails, and the ears a_i b_i are disjoint rungs. In the increasing case both arrays occur in the same cyclic direction, and among t=s+2 selected rungs a single arc of the original cycle joins b_t to a_1 off the rails. A path goes from a_1 towards increasing index, switching rails at an arbitrarily selected subset of rungs; an odd number of switches ends at b_t. Close by the off-rail arc. This is simple, and its rung set identifies the subset. There are 2^{t−1} cycles.

**3. Nested case: recover alternatives in the ambient graph.** The decreasing case gives t=T rungs P_i, 1≤i≤t, with the two rails ordered by increasing index from left to right when traversed in opposite cyclic directions. The unused end arcs of C (caps) join a_1 to b_1 and a_t to b_t. Let H be precisely C plus these t rungs as paths. Give each point on P_i coordinate i, each point on a cap its adjacent column 1 or t, and each internal rail vertex a coordinate strictly increasing between the consecutive integers of its rail segment. The edges of the rails and caps here are **the original subdivided paths**.

An H-bridge is either an edge outside H with both ends in H or a connected component of G−V(H) together with its incident edges to H. Any two distinct attachments of a bridge can be joined by an external path in that bridge with interior off H. Call the difference between its largest and smallest attachment coordinates its span (zero if only one). If any bridge has span ≥s+3, select extremal attachments x,y and an external x–y path. There are at least s+2 integer columns strictly between their coordinates. Within H, start at x and follow a rail to the right, optionally switching at any subset of these interior rungs, and continue to y. If x is interior to a rung or a cap, first go along it to a rail endpoint; otherwise use x's own rail; do the analogous thing at y, fixing one permissible rail at each end. The chosen parity of switches enforces these start/end rails, so at least 2^{s+1} distinct such paths are available. Since coordinates strictly progress along the rails and the switches are at distinct intermediate columns, these paths are simple. Closing by the external path yields distinct simple cycles.

Now every bridge has span <s+3. Consider s centers i=(2s+12)j, j=1,…,s, with disjoint slabs from column i−(s+4) to i+(s+4), strictly inside the two caps. The default linkage in a slab is the pair of subpaths of the top and bottom rails connecting its left ends to its right ends. We show that there is a second such two-path linkage, using an external edge, with the same two sets of ends (the pairing may swap).

Delete a_i and b_i. In H the vertex sets of coordinate <i and >i are its left and right components respectively; there is in addition the open rung P_i minus its endpoints if that interior is nonempty. Connectivity of G−{a_i,b_i} gives a path from the left to the right. Follow it until, on visits to the remaining H, it first has a visit outside the left component. From the last left visit x to that first outside visit y the subpath Q is external, contained in one H-bridge; there is no H edge across the two remaining components. Either y is on the open middle rung or it is on the right. In particular x has coordinate <i and y has coordinate ≥i; the span bound puts both attachments strictly between i−s−3 and i+s+3. Ignore any other available attachments of the bridge.

To use Q, approach x along a permitted rail from the slab's left boundary. At a rail vertex its rail is forced; if x is interior to an earlier rung, approach its upper endpoint and traverse the part of the rung to x (either rail could be chosen). If y is interior to P_i, follow Q and the part of P_i from y to the endpoint on the approach rail. Continue straight on that rail to the right boundary. The untouched other rail supplies the disjoint second path.

If y is on the right, choose a departure rail at y (and optional partial rung) by the same rule in reverse. Follow the approach, Q and departure. When the two chosen rails match, take the other rail straight through the slab. When they differ, the second path uses the remaining left rail up to P_i, the **entire unoccupied rung P_i**, and the remaining right rail after P_i. The first path's approach ends at coordinate <i, its departure begins at coordinate >i, and its external portion Q has no interior in H. These are disjoint simple paths; partial rungs at an attachment never reach the opposite rail. This proves the alternative in every case, including nontrivial subdivisions of the ladder rungs and rail edges.

A single bridge cannot supply Q for two centers: the interval between its extreme attachments contains each center it serves, whereas centers differ by 2s+12>s+3. Hence the alternate paths in distinct slabs have disjoint outside interiors; their parts in H lie in disjoint slabs. Independently using the default or the alternative in each slab, and joining by the two intervening plain rails, gives two disjoint paths from the left end-pair of the whole ladder to the right end-pair, in some order. Adding both end caps always produces **one** simple cycle. Its use of a chosen external edge of the specified Q detects the decision in its slab, so there are at least 2^s cycles. This completes both cases of the chord order.

**4. The exponent and the quantifiers.** Chen, Xu and Yu (2004) prove that the circumference of a 3-connected n-vertex graph of maximum degree at most D is at least A_D n^{log_b 2} for some constant A_D>0. Choose such a cycle, and take s=floor((L/(100(D+2)))^{1/4}); for sufficiently large n this is at least three and at least c_D n^γ for some c_D>0. Parts 1–3 give the stated bound.

Knor writes Γ_{k,δ,D} for graphs of connectivity at least k, minimum degree at least δ, and maximum degree at most D, and c_n for their minimum cycle count on n vertices. For each k>2 and k<δ<D all graphs in that class are 3-connected. It is nonempty for every sufficiently large n: choose an even h in {δ,δ+1}, and on the n-cycle join all vertex pairs of cyclic distance at most h/2. For n>h+1 the graph is h-regular and h-connected: to split the remaining cyclically ordered vertices into different components after deletions requires at least two intervening gaps each with at least h/2 deleted vertices. Thus c_n≥2^{c_D n^γ} eventually, contradicting the O(n^k) direction for every triple of parameters in the conjecture, without contradicting its Ω(n^k) direction. The bound is consistent with ordinary cubic families of subexponential cycle count and does not address exponential hypotheses under greater cyclic edge connectivity.
