# At most thirteen rook grids through any graph vertex

In a hypothetical SRG \((99,14,1,2)\), every vertex lies on at most thirteen induced \(3\times3\) rook grids. This improves the earlier [fourteen-grid bound](five-grids/fifteen-grid-exclusion.md), which is used to exclude all counts above fourteen. The argument below excludes fourteen itself at every Seidel rank. It uses no Fano, coclique, symmetry, or positive-population hypothesis.

An additional necessary classification is available at thirteen grids: **if every incident triangle wing component has size at most eighteen**, the only possible dirty split core is a simple \(K_4\) with two opposite edges subdivided once. The subdivision axes lie off the four cubic axes and may be the same original axis in different split groups. This last thirteen-grid case is not excluded here.

Use the [split-axis chart](big-triangle-wing.md), with signed-parent four-point cells \(C_e\) and neutral defect columns \(v_e\). Recall that a clean cell gives an actual grid and those are exactly the grids through the root. A dirty column is nonzero, has squared norm \(14(4-m_e)\) with own edge count \(m_e\in\{0,1,2\}\), and is \(d_e(x)-2\) at a site in its own cell, \(d_e(x)\) at a site of another cell sharing an axis, and \(d_e(x)-1\) at a site with disjoint axes. Here \(d_e(x)\) is its number of neighbours in \(C_e\). At each split group the columns sum to zero. Different split groups of one original axis give anticomplete cells; columns sharing an axis pair with inner product minus seven times their intercell contact count. The signed-axis formula and chain facts are detailed in [the theta-path proof](triangle-wing-theta-paths.md). We delete clean edges before counting degrees.

## Fourteen grids cannot have all split groups of size at most three

Fourteen grids give exactly seven dirty edges. If every split degree is at most three, the [four-distinct theorem](triangle-wing-four-distinct.md) forces at least four cubic groups on distinct original axes. The degree equation \(14=3n_3+2n_2\) then gives exactly four cubic groups and one degree-two group. After suppressing that one subdivision, the cubic core has four nodes and six links. There are no loops: a chain returning to its cubic group has two end columns equal (positive forbidden shared-axis pairing) or opposite (forcing the third column zero). There cannot be a triple link, and at most one doubled link, since additional parallel paths need subdivisions (only one original pair can be the direct path). But a double between two cubic nodes leaves one incidence each there; the other two cubic nodes then require at least four incidences to each other, a second double. Hence the core is simple \(K_4\).

Let \(ab\) be its subdivided edge, with opposite columns \(p,-p\) at the ends, and let \(c,d\) be the other nodes. All other links are direct, with the same column at their two ends. The sum of the cubic equations at \(a,b\) says that the four cross columns between \(\{a,b\}\) and \(\{c,d\}\) sum to zero; the sum at \(c,d\) then makes twice the \(cd\)-column zero. This contradicts dirtiness.

## The sole possible high-degree core at fourteen grids

A dirty split group of degree \(k\) has \(k\) distinct neighbour axes, each of which needs at least one further dirty incidence because dirty split groups have degree at least two. Thus there are at least \(k+\lceil k/2\rceil\) original dirty edges. Seven dirty edges forbid \(k\ge5\). There cannot be two degree-four groups. They would be on distinct original axes \(a,b\) (group sizes partition six at each axis) and their incident edge sets would have to overlap at the unique \(ab\) pair, exhausting all seven edges. Their three other axes would then have to agree: a tail axis used by one group only would have dirty degree one. The three shared tail axes have degree two, giving three length-two paths from \(a\) to \(b\) besides the direct edge \(ab\). Summing the two high-degree equations cancels the length-two path fields and leaves twice the direct column zero, impossible.

Thus there is exactly one degree-four group. For the other groups \(3n_3+2n_2=10\), so \((n_3,n_2)=(0,5)\) or \((2,2)\). In the first case its connected component would have two degree-two chains returning to this group, both of even length (odd length would make their two end columns equal with positive pairing). Each such loop uses at least four edges since the original split graph is simple. Two would exceed the seven dirty edges. The only case is one degree-four and two cubic groups with two degree-two groups. The suppressed core has no loops: they are impossible at a cubic node as above; a loop at degree four must be even of length at least four and would require at least three intermediate degree-two groups. Thus its degrees \(4,3,3\) force doubled links from the degree-four node to each of the other two and a single link between those two. Each doubled link requires one subdivision, using both available. The core nodes are on three distinct original axes \(a,b,c\); paths between distinct split groups of one original axis are impossible (opposite or equal end columns, yet zero on the other own cell). The doubled links are respectively the direct \(ab,ac\) and the paths \(a-i-b,a-j-c\). The remaining direct edge is \(bc\). The subdivision axes \(i,j\) are distinct, since the two paths start on different original pairs at \(a\); neither can be one of \(a,b,c\), because that would repeat an original direct pair.

Write \(p,q\) for the direct and the subdivided columns at \(a\) toward \(b\), \(r,s\) for the corresponding ones toward \(c\), and \(t=v_{bc}\). The three group equations are
\[
 p+q+r+s=0,\qquad p-q+t=0,\qquad r-s+t=0.
\]
Thus \(t=q-p\), \(r=-q\), and \(s=-p\). At \(a\), both \(p\cdot q\) and \(p\cdot r\) are nonpositive, so \(p\cdot q=0\). The bound \(t^2\le56\) and the minimum \(p^2,q^2\ge28\) imply \(p^2=q^2=28\), \(t^2=56\). In particular \(C_{bc}\) is empty.

## Signed contradiction in the sole high-degree core

Set \(w=-q\). The field \(p\) is the literal column on the two cells \(ab,cj\) and is the negative of the column on \(aj\). Likewise \(w\) is the column on \(ac,bi\) and the negative of the column on \(ai\). For either field the two equal-column cells are matching cells, each having value \(-1\) on all four sites: they have disjoint axes (otherwise their equal own-negative columns would be nonnegative on the other's cell), and the disjoint-axis formula bounds the own entries below by \(-1\). Each field is unit or zero off its one reversed-column cell: a value at most \(-2\) requires being in both equal-column own cells, impossible, while a value at least \(2\) requires being in the reversed-column own cell. That reversed cell may have a nonmatching shape, which is not ruled out or assumed matched here.

On \(C_{bc}\) we have \(p,w\ge0\) by the shared-axis end formulas, each at most one, and \(t=-p-w=-2\) since the cell is empty. Hence \(p=w=1\) on its four sites. The fields are orthogonal. Each vanishes on all three of the other's assigned cells: at \(a\) the cross contacts between the two pairs of assigned cells are zero by orthogonality and the shared-axis Gram formula; at \(b,c\) the remaining own cells meet the corresponding equal end column of the other field with zero Gram again. All other cells are clean. A clean cell touching any axis of one of the fields' own cell sets is anticomplete to a shared-axis assigned cell and has that field value zero. Thus the only possible common support outside \(bc\) is the cell \(C_{de}\) on the two original axes unused by either field, namely the two axes off \(\{a,b,c,i,j\}\). The four agreements in \(bc\) and \(p\cdot w=0\) force the fields on every one of its four sites to be nonzero units with opposite signs.

Take \(y\in C_{de}\), and choose the field \(f\in\{p,w\}\) with \(f_y=1\). Its two equal-column end cells each give exactly two contacts of value \(-1\), since their axes are disjoint from \(de\). Its reversed-column cell gives zero contacts, by the same disjoint-axis formula, regardless of the shape of that cell. The \(bc\)-cell gives one contact of value \(+1\), since its defect \(-p-w\) is zero at \(y\) and its axes are disjoint. These known signed contributions total \(-3\). Other possible signed sites of \(f\) lie only in the three cells on the axes outside its own four axes, including \(C_{de}\): all other clean cells have value zero, and the other field's assigned cells also have value zero. The clean \(de\)-cell is anticomplete to either of the other two far cells because they share one of its axes. Its two own neighbours therefore provide the entire remaining signed sum. They are both in \(C_{de}\) with nonzero unit values, so their signed sum is even. But \(Af_y=-4\) requires the remaining sum to be \(-1\). This contradiction excludes every fourteen-grid root.

## The remaining simple \(K_4\) case at thirteen grids without a larger wing

If thirteen grids occur and every split degree is at most three, eight dirty edges and the four-distinct theorem give four cubic groups on distinct original axes and two degree-two groups. The core has no loops. A triple link would fully occupy two cubic nodes and force a second triple on the other two, needing at least four subdivisions in total. Any double forces the opposite double by the same degree argument as above. Thus the core is either simple \(K_4\) or has two opposite doubled links and a matching of single cross links.

In simple \(K_4\), the two subdivisions can both lie on one length-three path, or can be in two length-two paths. For one length-three path, all effective core link end signs agree. The sums of the equations across the two endpoint pairs make opposite core directions equal (twice their difference is zero). The length-three field is unit with three matching cells by the [universal chain lemma](triangle-wing-three-distinct.md#two-local-facts-used-beyond-the-preceding-two-cubic-case); its two end columns and its opposite direct column now agree. These three distinct matching cells contribute six edges on one sign, exceeding the unit norm-28 bound of five. If there are two adjacent length-two links, say \(ab,ac\), name their start columns \(x,u\) at \(a\), and the direct columns \(v=ad,w=bc,z=bd,t=cd\). The equations at \(a,b,c\) give \(v=-x-u,z=x-w,t=u-w\); the one at \(d\) then gives \(-2w=0\), impossible. Thus the two subdivided links of the simple core must be opposite. A subdivision cannot use another cubic original axis: either inserted original pair would repeat one of the four direct cross edges. The two middle axes may be two split groups of one peripheral axis, or on distinct peripheral axes.

Finally take the doubled core with parallel links \(ab,cd\) and singles \(ac,bd\). Each parallel pair is exactly one direct and one length-two link, owing to the two subdivision groups. Let \(p,q\) be the direct and length-two columns at \(a\) toward \(b\). At \(b\) their pairing has opposite sign to that at \(a\), so \(p\cdot q=0\). The singleton columns are \(R=-p-q\) on \(ac\) and \(S=-p+q\) on \(bd\), forcing \(p^2=q^2=28\) and \(R^2=S^2=56\). At \(c,d\), the two singleton columns are these same \(R,S\), so the two equations force the \(cd\) direct column to be \(p\) and its length-two start column at \(c\) to be \(q\). Now \(q\) has four own matching cells, two of each sign, and is unit because both column signs are repeated. The two subdivision axes \(i,j\) differ (the two same-sign cells \(ai,cj\) cannot share an axis), and they are not cubic axes because of repeated original edges. On \(ab,cd\), \(q=0\) by the two opposite signed-path formulas from their respective endpoints. On \(ac\) its sum is \(4\) by \(q\cdot R=-28\) and the defect pairing formula, and on \(bd\) its sum is \(-4\) by \(q\cdot S=28\); thus the singleton cells are uniformly \(+1,-1\). This supplies twelve sites of each sign including its four own cells. Any other cell on either middle axis \(i,j\) has zero \(q\) by the zero rule; any other cell meeting \(a,b,c,d\) is clean and has zero \(q\) by its own shared-axis formula. There is only one unused original axis, hence no possible further support cell. This contradicts norm 28 and excludes the doubled core. No exclusion of the simple opposite-subdivision case is asserted.

**Further restriction.** In the residual shared-midpoint profile with no component of size at least 24, [each of the four subdividing dirty cells must have an internal edge](thirteen-grid-shared-middle-nonempty.md). This still leaves the overall thirteen-grid profile open.
