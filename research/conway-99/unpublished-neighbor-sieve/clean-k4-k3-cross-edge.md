# Disjoint rooted clean \(K_4\) and \(K_3\) allow at most one cross edge

In the [rooted grid chart](big-triangle-wing.md), form the simple graph on the seven original axes in which an edge is a clean cell (a rooted rook grid). If it contains vertex-disjoint \(K_4\) and \(K_3\), then **at most one clean edge joins them**. This is an ordinary subgraph exclusion for any two cross edges, whether they meet or are disjoint. Neither Seidel rank nor the state of the other axis pairs enters.

Some applications, all rank-free:

* The all-peripheral profile left by the [twelve-grid, four-distinct-cubic reduction](triangle-wing-twelve-grid-cubic-midpoint.md) is impossible: for \(a\) the cubic center and \(x,y,z\) the peripheral middle axes, the clean \(K_4\) is on \(a,x,y,z\), the clean \(K_3\) on \(b,c,d\), and the extra clean pairs are \(xd,yb,zc\), where the short dirty paths are \(b-x-c,\ c-y-d,\ d-z-b\).
* At any twelve-grid root without a size-24-or-larger wing, \(3n_3+2n_2=18\), and the [existing bound of four distinct cubic axes](triangle-wing-four-distinct.md) leaves \(n_3=4\) or \(6\). The four-axis case above is excluded. In the six-group case \(n_2=0\); a repeated axis would have dirty degree six and so force every original axis to carry a dirty group, impossible with six groups. Hence the dirty graph is simple cubic on six distinct axes, with the seventh saturated. A simple cubic graph on six vertices has complement a degree-two graph on six, which is either the six-cycle or two triangles. The dirty \(K_{3,3}\) case gives two disjoint clean triangles and a saturated axis; add that axis to either triangle and it has three clean cross edges to the other, impossible. Thus only the dirty triangular prism remains.
* In the [thirteen-grid opposite-subdivision reduction](triangle-wing-thirteen-grid-bound.md), assume no size-24-or-larger incident component. If the dirty core has direct links \(ac,ad,bc,bd\) and two length-two paths \(a-x-b,c-y-d\) with distinct middles \(x,y\), the seventh axis \(e\) is saturated. The clean \(K_4\) on \(e,a,b,y\) and clean \(K_3\) on \(c,d,x\) contradict the cross-edge theorem. This excludes only the distinct-middle case at these ranks, not the same-middle layout, and does not assert that a twelve- or thirteen-grid vertex occurs.

## The exact 51-site base and 55-site one-edge charts

Include the root, all fourteen signed first-layer parents, and the four sites in the six K4 and three K3 clean cells, for a total of \(1+14+36=51\) sites. Test each single extra cross-clean cell in turn, giving a 55-site chart. For any two sites of this induced graph their number of common neighbours in the chart is at most \(2\) for a nonedge, and at most \(1\) for an edge. These are necessary bounds, not assumptions about unmarked adjacency. A clean cell is a square on its four ordered signed labels \(00,01,10,11\), with edges the two kinds of flips. With matching to first-layer parents known from the labels, two marked clean cells are anticomplete if they share an axis and joined by an arbitrary perfect matching otherwise. In fact every outsider to the nine-site rook grid sees it exactly once; those on a shared axis already see a first-layer parent, and those on disjoint axes see none of the five inner sites. This also explains why an *unmarked* cell sharing an axis with a clean cell is anticomplete to its four sites.

Call the three K3 cells the complementary packets. For two of them sharing axis \(j\), fix a site \(v\) in the first. Its four counterparts in the second allow respectively \(1,1,2,2\) common neighbours amongst the K4 sites: two have the same \(j\)-sign and already share that parent, while the other two do not. Each of the six K4 cells supplies one common neighbour with exactly one of the four counterparts, by its perfect matchings to both packets. The six slots thus saturate, pointwise, with multiplicity one for a same-sign pair and two for an opposite-sign pair. The 24 K4-cell sites each have a word of three complementary neighbours. Their words are distinct, since duplicate words give three common neighbours. Consequently the set of words is a zero-one \(4^3\) tensor whose three two-dimensional marginals are exactly the just-described \(1/2\) tables. Moreover its words split into six four-word *transversals*, one per K4 cell, with every symbol used once in each coordinate.

One further exact constraint locates the signed parents of every added cross-clean vertex. Fix a complementary site \(v\) on the two complementary axes \(b,c\), and a K4 axis \(i\). The two signed parents \(i^+,i^-\) are nonadjacent to \(v\) and each has exactly two common neighbours with \(v\) in the full graph. There are already three such contacts, one in each K4 cell incident to \(i\). There are no common neighbours among the root or parents, nor in \(C_{ib},C_{ic}\) by the shared-axis clean rule for \(C_{bc}\). Thus the remaining contact is unique and lies in \(C_{id}\), where \(d\) is the omitted complementary axis. Its \(i\)-parent is the *minority sign* among the three known \(i\)-contacts; in particular those three cannot have one common sign. In the checker this identifies the \(i\)-sign of the unique vertex in the additional **marked clean** cell \(id\) matched to \(v\). Unmarked cells need not be constructed or tested. Across the four \(v\)'s the minority signs are balanced (each of the three known contact columns has two of each sign), and the other \(d\)-bit has two independent binary choices, one on each minority-sign row pair.

## Exhaustive coverage of the small chart

The [standard-library certificate](clean_k4_k3_cross_edge.py) uses the following steps. It never assigns any unmarked cell.

1. Construct the \(4^3\) tensor by fixing the first symbol and enumerating all \(4\times4\) zero-one layers with the two corresponding margins. There are 34 candidates for each first symbol. After the first three layers the fourth is determined by the remaining margin (the code uses parity and the entrywise upper bound followed by the fixed total). The code separately verifies the three integer marginal matrices for every resulting tensor. There are **816** tensors. The 48 legitimate relabelings of the complementary axes (permuting three axes and independently swapping their signed parents) give **34** tensor orbits.
2. For each canonical tensor, list every four-word subset that is a transversal. Repeatedly place the first remaining word in each possible transversal containing it. This gives every unordered complete partition exactly once; there are **106** such partitions in the 34 tensor representatives.
3. Assign the six transversals to the six edges of the K4. The axis permutation group sends the first transversal's edge to \(01\); its stabilizer is transitive on \(02,03,12,13\), so the least of their four indices is placed on \(02\). This leaves 30 edge assignments per partition. The cross edges are **not** fixed by this gauge: all four K4 endpoint choices and all three K3 endpoint choices are tried individually after each base core is formed.
4. Try all \(4!\) signed labelings of the four words in each cell. The optional first-site \(00\) gauge at the first cell only swaps the two parent signs independently on its two axes; later labels and the added matching cells are still unrestricted. For two K4 cells with a common axis, a word overlap of two requires opposite signs on that shared axis, since otherwise the parent would be a third common neighbour. For disjoint K4 cells, try each of the 24 matchings. The code tests the pair capacity between the two squares (word overlap and the common neighbours in the two squares) and between one square and a complementary site (its own square, the complementary square, and the possible coincidence in the matched opposite K4 square). These tests depend only on a square's internal antipodal pairing, so the three choices can be cached. It also tests the minority-sign constraint as soon as the three necessary cells are labeled. Every surviving 51-site chart, including all parents, is checked directly with adjacency bitsets.
5. For each of the 12 possible extra cross cells, label its four sites by their complementary neighbours. Use the forced first bit above and both choices of the other bit on each pair. The cell shares an axis with all but one complementary packet (whose matching is now fixed), and must be matched to the three K4 packets not touching its K4 axis. Enumerate every one-per-packet row whose chosen old neighbours still have one available common-neighbour slot per pair. Join four such rows with no repetition in a target packet; this covers every possible perfect matching to that packet. Add the forced square and parent edges and test every pair in the enlarged chart.

The replay has **768** verified 51-site labeled base charts. Of these, **576** admit no choice of extra clean cross edge under the 55-site pair-capacity test, and **192** admit exactly one axis pair for an extra cell. No chart admits two, even by separate tests of the two proposed edges, so they could not both hold in the same underlying graph. These are counts of necessary labeled charts in this gauge, not graph realizations, graph-isomorphism classes, or assertions that the surviving one-edge candidates can be completed.

All pruning is by the displayed graph capacities or relabeling. In particular no numerical solver, timed search, or additional hypothesis about the actual graph enters the conclusion.

Run `python3 clean_k4_k3_cross_edge.py` to reproduce the coverage and final counts.
