# If a triangle wing has two two-edge cuts, its three pieces each have twelve sites

In any Conway graph a triangle T admits at most two distinct **cutsets** of size two in its cubic wing D. If two occur, the whole wing is connected, the cutsets are disjoint, and removing them gives exactly three twelve-site pieces. In particular a cut with connected 18-site shores on both sides is its triangle's only cutset of size two. This does not rule out one cut or the 12+12+12 arrangement. The numerics are conditional on the no-six-site-shore theorem; the exclusion below of a six-site middle does not use it.

**Two cuts and their leaves.** By the [thin-cut theorem](arbitrary-wing-thin-cuts.md), each cutset consists of two edges in one particular matching between different physical wings, and emits an original Fano line. The two participating roots contact nonadjacent signed pairs and the third is in its anticomplete fifteen-set. At a fixed T a line determines its cutset: of the two positives and negatives on the wing, only the opposite-wing positive–negative pairings can be cut edges. Hence at most two cutsets occur since at most two lines are emitted globally. If both occur their Fano lines f,g are orthogonal. Their anticomplete fifteen-sets are disjoint, so their missed roots are different and their cutsets use different physical matchings. The cutsets are disjoint.

In a bridgeless connected graph a size-two cut divides the graph into two connected shores. If two such cuts cross, their four nonempty intersection regions each have boundary at least two. The sum of the four boundaries is twice the total number of edges between regions, hence at most eight since these edges come from the two cuts of size two. Equality gives four connected regions of boundary two (disconnected regions would need at least four). Their connected loopless quotient has four vertices of degree two, so is a 4-cycle. The boundary of a region is then a third size-two cutset, distinct from the two original crossing partitions. This is impossible here. Thus two cuts in one component can be oriented to have disjoint connected leaves U and V; there is no edge between them, since it would lie in both cutsets. Their remaining middle is connected: any paths passing through an outer leaf must stay on its (connected) complementary shore when testing connectivity on the other shore; more directly, if the middle were disconnected, each of its components would have boundary at least two and together at most four. Two such components would form another four-vertex 2-regular quotient with the leaves, yielding additional two-edge cutsets. There is a nonempty middle, since a partition into just U,V would use the same two edges for both cuts.

Label physical wings A,B,C so that f cuts AB and g cuts BC, and orient each field positive inside its own outer leaf. All three pieces meet each physical wing equally, in an even number of sites: for the leaves this is the cut parity argument, and subtraction from their connected component gives the middle. Each component has the same balance. Any component with 6j sites has exactly j neighbors at each far vertex, by [component balance](triangle-complement.md). If U has k sites per physical wing, any far x sees k/2−f(x) sites of U; the analogous formula holds for V.

**A middle of six sites is impossible.** Suppose the middle has two sites per physical wing. Subtract the contributions of the two leaves from their whole component by the preceding formulas: every far x has exactly
```
1 + f(x) + g(x)
```
neighbors in the six-site middle. This is negative at the common (−−) site if it is far. No signed site is on T; the only physical wing where both fields have endpoints is B. Hence the common (−−) site b lies in the middle of B. The other two middle boundary endpoints are a in A (f-negative), and c in C (g-negative). The shared (++) site p is far: the positive leaves are disjoint. It is adjacent to b by the [opposite doubles theorem](shared-norm-18/rank36-four-orthogonal-cut.md).

Write a′,b′,c′ for the respective same-wing mates. The three mate edges are internal, the AB matching remaining in the middle is a′b′, and the BC matching there is b′c′. AC is a full matching. If AC crosses, a′ and c′ have three common neighbors a,c,b′, impossible. Thus AC consists of ac and a′c′. The unsigned mates form a transversal triangle.

**A four-chart terminal check.** Use the [canonical 24-site support union](two-fano-core.md) with the signs above. For the three roots of T record their sets of neighbors among the 24 signed sites. The first is an f-only nonedge-contact pair (positive and negative), the last a g-only nonedge-contact pair. The middle root contacts one site of each sign for both; the two negatives agree at b. In particular the three root-contact sets are disjoint, as each root pair already has its common neighbor in T. For each Fano its cross-match between these sets uses the positive in one wing and the negative in the other. The corresponding four edges are the only possible edges between different pieces on these seven signed wing points. In the [standalone replay](thin_cut_six_middle.py) enumerate the four respective signed endpoint pairs on each Fano subject to these tests, the one-contact-per-wing property of each wing site, and the common-neighbor upper bounds with T and the fixed core. Sign and physical orientation here are by description, not by assuming graph automorphisms.

All 17 other signed sites are far. For each such x record the required remaining mate contact count 1+f(x)+g(x)−number of its neighbors among a,b,c. It must be between zero and three. There remain four labeled charts with the required edge ac. Each of a′,b′,c′ is unsigned, so it sees one of each sign or no signs of each Fano; it cannot miss both since the fifteen-sets are disjoint. List all such sets (size 2,3,4) permitted by the core pair bounds and its prescribed contacts to the seven already known signed wing points. The three lists must jointly hit every far signed point exactly its required number of times. There is exactly one possible triple per chart. In every case b′ sees p, the common (++); this is impossible since b and b′ already share their physical parent as the cap of their mate edge, and b also sees p. The arithmetic is only small sets and equality comparisons, not search for a full graph. The helper two_fano_core.py must be in the same directory.

Finally use the independent [no-six-site-shore theorem](no-six-site-wing-two-cut.md). Two cutsets cannot be in different components, since each affected component would have at least 24 sites, whereas the whole wing has 36. In the single-component case each leaf has at least 12 sites, and its middle, a positive multiple of six but not six, also has at least 12. This exhausts all 36 sites, proving the assertion.
