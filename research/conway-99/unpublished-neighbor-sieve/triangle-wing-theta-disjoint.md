# The last two-cubic theta needs a nongrid doubly covered square

The final remaining local type is excluded by the [subsequent positive-edge argument](triangle-wing-no-two-cubic.md). The analysis below gives the intermediate reductions.

Continue the [theta path classification](triangle-wing-theta-paths.md) and the [no separate cycles refinement](triangle-wing-theta-no-cycles.md). At a root \(o\) with no wing component of size at least 24 and exactly two cubic split vertices, the entire dirty split graph is the theta with paths of lengths \(1,3,3\); all other cells are clean. Write \(a,b\) for its distinct endpoint axes, \(p,q\) for the start defect columns of the two long paths, and \(r=v_{ab}\) for the direct edge. Then \(p+q+r=0\), \(p^2=q^2=28\), and the two long ends on each path are matching cells. The middle cells have two edges but might be paths plus isolates.

**Theorem.** The two length-three paths must have disjoint internal original axes. At least one middle cell is a two-edge path plus an isolate. Consequently each such root lies on a doubly prism-covered square contained in no grid. In the Fano-free case, if \(S\) is the number of nonordinary unit positive-charge fields of cost 23, there are at most \(4S\) such roots on the graph. This does not eliminate the last local layout when \(S>0\).

## Ruling out crossed internal axes

The two internal-axis pairs can share at most one axis: sharing both would repeat their original middle edge. Their initial axes are distinct from each other and their terminal axes are distinct from each other, since each endpoint's three cells differ. Thus sharing one axis means it is the initial internal axis of one path and the terminal internal axis of the other. The first defect vanishes on the other path's endpoint cell at this shared internal axis by the [degree-two zero rule](triangle-wing-theta-paths.md#defects-and-short-chains). The shared-end Gram formula then gives \(p\cdot q=0\). Since \(r=-p-q\), the direct cell \(C_{ab}\) is empty, by \(r^2=56=14(4-m_{ab})\).

Outside its own middle cell, a length-three path defect has absolute value at most one. A value \(+2\) or larger would make the reversed middle column at most \(-2\), possible only in its own middle cell. A value \(-2\) or smaller would require the own cells of both of its equal end columns, impossible. On \(C_{ab}\), the shared-axis coordinate formulas give \(p,q\ge0\); here \(r=-2\), hence \(p=q=1\) at all four sites.

Each of the two long fields is zero on the other's end cells, because zero inner product means no cross edges at either endpoint. It is also zero on the other's middle cell, by the shared internal axis and the degree-two zero rule. Besides \(C_{ab}\), common support can only be on the cell \(C_{de}\) of the two axes \(d,e\) unused by either path. Indeed any clean cell touching \(a\) or \(b\) is in another split group and both defects vanish there; a clean cell meeting one of the internal axes of either path has its corresponding defect zero. The fields already have four agreements on \(C_{ab}\), so orthogonality forces all four sites of \(C_{de}\) to have opposite nonzero unit signs for \(p,q\).

Choose \(y\in C_{ab}\). Relative to \(p\), it has exactly one neighbour of value \(-1\) in each of the two \(p\)-end cells: use the shared-axis formula and \(p_y=1\). It has no neighbour in the \(p\)-middle cell, because the reversed middle column evaluates to \(-1\) at \(y\) and their axes are disjoint. The other long path has \(p=0\) on all of its cells, and \(C_{ab}\) is empty. All remaining possible \(p\)-support lies in the three cells on the axes outside its own four axes, including \(C_{de}\). They are clean and disjoint in axes from \(ab\). Each such nine-site rook grid supplies exactly one neighbour to \(y\), in the four-site cell: it saturates every internal adjacent or nonadjacent pair's original common-neighbour count and has 90 crossing edges over 90 outsiders, so every outsider meets it exactly once; the root and first-layer parents on the grid's two axes cannot meet this \(y\). These three neighbours have values \(0,\pm1\). For \(Ap_y=-4\), the unique \(C_{de}\) neighbour must therefore have \(p=-1\): with \(+1\), even two other negatives would contribute at least \(-1\), whereas the three cells must contribute \(-2\). By the same argument this very same neighbour must have \(q=-1\). This contradicts \(q=-p\) on \(C_{de}\), and excludes the crossed case.

## The exact overlap identity when the internal axes are disjoint

Write \(m=e(C_{ab})\). Then \(r^2=56-14m\) gives \(p\cdot q=-7m\). Away from the seven dirty cells there is no common support: a clean cell that meets \(a\) or \(b\) has both columns zero, and any clean cell not meeting those endpoints must meet at least one internal axis of the two disjoint paths, since just one axis remains unused.

For a dirty cell with defect \(v_f\), the inner product against the other long field is \(-7\) times the sum of that field's entries on \(C_f\). Indeed \(v_f=-M1_{S_f}\), \(M=3I-A+J/9\), and the other field is a neutral eigenvector with eigenvalue \(7\) for \(M\) and vanishes on the root and first layer. At either \(p\)-end cell, the column is \(p\) and \(p=-1\) on its four sites, so its contribution to \(p\cdot q\) is \(-m\). Similarly for both \(q\)-end cells. At the \(p\)-middle cell the column is \(-p\), and the sum of \(q\) there is \(-m\). If it is a matching, \(p=1\) on all four sites, contributing \(-m\). Otherwise its two edges form a path plus isolate, and the \(p\)-values are \(2,0,1,1\), at the isolate, the path centre, and the two ends. The contribution is then \(-m+\delta_p\) with \(\delta_p=q_{\mathrm{isolate}}-q_{\mathrm{centre}}\). Define \(\delta_p=0\) in the matching case and \(\delta_q\) symmetrically. They lie in \([-2,2]\).

On \(C_{ab}\), \(p,q\in\{0,1\}\) by their shared-end formulas and the preceding unit-outside-middle argument. The relation \(p+q=2-d_{C_{ab}}\) shows the product equals one exactly at the isolates of that cell. Let \(u\) count those isolates. Summing the five contributions yields
\[
-7m=-6m+\delta_p+\delta_q+u,\qquad
\delta_p+\delta_q=-m-u.
\]
Here \(u=4\) for \(m=0\), \(u=2\) for \(m=1\), and \(u=0\) or \(1\) for \(m=2\). In every case at least one middle cell is a path plus isolate. If \(m=0,1\), or if \(m=2\) and the direct cell has an isolate, both middle cells are of this nonmatching type.

## Charging the roots to actual short marks

The [nongrid double-square correspondence](doubly-covered-square-short-mark.md#literal-eight-site-set-and-its-single-mark) identifies every rooted two-edge path-plus-isolate cell with one doubly covered nongrid square through its root. Explicitly the root, the path's degree-two label and that label's two first-layer parents form the square; the two sign flips of the label supply its two prism directions. The two-edge cell precludes a grid on those lines. The same double-square correspondence gives four rooted cells per square and, on the Fano-free face, injects the squares into the \(S\) nonordinary unit cost-23 fields. Thus at most \(4S\) roots have exactly two cubic split vertices and no split degree at least four. A root with two nonmatching middle cells uses two distinct rooted square incidences, one for each distinct axis pair.

There is an immediate aggregate consequence. Let \(H\) count the actual order-18 wing components over all triangles (a triangle with two such components contributes two), let \(L\) count triangles with a wing component of order at least 24, and let \(R\) be the number of roots incident with such a large triangle. Thus \(R\le3L\). The earlier [large wing theorem](big-triangle-wing.md) and the split handshake lemma give at least two cubic split vertices at every root not in \(R\); unless one of the theta roots just bounded, such a root has at least four. The [global bound of 118 grids](../rook-array-exclusion/rectangle-class-forest.md) also bounds the number of theta roots by 75, since each of them has fourteen grids and each grid uses nine roots. Each 18-site component is counted at its three triangle vertices. Hence, on the Fano-free face,
\[
3H\ \ge\ 4(99-R)-2\min(75,4S),\qquad
H+4L\ \ge\ \left\lceil\frac{396-2\min(75,4S)}3\right\rceil.
\]
The weaker \(H+4L\ge82\) holds without Fano-freeness, using the 75-root bound alone. At the four rank-42 two-type possibilities \(S=2,5,8,11\), these lower bounds are respectively \(127,119,111,103\). They count components, not only triangles, and do not yet imply that a component of order at least 24 exists.
