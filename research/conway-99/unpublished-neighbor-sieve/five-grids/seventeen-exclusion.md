# At most fifteen rook grids at any Conway point

**Theorem.** In any hypothetical Conway graph each point belongs to at most fifteen induced nine-point rook grids. If fifteen occur, the six pairs of axes failing to span grids form $K_{2,3}$, leaving two axes isolated. Both of those lines belong to six grids and have all twelve possible prism partners.

The [regular-cell common-neighbor check](fifteen-grid-exclusion.md) subsequently excludes the fifteen-grid diagram.

## The four-cell defect

Fix a point. Use the [cell vectors and unconditional exclusion of 21 grids](complete-point-exclusion.md), this time with the sign $v_e=-G1_{S_e}$. The vectors vanish on the root and its fourteen neighbors; the sum over cells incident to any one axis is zero. At a point of cell $h$, the entry in column $e$ is its number of neighbors in $C_e$ minus $2[h=e]+[h,e\text{ disjoint}]$. This follows by counting its signed parents in $S_e$. A clean cell has zero vector. A point of a clean cell has no links to another cell sharing an axis, since its common-neighbor quota with each parent there is already attained.

Suppose first there are seventeen grids. The four nonzero columns must form a four-cycle: their axis graph has minimum nonzero degree two and a triangle alone would force zero by alternation. Label the two sides $0,1$ and $2,3$, and the three remaining axes $4,5,6$. Call the four cells of the cycle dirty. Their columns have alternating signs and otherwise agree. For a dirty vertex of internal cell degree $d$, the entry in its own column is $d-2$. In the opposite dirty cell the column is identical and the cells are disjoint, giving $d-1$ neighbors there. Thus $d\ge1$. Each dirty cell has at most two internal edges by the [nine-point density gap](../../../conway-99-involution/grid-density-gap/README.md). It follows that it is a perfect matching. Each dirty site has zero neighbors in its opposite dirty cell and one in each of its two adjacent dirty cells.

Write $K_k$ for the cell on the two axes of $4,5,6$ other than $k$, and call these three cells the cores. Write $L,R$ for the cells $01,23$, and $F_{i,k}$ for the cell on active axis $i$ and inactive axis $k$. Call $L,R$ and the twelve mixed cells $F_{i,k}$ regular. Each regular cell shares an active axis with a dirty cell, and its vertices see no vertex of that dirty cell. Its entries in all four dirty columns are therefore zero. Against a disjoint dirty cell this means exactly one neighbor. In the reverse direction the same is true by the grid's one-neighbor property. In particular every regular cell is matched bijectively to each of its ten disjoint cells.

Each dirty vector has squared norm 28, and its entries on the sixteen dirty sites use 16 of this. Only the twelve core sites can supply the rest. At any such site the counts into the dirty cells alternate between $1+v$ and $1-v$ for some integer $v$, hence $|v|\le1$. Thus every core site sees two points in each of two opposite dirty cells and none in the other two. In each individual core there are two sites of each choice of opposite pair: each dirty cell sends four edges to that core.

## Diagonal mates and their caps

A point of $K_k$ has ten neighbors besides its already-paired signed parents and internal square neighbors. Four are in the dirty cells just described; the other six are one in each of $L,R,F_{0,k},F_{1,k},F_{2,k},F_{3,k}$. If none of the four dirty contacts are adjacent, all four need different mates among the four mixed contacts, since neither $L$ nor $R$ is disjoint from any dirty cell. The $L,R$ contacts must then be mates. Each of the four edges of the matching between $L$ and $R$ has just one triangle, so at most four of the twelve core points can have this property.

The other eight or more core sites must complete an internal dirty edge. The two opposite dirty cells have no link to each other. There are only eight internal dirty edges and each can be completed only once. Consequently exactly eight different core points complete one such edge each, and the other four complete all four $L,R$ edges. Every dirty edge is diagonal, since a single-sign flip would already have its triangle at its shared signed parent.

Each core partitions the four points of a particular dirty cell into two pairs according to their core contacts. An internal diagonal pair can share at most one core vertex. Every other pair in that dirty cell differs in one sign and already has a common signed parent, so it also can share at most one core vertex. The three partitions use six pair occurrences and must use all six different pairs. Thus the two internal diagonals of dirty cell $D$ are capped at the same core $K_{k(D)}$. Opposite dirty cells must have different $k(D)$: their contacts in that core are at the same two core points, which cannot each complete two dirty edges.

## Counts involving a core or a dirty cell

For a dirty cell with axes $i,j$, put $u=1-i$, $v=5-j$, where $i\in\{0,1\}$ and $j\in\{2,3\}$. At a core point whose dirty contacts in this cell are not internally paired, those two contacts must pair with the points of $F_{u,k},F_{v,k}$. They cannot pair with the opposite dirty contacts. If neither dirty cell uses an internal pair, the remaining $L,R$ contacts pair. If one uses an internal pair, the $L,R$ contacts cannot pair, since all their edges already have their cap at the four former sites. For the dirty cell $ij$ whose contacts still need mates, the remaining mixed sites are $F_{i,k},F_{j,k}$; $L$ pairs with the latter, $R$ with the former. There are two core sites of each opposite-pair choice. This determines all counts of triangles through a core meeting three disjoint cells.

At a point of the dirty cell $ij$, its two signed parents are paired with its contacts in the two adjacent dirty cells: the internal diagonal uses the opposite sign on each common axis, leaving the same-sign partner on the link to the other dirty cell. The diagonal mate pairs with the cap in $K_c$, $c=k(D)$. The remaining contacts are one in each of the other two cores and one in each of $F_{u,k},F_{v,k}$ for $k=4,5,6$. Write $a,b$ for the other two core indices. Each core can pair only with a mixed contact carrying its omitted axis. These two core mates must use one $u$ and one $v$, since the remaining four mixed contacts have to pair across those two groups. If the core mates are $F_{u,a}, F_{v,b}$, the remaining pairs are forced to be $(F_{u,b},F_{v,c})$ and $(F_{u,c},F_{v,a})$, since two mixed cells sharing an inactive axis have no links. There are two choices according to the ordering of $a,b$. Each occurs at two of the four dirty sites, by the preceding two-mate count at $K_a$.

Finally each regular point chooses a Petersen matching on its ten disjoint cell neighbors, just as in the [21-grid calculation](complete-point-exclusion.md). The only additional possibility to check would be partners in different dirty cells sharing an axis. Their link, if present, already has its triangle at their shared signed parent and cannot have the regular point as cap.

There are $3\cdot2\cdot3\cdot2=36$ assignments of capping core indices to the four dirty cells. The [checker](seventeen_grids.py) computes the triangle counts above and tries every multiset of four Petersen matchings at each regular cell, 126 choices. In each of the 36 cases, the prescribed counts involving a nonregular cell leave exactly one multiset at each of the fourteen regular cells. These local answers must agree on the counts for three disjoint regular cells. Twelve assignments disagree on 28 such triples each, and 24 disagree on 35 each. This excludes seventeen grids without a coverage assumption.

## Five or six dirty cells

The dirty edges support the tuple $v_e$ in the kernel of their unsigned vertex-edge incidence matrix. If their nonzero columns are all scalar multiples of one vector, any two incident edges must have scalars of opposite sign. In fact each dirty cell has a site of internal degree less than two, giving a strictly negative own-column entry, while every other column sharing an axis is nonnegative at that site. Each axis then has dirty degree at most two. The zero axis sums force all nonisolated degrees to be two and signs to alternate around each cycle.

For five dirty edges the graph must be connected, since every nonempty component supporting such columns has at least four edges. On five vertices minimum degree two would force a five-cycle, which cannot alternate. On four vertices it must be $K_4$ minus an edge. Its unsigned incidence matrix has rank four and kernel dimension one, again impossible because degree three occurs. Here the unsigned incidence rank on a connected graph is the number of vertices, less one in the bipartite case, as follows by alternating any relation among the rows. This excludes sixteen grids.

For six dirty edges the same connectivity argument applies. A one-dimensional column span requires a six-cycle. Each own-cell vertex then has degree at least one, since another disjoint edge with the same column exists. The density gap again makes each dirty cell a perfect matching. Each nonzero column has magnitude one on the 24 dirty sites. All other entries vanish: every clean cell shares an axis with a dirty cell since only one inactive axis remains. This contradicts the squared norm 28.

If the column span has dimension at least two, the incidence kernel does also. The rank formula and the simple graph size bound leave $K_4$ or $K_{2,3}$. In $K_4$ the axis sums imply equality of columns on each pair of opposite edges, with the three resulting vectors $p,q,r$ summing to zero. The opposite-edge argument again gives perfect internal matchings. At each of the 24 dirty points the entries $(p,q,r)$ consist of $-1,1,0$ in some order: the own column is $-1$ and the other two are nonnegative. At a clean cell meeting an active axis all three entries vanish. This leaves twelve sites on pairs of the three inactive axes. At each such site the counts into the six dirty cells are $1+p,1+p,1+q,1+q,1+r,1+r$. A value two among $p,q,r$ would give three neighbors in each of two opposite dirty cells, and hence two completed internal matched edges. But the other four of its ten external neighbors lie in four mixed cells sharing the third inactive axis and are pairwise nonadjacent. Those four need four distinct mates among the six dirty contacts, allowing only one internal matched pair. Thus on these twelve sites $p,q,r$ lie in $\{-1,0,1\}$ and sum to zero. The total squared norm of the three vectors is at most $2(24+12)=72$, less than $3\cdot28$. This excludes $K_4$. In the remaining $K_{2,3}$ diagram each isolated axis belongs to six distinct grids, accounting for all twelve disjoint prism partner triangles on its line.

## Fifteen grids force at least 36 uncovered squares

In the surviving local diagram label the rows of $K_{2,3}$ by $0,1$, its columns by $2,3,4$ and the two inactive axes by $5,6$. The column at $0j$ is the negative of the column at $1j$ by the axis sum at $j$. The three columns at row zero sum to zero. Use the eight clean cells $01$, the four row-inactive cells and the three column-column cells. At a point in any one of the eight, all dirty vector entries are zero. For a cell containing a row axis this follows because it meets all three dirty cells in that row. For a column-column cell, it meets the dirty cells from two columns, and the vanishing sum forces the third to vanish too. Thus each of the eight cells has a bijective matching to **every** disjoint cell, including dirty ones.

Let $a,b$ be disjoint clean cells. If all three cells $c$ disjoint from both are clean, or both endpoints are among the eight cells just distinguished, all three two-step maps through $c$ are permutations. The [symmetric permutation argument](complete-point-exclusion.md) again forces even triangle counts on all three triples. Moreover, if at most one such $c$ is dirty, no two such third cells have a connecting edge, since they intersect and at least one of any pair is clean. The [aligned-square argument](complete-point.md) then shows that even one covered aligned square on $a,b$ forces all four triangles at the matching to use the same third cell. In particular, a triangle count of two on one triple forces at least two uncovered squares using those two cells.

At each point of each distinguished cell, the ten neighbors in disjoint cells must pair into triangles. Two different cells supplying mates must either be disjoint or both dirty, since a clean cell has no links to any intersecting cell. Allow all pairings with this property; this may admit extra candidates. At each cell choose a multiset of four such matchings. Keep the evenness restrictions on triangle counts just proved and require the eight cells to agree on their counts for every common triple. The [checker](fifteen_grids.py) regenerates all possibilities. Ninety distinct count profiles remain. The numbers of qualifying cell pairs forced to have uncovered aligned squares in these profiles are 18, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 and 28, with respective multiplicities 6, 30, 12, 12, 12, 12 and 6. Distinct cell pairs give distinct squares. Thus a point on fifteen grids forces at least 36 uncovered squares in the graph. In particular every point of a fully covered graph belongs to at most fourteen grids.

## Fourteen grids force an uncovered square through the point itself

Suppose all squares at a point $o$ are covered and exactly seven of its axis pairs are dirty. Use the [covered-root flow identities](../../../conway-99-involution/uncovered-squares/covered-root/Theorem.md). Each dirty arc gives a ternary column $v_e$ of squared norm 28 with value $-1$ on its own four sites. The sum at each source, and separately at each target, vanishes. Incoming columns are orthogonal to outgoing columns at the same underlying axis. Also $\langle v_e,v_f\rangle=-7\sum_{u\in C_e}v_f(u)$.

Draw a bipartite graph on source and target copies with one edge per dirty arc. All nonisolated vertices have degree at least two. A component needs at least four edges, so seven edges give a connected graph. If one side had two vertices, each vertex opposite it would have degree two and the number of edges would be even. Consequently there must be three vertices on each side. The graph is $K_{3,3}$ with two independent edges removed. Between the two degree-three vertices there is a single edge and two paths of length three.

Write $x$ for the single edge's column, and $y,z$ for the first columns of the two longer paths at the same end. Each path alternates signs, so its three columns are respectively $y,-y,y$ or $z,-z,z$. We have $x+y+z=0$. All three columns have squared norm 28, giving pairwise inner products $-14$. In particular no two of the seven arc columns are orthogonal. The three underlying source axes must therefore be disjoint from the three target axes.

At any site where $y,z$ are both nonzero, their ternary values must have opposite signs because $x=-y-z$ is also ternary. Their dot product requires exactly fourteen such sites. None can be in the cell of the single edge, since $x=-1$ there. In each of the three $y$-path cells, $y$ has constant sign and magnitude one. The Gram identity with $z$ gives absolute sum two for $z$ there, and its nonzero entries have the opposite sign to $y$. Hence exactly two sites in each of these cells contribute. The three $z$-path cells give another six sites. No clean cell can contribute: its two axes include one of the six different path axes, and the column of an incident dirty path edge vanishes on this clean cell. Root and parents also contribute zero. This leaves only twelve sites instead of fourteen.

Thus a point lying on fourteen grids has an uncovered square incident with it. Together with the fifteen-grid count above, a graph in which every square belongs to a triangular prism has at most **thirteen grids at each point**.
