# A global bound on repeated emissions of an independent-positive three-cut mark

The [balanced three-cut construction](three-cut-original-fields.md) sends an oriented boundary of size three at a graph triangle to an original unit charge-three field of squared norm 39. Fix one such field b with independent positives. It is emitted by at most seven oriented balanced boundaries over the whole graph, or at most six among the occurrences where all three positive boundary sites are in transversal wing triangles. The latter includes each nontrivial balanced bond at a saturated triangle. Thus C actual unordered nontrivial bonds over all saturated triangles force at least ceil(C/3) distinct original positive-charge fields of that subtype. This does not assert C>0.

Let \(P=\{p_1,p_2,p_3\}\) be the positive sites of one fixed emitted field \(b\), and write \(N,Z\) for its negative and zero sites. The signed-unit norm and mass give 36 negative sites. The charge equation \(Ab=-4b-6\mathbf1\) and independence of \(P\) give ten negative and four zero neighbors at each \(p_i\). Put \(D_i=\{x\in N:N(x)\cap P=\{p_i\}\}\). In an emitting root triangle \(T\), label its negative sites \(x_i\) by their own positive wing dart \(p_i\). Let \(n_i\) be the negative boundary dart paired with \(p_i\) by the mate matching in the wing of \(x_i\).

Both \(x_i,n_i\) belong to \(D_i\). For \(x_i\) this follows from the one-root contact of a wing site. For \(n_i\), a different positive dart would have to meet it across the balanced cut in an inter-wing edge, whereas all three cut edges are within-wing mates. The edge \(p_ix_i\) has its unique triangle cap at \(n_i\). A fixed \(x_i\) therefore determines \(p_i,n_i\). It also has exactly three negative neighbors, by the charge equation, and the other two apart from \(n_i\) are the two other vertices of \(T\). Thus distinct emitting roots are vertex disjoint. At a fixed \(T\) the positive sites recover the oriented cut edges as in the original theorem, so there is just one bond per such root. If \(m\) of the roots emit the fixed field, then \(m\le2\alpha_i\) for every \(i\), where \(\alpha_i\) is the number of mate pairs in \(N(p_i)\) lying wholly in \(D_i\).

For \(i\ne j\), \(p_i\) and \(x_j\) are nonadjacent. Their two common neighbors include \(x_i\). The second must be zero-valued: the negative neighbors of \(x_j\) are the two other roots and \(n_j\), and of these only \(x_i\) sees \(p_i\). No positive site is adjacent to \(p_i\). Let \(a_{ij}\) be the number of common zero neighbors of \(p_i,p_j\). Each of the four zero neighbors of \(p_i\) sees at most two of the candidate \(x_j\) sites, or at most one if it also neighbors \(p_j\). Thus \(m\le8-a_{ij}\). If \(m=8\), all \(a_{ij}=0\), and each two positives share two negative neighbors. Since \(|D_i|\ge8\) and \(p_i\) has just ten negative neighbors, the two pair intersections incident to \(i\) must coincide. This gives two negative vertices adjacent to all three positives, contrary to the two-common-neighbor bound. So \(m\le7\).

For the six cap, restrict to the indicated occurrences and suppose exactly seven emitters. In each, the transversal triangle containing \(p_i\) consists of \(p_i\) and two zero sites. Its two inter-wing mates are on the same shore as \(p_i\), cannot be the other positive boundary sites because \(P\) is independent, and cannot be negative boundary sites. Those two zero sites are a zero mate pair in \(N(p_i)\). They are precisely the second common neighbors of \(p_i\) with the respective two other roots, one for each. A fixed zero pair can serve at most four emitters, since either endpoint can see at most two candidate roots of each of the other two labels. Therefore at each \(p_i\) there are exactly two zero mate pairs, used four and three times. A zero site shared with another positive must lie in the three-use pair, because its capacity is at most three. A zero adjacent to all three positives would restrict that pair to at most two uses and is impossible.

Now \(a_{ij}\le1\) for every \(i\ne j\) because \(m=7\). The negative intersection sizes are \(2-a_{ij}\). Each \(D_i\) contains at least four private mate pairs, so at most two negative neighbors of \(p_i\) are nonprivate. The triple negative intersection has size at most one. Consequently either all three \(a_{ij}=1\), or exactly one of them is zero and there is one triple negative neighbor. In the first case the three shared zero sites are distinct, since a triple shared zero was excluded. At each positive, its two shared zero sites lie in its three-use pair, so they are adjacent. The three zeros form a triangle, and one edge would have both the third zero and its corresponding positive as common neighbors, impossible. In the second case the positive pair with \(a_{ij}=0\) shares two negatives. At \(p_i\) these are the two remaining neighbors after the four private negative pairs and the two zero pairs. They must be adjacent as mates, but already share the two positives \(p_i,p_j\), also impossible. This proves the six cap.
