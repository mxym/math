# A rook grid cannot have nine exceptional points

Together with the [moving-pair obstruction](../saturated-choice.md), this forces a saturated triangle whenever all squares are covered.

Assume full square coverage. Call a point exceptional if its seven incident lines have the local orientation diagram 733007422869 from the [covered-grid choice](../covered-grid-choice.md). No induced three-by-three rook grid can consist entirely of exceptional points. In particular the exceptional eleven-grid alternative in that reduction is impossible. Under the temporary assumption that no triangle has twelve prism partners, this forces a triangle with eight partners belonging to exactly one grid. The moving-pair argument then treats that case.

## The five pairs at a grid point

Suppose for contradiction that all nine points of a grid $B$ are exceptional. Each point outside $B$ sees exactly one of its points. Every pair in $B$ already has its full common-neighbor quota there, and its 90 outgoing edges reach 90 outside sites. For $p$ in $B$ write $F_p$ for its ten outside neighbors. They form five mate pairs because the other four neighbors of $p$ are matched inside the grid. Call these pairs axes. For adjacent $p,q$ in $B$ the edges between $F_p,F_q$ form a perfect matching: a point in $F_p$ has $p$ as one common neighbor with $q$ and needs exactly one in $F_q$. For opposite $p,q$ (different rows and columns) the point needs two neighbors in $F_q$. These belong to distinct axes since mates have their unique common neighbor at $q$.

In the exceptional diagram both grid axes point to all five outside axes. Label the latter by $\mathbb Z/5$ so that their ten arcs are $i\to i+1,i+2$. At either grid axis the five outgoing cells carry the pair permutation in the [covered-spread proof](../covered-spread.md). Its undirected cycle must consist of the sides $\{i,i+1\}$ of this pentagon. For the first grid axis the [certificate](pentagon.json.gz) eliminates each of the other 21 undirected derangement patterns from the same cell-sum and Gram equations. Interchanging the two grid axes preserves the diagram, giving the same assertion at the other axis.

Consider the triangle on a grid line. In the [triangle-neighborhood description](../covered-square.md) the ten outside sites at each of its three roots are all on moving axes. Transfers between each two of its three outside fibers match whole pairs. The product of the transfers once around the triangle gives the moving permutation on axes. This is precisely the outgoing-cell permutation: the two nonroot fibers are labeled by transferring the signed partners from the first, and their cross matching takes positive cell pairs to negative ones. The starting vertex on the line can be changed by conjugating by one transfer. Thus every edge transfer on axes carries the canonical pentagon at one endpoint to the one at the other. In pentagon coordinates each transfer is in the ten-element dihedral group, and the product on every row or column triangle is rotation by one step in either direction.

## Listing the transfers

Number the grid in row-major order starting at zero. Identify the pentagons along the eight tree edges from the first column's top point to its other two points and from the first point of each row to its other two points. In these coordinates all eight maps are identities. Four edges remain free, from the top point of each of the last two columns to the other two points of that column. Each has ten choices. The third edge of each of the six lines is determined by its first two maps and the sign of its one-step rotation. There are therefore $10^4 2^6=640000$ normalized tables. Taking row and column permutations, transposition and a simultaneous change of pentagon coordinates leaves 1,364 representatives. At each of the nine points its tournament in the chosen pentagon coordinates is either $i\to i+1,i+2$ or its reverse. Allow all 512 sign assignments for each representative.

## Which neighbors can occur at one site

Fix $y$ on axis $i$ of $F_s$. Over each of the four positions adjacent to $s$ its contact is on the transferred axis. Over each of the four opposite positions $q$, its two contacts determine an ordered pair of axes, the tail and head of their arc at $q$. An unsigned word consists of $i$ and those four pairs.

If contacts at adjacent positions $p,q$ have matching axes, the two contacts must be adjacent. The nonmatched pair of sites on matching axes already has two common neighbors, their respective mates, and $y$ would be a third. Such forced edges form a matching on the contacts because the neighborhood of $y$ is a union of disjoint edges.

The tail contact over an opposite $q$ cannot occur in any of these forced edges. Indeed $y$ belongs to the dirty four-cell of its two contacts in $F_q$. Its internal flip preserves its tail contact. The unique common neighbor of $y$ and this contact is the flipped point in that cell. All four points of the cell lie over positions opposite $q$: each sees two sites of $F_q$, which accounts for both its common neighbors with $q$. A point lying over a position adjacent to $q$ would also share that grid neighbor with $q$. These restrictions are imposed when generating the words. Signs of individual mates are not needed.

## Incidence equations

Give each allowed word at $s$ a nonnegative multiplicity. The sum at each of the five initial axes $i$ is two. For opposite $q,s$ and any two axes $a,b$ at $q$, write $n_{s,q}(a,b)$ for the number of sites of $F_s$ selecting that pair. The following equations are necessary.

* At fixed $s,q$ the five values $n_{s,q}$ on sides of the pentagon at $q$ are equal, as are the five on diagonals. In the [covered-root flow identity](../../../conway-99-involution/uncovered-squares/covered-root/Theorem.md) evaluate the dirty columns rooted at $q$ on the grid point $s$. That point lies in the clean grid cell, so the columns touching either grid axis vanish there. Each remaining value is $n_{s,q}(a,b)-1$. The bipartite graph of the ten oriented arcs $i\to i+1,i+2$ is a single ten-cycle, with edges alternating between sides and diagonals. Vanishing of the flow sums proves the two equalities, also after reversing the tournament.
* For each pair at $q$ its counts summed over the four opposite $s$ are four. The four signed choices have exactly one exterior point each in the four-cell.
* For each pair of opposite positions and chosen axis in each, the number of incidences from the first to the second equals the reverse count. These are edges between the same two pairs of sites.
* For adjacent $p,q$ each transported axis contributes two matched edges between their fibers. Each of the two edges has exactly one triangle cap, outside $B,F_p,F_q$. Conversely every overlap of the corresponding axes in the word of such a cap is an actual matched edge. The total number of these overlaps is therefore two for each edge of the grid and each starting axis.

When all four free maps are rotations, adding a constant to all the axis labels preserves the transfers, orientations and equations. Average the hypothetical multiplicities over these five translations; real multiplicities suffice. Use words starting on label zero with total mass two per fiber. Opposite-axis incidence depends only on label difference, there is one cap equation per grid edge with right side two, and the four-cell equations on sides and diagonals are each constant under translations. The total contribution to sides at a fixed root from these representative words is four, by dividing the twenty required occurrences by five. The checker uses this side equation; the diagonal total follows from the four opposite fibers and their masses. This averaging concerns the necessary equations and assumes no graph automorphism.

## Exact replay

Run `python3 verify.py` from this directory with NumPy and a C++17 compiler. The [enumerator](charts.cpp) constructs all the transports and the allowed words. The [integer checker](verify.py) interprets the [chart certificates](charts.json.gz). A certificate assigns integer weights to some of the incidence equations. Each word then has a score. Twice the maximum score in each initial-axis group, summed over groups, is an upper bound on any true configuration; its required weighted sum is the same weights applied to the right-hand sides. A strict excess of the latter gives a contradiction. An empty group is impossible by its required mass two. The local certificates use only the two sets of equalities between pair counts at each opposite position. The global certificates can also use the other equations. The verifier checks every orientation of every representative by integer inequalities. No optimization solver is needed for replay.
