# No seven norm-eighteen markings at a seed when norm 16 and unit (1,27) are absent

Work in a hypothetical SRG(99,14,1,2). Assume just \(N_{16}=U_{1,27}=0\), counting actual graph fields. No other shell absence or Seidel-rank assumption is used.

**Theorem.** At any norm-eighteen seed there are at most six markings. In particular \(N_{18}\le33,110\) graph-wide, with neutral orientations counted separately.

Use the notation and exact prior bounds of [the saturated seed theorem](norm18-saturated-seed-cap.md). A seed consists of a positive root \(o\) and an independent set \(Q\) of four specified negative neighbours. Its six labels are the second common neighbours of the six pairs in \(Q\). Choose four actual positive labels for each marking and write \(L\) for the resulting omission graph on the six labels. The quartets are distinct by [the window theorem](exclusion.md), and \(L\) is triangle-free by the [all-triple cap](norm18-dot8-nonstar.md). A marking is called thin at this seed when it actually has exactly four positive labels. The prior theorem already excludes seven markings on any seed whose label graph has an actual edge; thus consider seven markings and six independent labels.

## Facts about a degree-three omission and its satellites

We call a far vertex with one \(Q\)-parent a satellite. A thin marking's four private positives are all satellites. For each parent \(q\in Q\), their number there equals the number of omitted labels that have parent \(q\). Each label has precisely six satellite neighbours and six zero-parent far neighbours, as counted in [the preceding proof](norm18-saturated-seed-cap.md).

If three thin markings omit a fixed label \(z\), their omissions share \(z\), hence any pair has dot eight and they have pairwise disjoint private sites: they share three labels, [the dot-nine panel theorem](panel-heights.md#the-three-label-exclusion-and-the-rank-38-contradiction) rules out dot nine, and dot ten would give norm 16. Because the labels are independent, the row at the omitted \(z\) needs at least two positive satellites in each of these markings to balance two negative parents. Its six satellite neighbours are therefore exhausted, and the three markings each use exactly two. For each of the two nonparents \(q\) of \(z\), two satellites of \(z\) are at \(q\). They must be used in distinct markings whose other omitted label has parent \(q\), since each such marking has at most one satellite at \(q\). In particular, among the three partner labels of \(z\) in \(L\), the label \(\bar z\) with complementary parent pair must be present: without it the three partner pairs supply at most three incidences on the two nonparents, rather than the required four. The other two partners meet those two nonparents once each.

If the two always-selected labels \(b,c\) of these three thin markings share a parent \(p\in z\), there is a contradiction. In fact put \(Y=Q\setminus\{p\}\), \(d_i=(3I-A)e_i\), and
\[
 b'=t_1+t_2+t_3-d_b-d_c+\sum_{q\in Y}d_q.
\]
This is the [same charge-one, cost-27 computation](norm18-saturated-seed-cap.md) as for the disjoint-parent label edge, because \(b,c\) are independent, each has one parent in \(Y\), and the triple has dots eight. Here the omitted label \(z\) has no label neighbours but has exactly one parent in \(Y\), so its new value is \(-1\). On \(b,c\) the new value is \(-1\), at the remaining other labels it is \(2-|N(i)\cap Y|\) in \(\{0,1\}\), and at \(o,p,Y\) the values are respectively \(0,-1,\) and numbers in \(\{0,1\}\), as before. Private positive and negative entries remain unit, since they respectively miss \(b,c\) and miss \(Q\). An unmarked site could fail only by seeing both \(b,c\) and missing \(Y\); seeing \(p\) as well would violate the matching in \(N(p)\), so it must be a zero-parent far neighbour of \(c\). The six such neighbours of \(c\) are exhausted by the six distinct private negatives at \(c\) in the three markings, contradiction. Thus the translate is truly a forbidden unit \(U_{1,27}\).

Consequently, when the three omitting fields are thin, their two always-selected labels must have disjoint parent pairs. Indeed, aside from \(\bar z\), the other two omitted partners have one endpoint each among the two nonparents and are cross pairs between the parents and nonparents of \(z\). If they are not complementary, they share an original parent of \(z\), and the two remaining cross labels share the *other* original parent \(p\in z\). This is precisely the forbidden transfer case. We have proved the necessary neighbourhood pattern
\[
 N_L(z)=\{\bar z,x,\bar x\}
\]
for a degree-three \(z\) when its three omitting markings are thin.

## The thin and five-label possibilities

A triangle-free seven-edge graph on six vertices is either bipartite, or a chordless 5-cycle plus one vertex joined to two nonadjacent cycle vertices. In the bipartite case it is \(K_{2,4}\) minus one edge, or \(K_{3,3}\) minus two edges. The initially chosen part sizes follow because a 1|5 bipartition offers at most five edges, and in the nonbipartite case an odd cycle has length five (a triangle is forbidden), has no chord, and the sixth vertex accounts for the other two edges.

A marking with five actual labels must omit a label of degree one in \(L\), as otherwise another quartet is repeated. Six actual labels are impossible by the window theorem. There is at most one degree-one label in any of the listed seven-edge graphs.

* For \(K_{2,4}\) minus one edge, if all fields are thin, the degree-four label would need eight distinct satellites and has only six. Otherwise the five-label marking omits the sole degree-one label \(d\) and selects its only omission partner \(b\). The six other markings are thin, with omission graph \(K_{2,3}\) between two labels \(a,b\) and a common set \(C\) of three partners. The three true omissions of each of \(a,b\) are thin and saturate six satellites. Thus every parent outside \(a\) or \(b\) has degree at least two among the three parent pairs of \(C\). If \(a,b\) are complementary this requires eight incidences on the four parents, whereas \(C\) gives six. If \(a,b\) share \(p\), the condition uses all six incidences on the other three parents, so \(C\) is the triangle there and \(\{a,b,d\}\) is the star at \(p\). The three \(a\)-omitting thin markings have common selected labels \(b,d\), sharing \(p\in a\); the preceding unit transfer applies.
* For \(K_{3,3}\) with two edges deleted at one label \(d\), the other two labels of its part \(U\) have degree three and all three of their omitting markings are thin, even if the unique \(d\)-omission marking uses five actual labels. At a degree-three label in \(U\), every parent of degree at least two in the three-edge parent-pair graph \(U\) must belong to that label: all the other part's partner pairs have total degree at most one at such a parent. With two degree-three labels in \(U\), \(U\) must be a star, because a triangle has three high-degree parents and a three-edge path has only one pair containing both high-degree parents. But for either regular label \(z\in U\), its two always-selected labels are the other two members of that star, sharing its centre \(p\in z\). Again the unit transfer is forbidden.

The remaining graphs are thin, of degree sequence \(3,3,2,2,2,2\).

* In the bipartite case write \(a,b\) for the degree-three labels in their respective \(3\)-parts. Each part's parent-pair graph must be a three-edge path with its regular label the middle edge (a triangle has too many high-degree parents; a star gives a complementary triangle). Hence \(a,b\) are complementary. Write \(u_1,u_2\) for the other labels on the side of \(a\), and \(v_1,v_2\) on that of \(b\); each of these two pairs of labels is complementary. Renumbering the two \(v\)'s, the omissions are \(ab,av_1,av_2,bu_1,bu_2,u_1v_1,u_2v_2\).
* In the nonbipartite case the degree-three labels \(a,b\) are nonadjacent in \(L\) and share precisely two \(L\)-neighbours. By the degree-three rule they cannot be complementary, for then they would be adjacent. Their neighbours are thus respectively \(\{\bar a,c,\bar c\}\) and \(\{\bar b,c,\bar c\}\) for the third complementary pair \(\{c,\bar c\}\): neither can contain the other's full complementary pair because it would contain the other regular label. The seventh edge must be \(\bar a\bar b\), since these are the only vertices of degree one in the six-edge diagram, and both need degree two.

## The forced shared-negative witnesses

Restrict here to the thin diagrams established above. When two omission pairs meet, the markings agree on the root, the four parents and three labels, have dot eight, and share no private site. At any fixed private site the omissions of all markings using it therefore form a matching on six labels, of size at most three.

For a fixed label \(z\), use only the markings selecting it. A private negative belongs to at most two of those markings: their omissions form a matching in \(L-z\) on five vertices. No assertion about markings omitting \(z\) is needed. Two markings with disjoint omissions cannot share two private negatives, since that would give dot nine with only the root and two labels in common on the positive side. The [dot-nine panel theorem](panel-heights.md) requires four shared positives and five shared negatives.

Now take a degree-two omission label \(z\) for which \(L-z\) has exactly four unordered disjoint pairs of edges. Since \(z\) is selected by five markings, it needs \(5\cdot2=10\) private negative incidences across those markings. There are only six zero-parent far neighbours of \(z\). Each is used at most twice, and a pair of markings can share at most one such private negative site. Therefore at least four distinct zero-parent sites must be used twice, one on *each* of the four disjoint omission pairs in \(L-z\). This is a requirement of the actual graph, not an occupancy relaxation.

In the bipartite graph, each of the four degree-two labels has \(L-z\) a 4-cycle plus a pendant edge on the cycle, which has exactly four pairs of disjoint edges. Select \(z=u_1\) and \(z'=v_1\). They belong to different complementary pairs and so have a common parent in \(Q\). Deleting both leaves the 4-cycle on \(a,b,u_2,v_2\). Its two matching pairs of omission edges therefore both require a shared private negative seeing both \(z,z'\), since each marked pair individually can share at most one private negative. The two witnesses cannot be the same site (their markings are disjoint pairs, and a private negative belongs to at most three markings). They and the common parent give three common neighbours to \(z,z'\), contradiction.

In the nonbipartite graph, choose \(z=\bar a,z'=\bar b\), also different complementary pairs and sharing a parent. Each of \(L-z,L-z'\) is a 4-cycle plus pendant edge with the same four-pair property. Deleting both leaves the 4-cycle \(K_{2,2}\) on \(a,b,c,\bar c\). Its two matching pairs give the same contradiction. This exhausts seven independent-label markings.

## Population count

Combine \(m\le6\) with the earlier two-parameter seed table, including \(m\le5\) when the sole label edge joins disjoint parent pairs and \(m\le4\) for three or more label edges. In the resulting table the pointwise inequality
\[
 8m+j+8k\le49
\]
holds, where \(j\) and \(k\) count label edges with shared and disjoint parents. This follows by substitution into the table of the preceding theorem, replacing its top-left entry by 6. Summing over the 55,440 actual seeds uses \(\sum m=9N_{18}\), \(\sum j=8(99\cdot84-6P)\), and \(\sum k\ge99\cdot336+6P\), with \(P\) the actual prism count. The \(P\) terms cancel. Hence \(72N_{18}\le49\cdot55440-8(99\cdot420)=2383920\), or \(N_{18}\le33110\). This alone does not claim a contradiction for the last palette.
