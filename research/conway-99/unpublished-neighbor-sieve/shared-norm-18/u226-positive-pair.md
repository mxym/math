# The positive pair of an actual charge-two, cost-26 field

This is a graph-side statement with no rank, symmetry, or support hypothesis except for the final conditional multiplicities. Write b for an actual signed-unit (2,26) field, with positive pair p,q and 24 negative sites N. It obeys d_N(x)−d_{p,q}(x)=4b_x+4. The pair p,q is nonadjacent: otherwise b−d_p−d_q is neutral norm 26+36−44=18, with adjacent negative entries −1 at both p and q. Every norm-18 field is unit with each side an independent nine-set (the standard norm-18 structural lemma; see [the shared norm-18 analysis](README.md)). Thus p and q each see eight negatives and six zeros.

Write c∈{0,1,2} for the number of negative common neighbors of p,q, D for those sites, Z for their 2−c zero common neighbors, A and B for the exclusive negative neighbors of p,q, and U,V for the respective exclusive zero neighbors. Then |A|=|B|=8−c, |U|=|V|=4+c. The remaining negative sites X, of size 8+c, are isolated in N and nonadjacent to p,q. Every A or B site has degree one in N, each D site degree two. The two common neighbors are nonadjacent since otherwise their edge would see both p,q. Each D site must therefore be mated to precisely one A and one B: its two negative neighbors are in A∪B, and a neighbor of p has exactly one mate in that neighborhood, likewise for q. Within A and within B the number of edges is the same m: after the c edges to D, leftover single stubs are matched across A–B. There are 8−2c−2m cross edges. The p-neighborhood consists of seven mate pairs. With c+m negative-negative mates, there are c+m−1 zero-zero mates.

Each common zero z has four X-neighbors and is mated to a negative on both hubs. Indeed let a,b∈{0,1} indicate if its p- and q-mates are negatives. Then its other 6−a−b negative neighbors lie in X. Each such x sees a unique *other* p-zero neighbor besides z, in U (it cannot be in Z since the two common sites already share p and q). These other neighbors are distinct: z and a given U site already share p and can have at most one other witness. If z's p-mate were zero, then it would be in U and also unavailable (the edge at z already shares p), giving at most |U|−1≤4 choices when z exists. But 6−a−b≥5 then. Apply the same argument on q.

In all cases m≤1. When c=0 the eight B sites need sixteen common-neighbor witnesses with p. Edges to A provide 8−2m, leaving 8+2m contacts to the six p-zeros. The two common zeros furnish at most one B mate each, the four exclusive zeros at most two B neighbors each (they are nonneighbors of q). Hence m≤1, and c+m≥1 forces m=1. Generally each x∈X sees an unordered pair of p-zeros, as do the 2m vertices mated within B: these B vertices already have their unique N-neighbor, so their two p-contact sites cannot be negative. All these pairs are distinct since p is already a common neighbor, and the c+m−1 p-zero mate edges cannot occur. Thus 8+c+2m+c+m−1≤15; this gives m≤1 for c=2 and m≤2 for c=1. At c=1,m=2 every available pair must have been used. The common zero z has *five* possible partners among the other p-zeros (its own mate is negative); only four such pairs can belong to X, and none to a negative vertex mated inside B, since z's q-mate is already another vertex. This contradiction gives m≤1 also for c=1.

## Fixed positive pair when c=0

The six p-zero sites constitute Z∪U, with sizes 2 and 4, and none is mated to another, since c+m−1=0. Every one of the two common zeros has exactly four X-neighbors. An x∈X cannot see both common sites, so each sees exactly one of them and one of U (and one of V). For each z∈Z and y∈U the other common neighbor of z,y besides p is consequently a unique x∈X: the four X-neighbors of z have distinct U contacts. Thus the pair graph from X on the six p-zeros is exactly K_{2,4}; the identical statement holds for q.

The following bijections depend only on p,q, not on b. At a common neighbor z, the vertices p,q have two distinct mates in its neighborhood, one p-exclusive and one q-exclusive; they cannot mate one another. Each of its remaining ten neighbors x is nonadjacent to both hubs (the unique common neighbor of p,z or q,z is already its mate). Besides z, x sees precisely one other p-neighbor y and one q-neighbor w. Both are exclusive because the common sites already have p,q as their only common neighbors. Each possible y appears at most once, since z,y already share p, and likewise for w. Thus the ten x give a partial bijection π_z between ten of the twelve p-exclusive neighbors and ten q-exclusive neighbors; x is recoverable from either end.

For a field of the c=0 kind, U lies in the domain of both maps and π_z(U)=V for each z. Its X consists precisely of the eight witnesses indexed by U at the two maps; the groups of witnesses are disjoint (the two common sites have no third common neighbor). All of A,B and thus b are determined by U. If U,U′ give two fields, put d=|U∩U′|. Since either π_z is injective, |V∩V′|=d and |X∩X′|=2d. Also |A∩A′|=|B∩B′|=4+d. The negative sets of the two charges have intersection 8+4d, and the neutral difference therefore has squared norm 32−8d. In particular d=3 is impossible. If norm16 is absent, intersections of distinct 4-sets must have size at most one. All lie in the domain of a fixed π_z, with just ten points. A family of six such sets would have 24 incidences and hence at least 18 intersecting-set pairs counted with multiplicity (minimizing the sum of binomials over ten point degrees), but there are only fifteen pairs. So **at most five** c=0 fields occur per actual positive pair under norm16 absence. If norm24 is absent too, the 4-sets are disjoint, giving **at most two**. For these two statements the shell absences can be graph-wide or in a fixed extension when counting only its eligible fields, since differences of two eligible fields are eligible. Even without these absences, in any single signed residual seven-class of a fixed extension there is at most one such field on p,q: none of 8,16,24,32 is divisible by seven.

These multiplicity bounds cover the c=0 subtype only. Without further hypotheses the other core parameters (1,0),(1,1),(2,0),(2,1) are not excluded by the argument above.

## What absence of true unit (1,27) forces

Define the neutral z=d_p+d_q−b of norm 50+26−44=32. Its positive doubled entries are the two hubs. Its negative doubled entries are exactly their common *zero* neighbors; an exterior site with k hub contacts has value −k−b_i. When c=1 let w be the unique common zero. Then u=z+d_w has charge one and norm 32+23−28=27; its values at p,w,q are all +1. It is signed-unit: the two positive doubles have been reduced, the only negative double is w, and no other negative double is created. Indeed a former −1 can occur only at the common negative (nonadjacent to w since both have p,q as common neighbors) or at an exclusive zero neighbor of p or q (also nonadjacent to w, whose two hub mates are already negative). There is no assumption here about other (1,27) fields.

Conversely, if a unit (1,27) field u has a marked induced P3 of three positive sites p,w,q, then b=d_p+d_q+d_w−u has charge two and norm 53+27−54=26. Every such cost-26 field is unit: a positive double shifts to charge one at cost 26+23−2·18=13, below its minimum; a negative double shifts to charge three at cost 26+23+2(−14+4)=29, below 33, and larger amplitudes are still easier. Its positive sites are p,q, and w is a common zero. At the other common site s (nonadjacent to w) b_s=−2−u_s; hence u_s=−1 and b_s=−1. Thus its c is exactly one, and this is a bijection between those U226 fields and marked induced positive P3s of U127, identifying a path with its reversal.

Moreover any zero-zero mate y,y′ under a hub p of b lies in U (the common zeros are already mated to negatives). The triangle T={p,y,y′} is anticomplete to the other positive q. Since D_T=Σ_{i∈T}d_i has norm39 and b·D_T=7+12=19, the field D_T−b has cost 39+26−38=27 and charge one. On T its values are 0,1,1. Every exterior site contacts at most one site of T, and the only other positive of b, namely q, has no T contact. Thus this too is a true signed-unit U127. These translations preserve eligibility in the same extension (or its opposite residual class as appropriate).

If U127 vanishes, the zero-zero mate count c+m−1 must therefore vanish and c=1 is impossible. Consequently **every** U226 is of kind (0,1). If N16 also vanishes, the preceding local bound applies to all fields and gives at most 5·4158=20,790 such positive-charge fields graph-wide (4158 unordered nonedges). Unconditionally in this c=0 subtype there is a second U226 with the opposite positive diagonal: for the two shared zero neighbors w,w′, z has value −2 at both. The field d_w+d_w′+z has charge two and norm 50+32−56=26, hence is unit, and its positives are w,w′. Those have p,q as their two common neighbors with value zero, so c=0 again. The map is an involution (the four marked sites stay fixed). This pairs the fields globally and does not divide the positive-pair multiplicity bound by two. None of these statements eliminates a whole Seidel rank or establishes absence of U226.

**Strengthening.** The [pair midpoint argument](u226-pair-midpoint.md) shows that two distinct eligible c=0 fields on the same positive pair force eligible norm 16. Hence norm-16 absence improves the earlier caps to one per pair and, if U127 is also absent graph-wide, to 4,158 fields in total.
