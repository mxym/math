# Counting good pentagons using bad prisms and good rectangles

Use the definitions and incidence conventions of [the thirteen-fiber argument](partial-k6.md#a-global-consequence-for-pentagons-of-good-prisms), [the two 12-fiber exclusions](partial-k6-12.md), and [the equal-prism packing theorem](triangle-pair-packing.md). Let N5 count **unoriented** Berge pentagons of distinct triangles in distinct parallel-line grid blocks. Let B count distinct bad prisms (not marks), R count unoriented rectangles of good prism mates, and H count unordered equal-mark pairs of distinct bad prisms. Then
\[
  N_5\le 8B+\tfrac32R\le 8B+3H\le11B.
\]
Without the rectangle count, the local packing bound alone gives \(N_5\le14B\).

**Reconstruct an oriented charge.** Each Berge pentagon has at least four distinct bad secondary prisms by the two 12-fiber exclusions. Fix one bad prism with its two parallel triangular faces distinguished as U and T. To charge the orientation U to T, the five cyclic vertices in order must be A,B,C,T,D where (A,B,U) are the parallel lines of a grid g. Once g is specified, distinguish A and B by any fixed total order on graph triangles; an unoriented cycle then has only the one displayed order through this marked edge. The other two vertices C,D must be common good mates of B,T and A,T, respectively, in **distinct** grids through T because the pentagon's blocks are distinct. We may use all grids through T for an upper bound and then impose this required distinctness. The marked secondary U,T determines the position of the edge A,B and T in this cycle. The five secondary prisms have different abstract K6 labels, so they are indeed distinguishable prisms in the count.

Both A and B are anticomplete to T. For instance, the sum of their marks is minus the mark of U and the prism U,T gives inner product -2. Thus the sum of the two inner products with T is 2, attaining the bound 1 for both. At either anticomplete pair (A,T) or (B,T), the [packing theorem](triangle-pair-packing.md) permits at most three common good mates. Each grid through T contains at most one candidate for each pair: the anticomplete target lies outside the grid, and its three sites have only one contact per site with the grid, so they cannot match both other parallel lines.

Fix g. Let a,b be the two candidate list lengths. Let d count the T-grids contributing to both lists. The row supplies at most ab-d cycles, because the two chosen T-grids must differ and every remaining ordered choice determines A,B,C,T,D. We have a,b<=3 and a+b-d<=g_T<=4, the last inequality from T being a face of a bad prism. Hence ab-d<=7, with numerical equality possible only at a=b=3,d=2. As g_U,g_T<=4, summing both orientations gives at most 56 charges per bad prism and 4N5<=56B.

**The budget of double cells.** If grid h through T contributes to both lists for fixed g, its candidates C (for B) and D (for A) are distinct. A common candidate X would have inner products -2 with both A,B and thus inner product 4 with U (whose mark is minus their sum). Equality forces X=U by norm four and unique triangle marks, impossible because U,T is bad and X,T good. Consequently A,B,C,D in that order form a rectangle of good prism mates: the edges are AB in g, BC by choice, CD in h, and DA by choice. Its diagonal bad prism from the parallel completions of AB and CD is U,T. The rectangle determines this marked cell: with U,T specified, the marked opposite edges are fixed because the four completing lines of a rectangle are distinct. The cells are the grids of these opposite edges: the closed prism on AB has third line U, and that on CD has third line T. Exchanging U and T exchanges the two grids and gives the same unordered cell. Thus d_{UT}, the number of double (g,h)-cells for the fixed **unordered** bad prism, is independent of the chosen orientation, and
\[
  \sum_{\{U,T\}\text{ bad}} d_{UT}\le 2 R,
\]
since each good rectangle yields just two bad diagonal prisms, as in [the rectangle argument](bad-prisms.md#rectangles-of-good-prism-mates).

For a row with d=0,1,2,3, the bounds a,b<=3 and a+b<=4+d give ab-d<=4,5,7,6 respectively. These are all at most 4+3d/2. Thus the two orientations of U,T receive at most 4(g_U+g_T)+3d_{UT}<=32+3d_{UT} charges. Summing and dividing by four gives the first inequality. The others follow from R<=2H in the packing theorem and H<=B since each bad-mark class has size at most three. This incidence inequality is unconditional, not an existence decision or rank exclusion.
