# Integer counterexample and rational-affine stability

## 1. The source's comparison

Fix an integer n≥1, a nonempty S⊆{0,…,n}, and a possibly randomized f:{0,…,n}→{0,…,n}. A random seed R is sampled as in the source. Draw X uniformly from S independently of R and set Y=f(X;R). The source's expected relative Hamming distance to a fixed line is exactly its disagreement probability against Z=(X,Y). This remains true even if outputs at different x share a common seed: only their marginals enter the average.

For a coefficient set C, let A_C be the maximum agreement probability of Y=aX+b with a,b in C, using exact equality of numbers. Let A_p be the maximum of Pr[Y≡aX+b (mod p)] over a,b in F_p. Reducing a,b in N modulo p attains every pair of residues, whether N means nonnegative or positive integers. Thus the source's α is 1−A_N and its αhat is 1−E_p A_p for a uniform prime from the specified bit-length set. The best modular line may depend on p but not on R. Maxima and minima exist: there are only finitely many incidence subsets of the finite grid.

An exact integer match is a modular match to the reduced line, so A_p≥A_N for every p. In the published ePrint notation, αhat−α≤0; its maximum is exactly zero for n≥1 (take S={0}, f(0)=1, and a=b=1). The printed inequality is therefore vacuous. The opposite difference, α−αhat=E_p A_p−A_N, is the meaningful modular gain in the sign-corrected reading. Only nonempty S and nonempty prime sets are used.

## 2. Counterexample to the sign-corrected integer assertion

For any n≥3 set S={0,2}, f(0)=2 and f(2)=3, with arbitrary values off S. To match both points over the integers would require b=2 and 2a=1. A positive-coefficient integer line can match (0,2), so the maximum agreement is exactly 1/2 both for nonnegative coefficients and for positive coefficients, and even for signed integer coefficients.

For every odd prime p, use a=2⁻¹ in F_p and b=2 mod p; this matches both points. Nonnegative or positive representative coefficients give the same modular line. For any bit length κ≥3, every κ-bit prime is odd. In particular the corrected worst-case gain is at least 1/2 even for n=3. For any proposed cδ,cn>0, sufficiently large κ satisfy n=3≤2^(cn κ) and 2^(−cδ κ)<1/2. This refutes both the randomized assertion and its deterministic restriction, without needing a promise on the values of f outside S.

The dependence on the convention for N can be made exact, although not needed for the counterexample. For nonnegative coefficients, one of the n+1 constant lines has agreement at least 1/(n+1). This bounds the corrected worst-case gain by 1−1/(n+1) for any nonempty set of primes. Equality is achieved by S={0,…,n} and f(x)=n−x: every nondecreasing integer line meets this graph at most once, and the graph is a modular affine line for every prime. For strictly positive coefficients, the corrected worst-case gain is 1: choose f≡0, which has no exact match at nonnegative x, yet has a perfect modular match from a=b=p. The two-point counterexample above does not rely on either of these convention-specific effects.

## 3. Separate theorem: rational comparator, exact large-prime threshold

Now change the comparison to all rational affine graphs y=ax+b with a,b in Q; write A=A_Q. The following statements hold for any probability distribution on the whole grid {0,…,n}², not just a distribution generated by f as above.

For every prime p>n², A_p=A. Given three grid points z_i=(x_i,y_i), put

    D(z_1,z_2,z_3)=(x_2−x_1)(y_3−y_1)−(x_3−x_1)(y_2−y_1).

Its absolute value is at most n². Indeed the signed determinant is affine separately in each of the six coordinates; either sign reaches its maximum at six box endpoints, where it is the doubled signed area of a triangle of square corners, thus at most n². Points on a fixed modular affine graph have determinant zero modulo p, so for p>n² each such determinant vanishes as an integer. In addition p>n, hence no two distinct points of a modular affine graph have the same integer x: equal x forces equal y modulo p and therefore exactly equal y. If a modular graph has at least two positive-mass points, their distinct integer x-coordinates define a unique rational affine graph, and every other point of the modular graph lies on it. If it has at most one positive-mass point, use any horizontal graph through that point when present. This shows A_p≤A.

Conversely, take an optimal rational graph. If it contains two positive-mass grid points, it can be written using slope and intercept with common denominator x_2−x_1, a nonzero integer of absolute value at most n. For p>n this denominator is invertible, and every grid point on the rational graph is on the reduced modular graph. With a sole positive-mass point use an integer horizontal graph. This shows A≤A_p for all p>n, independently of the other direction.

The large-prime threshold is sharp in the following pointwise sense: for every prime n<p≤n² with n≥2, there is a deterministic graph in the grid with rational agreement 2/3 and modular agreement 1. Set v=ceil(p/n) and u=nv−p. Since p>n and n≥2, n does not divide p, so 1≤u≤n−1 and 1≤v≤n. The three points (0,0),(n,1),(u,v) have distinct x and determinant nv−u=p. They are not rationally collinear but are all on the modular graph of slope n⁻¹ and intercept zero. Use the uniform law on those three points. This is a worst-graph claim for a given prime, not a simultaneous lower bound for the average over many primes.

## 4. Separate theorem: arbitrary finite prime set, weighted average

Let P be any nonempty finite set of primes, p_min its minimum, and define

    L_1=floor(log n / log p_min),   L_2=floor(log(n²) / log p_min).

For Z on the grid as above, with no additional assumption on its law, the following bounds hold under a uniform draw of p from P:

    E_p (A_p−A)_+ ≤ ((L_1+L_2)/|P|)^(1/3),
    E_p (A−A_p)_+ ≤ L_1/|P|.                              (1)

Proof of the first line: use three independent draws Z_1,Z_2,Z_3 from the point law (including independent seeds if the law comes from f). Fix p, an optimal modular graph L, and d=(A_p−A)_+. Each individual point has mass at most A. If d>0, the probability that the first two draws are distinct and both lie on L is at least A_p d: given the first on L, removing it leaves mass at least A_p−A. On such a pair there are two cases.

* If x_1=x_2, then the nonzero y difference is divisible by p. Write E_2(p) for this event with no restriction to L.
* If x_1≠x_2, the rational graph determined by the first two draws has mass at most A. The third draw lands on L but off this rational graph with probability at least d. In that event the determinant D is nonzero and divisible by p. Write E_3(p) for the event of a nonzero determinant divisible by p, without restriction to L and with x_1≠x_2.

The two types of pairs partition the distinct-pair event. Each pair of the first type contributes with weight one to E_2, and each of the second type contributes with conditional weight at least d to E_3. Hence Pr[E_2(p)]+Pr[E_3(p)]≥d (A_p d)≥d³, using d≤1 and A_p≥d. The same inequality holds trivially for d=0. The pair case is essential when p≤n, since distinct integer points can share an x-coordinate and have different but congruent y-coordinates.

For any fixed pair or triple, the corresponding nonzero number (if any) has absolute value at most n or n² respectively. If it is divisible by k distinct primes from P, their product is at least p_min^k and divides the number. Thus the average over P of the two event probabilities is at most (L_1+L_2)/|P|. Jensen's inequality gives the first line of (1), with the modular optimum fully inside the prime average.

For the other direction fix an optimal rational graph independently of p. Its common denominator can be taken to be a nonzero difference of two x-coordinates, of absolute value at most n, or one for a horizontal graph when it has at most one positive-mass point. Its incidences survive for every prime not dividing that denominator. At most L_1 primes in P divide it and the error is at most one on each. This proves the second line.

In particular, for P the κ-bit primes, the prime number theorem gives |P|=Θ(2^κ/κ) as κ grows. If n≤2^(cκ) with c fixed, the average positive gain is O_c(κ^(1/3) 2^(−κ/3)) and the average reverse loss is O_c(κ 2^(−κ)). These are statements for the rational comparator only. Neither an exponent 1/2 nor any implication for a security theorem is claimed.
