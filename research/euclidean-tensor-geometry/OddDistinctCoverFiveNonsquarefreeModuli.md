# Odd distinct covers with at most five nonsquarefree moduli

**Yongxian Zhang.** AI-assisted proof development and internal verification; no claim of external peer review or priority.

**Theorem.** A finite covering system of distinct odd moduli greater than one, if it exists and has at most five nonsquarefree moduli, contains modulus \(3\) or modulus \(5\) itself. With at most four nonsquarefree moduli it must contain modulus \(3\). With at most two nonsquarefree moduli it cannot exist.

Here containing a modulus is stronger than the least common multiple being divisible by it. The general [Erdős–Selfridge problem](https://www.erdosproblems.com/7) remains open. The [recent restriction on the minimum modulus and on the lcm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15110) concerns general candidates; the conclusion here instead uses the small number of nonsquarefree moduli. We use the [Balister–Bollobás–Morris–Sahasrabudhe–Tiba squarefree noncovering theorem](https://doi.org/10.2140/ant.2021.15.609).

## Fibres over the squarefree radical

Let \(L\) be the lcm of the moduli, and let \(C_0\) be the subfamily with squarefree moduli. There is at least one residue \(x\) modulo \({\rm rad}(L)\) not covered by \(C_0\), by the squarefree theorem. Call such a residue a survivor. Its entire fibre in \(\mathbb Z_L\) must be covered by nonsquarefree classes. For a class \(a\bmod m\) to meet this fibre, we need \(x\equiv a\bmod{\rm rad}(m)\); call such a class active at \(x\). By CRT it then covers the fraction \({\rm rad}(m)/m\) of the fibre, at most \(1/3\). This excludes at most two nonsquarefree classes. If there are exactly three, all must be active at every survivor, each with fraction \(1/3\); thus all have \(m=9d\) with \(d\) squarefree and coprime to \(3\). Their three mod-9 children exhaust one mod-3 root.

In a fibre one can also independently choose the next digit of any prime \(p\) for which \(p^2\mid L\). Each active class whose modulus is divisible by \(p^2\) forbids at most one of the \(p\) possible values of that digit. A choice avoiding all these forbidden values eliminates those classes from the fixed slice. The choices for different primes can be made independently.

## Five or fewer classes

First, at each survivor, choose next digits at all primes \(p\ge7\) to avoid every active class whose modulus has such a repeated prime. This is possible since each prime has more than five choices. When no square of a given prime divides \(L\) there is no choice to make. The only remaining classes may have repeated prime factors \(3\) or \(5\).

Suppose for some survivor that the remaining active classes with repeated \(5\) have projections covering the entire \(5\)-lift fibre. Each forbids at most one fifth of that fibre. With at most five classes, all five must participate, each must have \(5\)-exponent exactly two, and their residues must be in different mod-25 children of the same mod-5 root. On the unique child assigned to any one class, no other class can cover a point. Hence that class cannot restrict any higher 3-adic digit or other unfixed lift coordinate: it would leave a point in the survivor fibre uncovered. Consequently all five moduli have the form \(25d\) with \(d\) squarefree and coprime to \(5\). Globally all five use the same mod-5 root. Every survivor must activate all five classes, since at any survivor each child again has only its own possible class. In particular the survivor set lies in one mod-5 root.

Otherwise, for each survivor choose a \(5\)-lift outside the union of the remaining active repeated-5 projections. After fixing that lift as well as the earlier digits, only classes with repeated prime \(3\) can cover the surviving slice. There are three mod-9 children of its mod-3 root, each requiring at least one class. Two different roots would require at least six different classes globally. Therefore all survivors lie in one mod-3 root. Moreover at least one of the three children of this root has only one possible repeated-3 class globally that has no other repeated prime; otherwise at least six such classes would be needed. This class is active at every survivor, and its \(3\)-exponent must be exactly two, since a deeper congruence cannot cover the entire child. When at most four nonsquarefree moduli are present, two of the children have only one possible class each. The repeated-5 saturation case cannot occur with only four classes.

Finally, if all survivors lie in a single mod-\(p\) root for \(p=3\) or \(p=5\) and there is no literal modulus \(p\), append this root as a single class to \(C_0\). This is an odd squarefree covering with distinct moduli, contradicting the squarefree theorem. This proves the asserted restrictions.

The same argument supplies a finer structural statement: for any collection of classes that are mandatory (active at every survivor), every squarefree divisor \(d>1\) of the lcm of their radicals must occur as a modulus in \(C_0\), with its residue different from the forced common residue modulo \(d\). The mandatory classes' radical residues are compatible because at least one survivor exists. Appending the forced residue modulo \(d\) would otherwise give the forbidden squarefree covering; an existing class with that same residue would already cover every survivor. In the saturated \(5\) case the five distinct squarefree cofactors require at least three primes besides \(5\), giving at least 15 such squarefree moduli. This is a necessary structure, not a construction or exclusion of that remaining case.
