# Degree-five localization on the concise fourth Segre secant

Over C, let I be the prime ideal of the affine cone \(X=\widehat{\sigma_4}(\mathbb P^3\times\mathbb P^3\times\mathbb P^3)\), and J the ideal generated by **all polarized degree-five Strassen commutation equations, in all three modes**. On the open locus where all mode flattenings have rank four, \(J=I\) as ideal sheaves. More generally, on any inverse-slice chart, equality holds at every point whose normalized commuting slices generate a four-dimensional unital algebra, including some nonconcise points. This does not assert global ideal generation.

## 1. Inverse slice and the commuting scheme

Write the slices in one mode as \(M_0,\ldots,M_3\). On \(\det M_0\ne 0\) put \(X_i=M_iM_0^{-1}\). This is a coordinate isomorphism with \(\mathrm{GL}_4\times\mathrm{Mat}_4^3\). The pairs \(M_i(\operatorname{adj} M_0)M_j-M_j(\operatorname{adj} M_0)M_i\) for \(i,j=1,2,3\) generate there the ideal \(K\) of pairwise commutators of the \(X_i\), since these expressions are \(\det(M_0)[X_i,X_j]M_0\). Thus \(K\subseteq J\subseteq I\) on the chart.

Set-theoretically, the commuting triples are irreducible of dimension 24 by Guralnick–Sethuraman, Theorems 6 and 8. The locus where \(X_1\) has distinct eigenvalues is dense and has common diagonalization. Such slices give an actual four-term tensor decomposition after changing bases; consequently the reduced commuting scheme is precisely \(X\) on the chart. The generated algebra of any commuting triple has dimension at most four: on the dense diagonalizable locus every wedge of five words is zero, so this holds identically by polynomiality. In particular when the identity \(I_4\) and \(X_1,X_2,X_3\) are linearly independent, the generated algebra has dimension four. (Here \(I_4\) is the identity matrix, not the tensor ideal.)

We prove the commuting scheme itself is reduced locally when the generated algebra has dimension four. Finally, every concise tensor in \(X\) has an invertible slice in some mode: the degree-five set criterion in Friedland–Gross §4, following Landsberg–Manivel §5, says an all-modes singular-slice case is contained, up to permuting modes, in a \(3\times3\times4\) compression. This is also the \(d=3\) statement of Jagiełła–Jelisiejew, proof of Thm 3.29. Since the common zero set on the concise open is \(X\) by the same criterion, the local equality at its closed points yields the ideal-sheaf equality.

## 2. Cyclic and transpose-cyclic cases

Suppose a fixed vector \(v\) is cyclic for the three matrices. Allowing a variable marked cyclic vector, the open framed commuting scheme is the frame bundle of the tautological rank-four bundle on \(\operatorname{Hilb}^4(\mathbb A^3)\). This identification is scheme-theoretic: over any base ring a commuting triple and cyclic \(v\) give a surjective map \(R[x_1,x_2,x_3]\to R^4,\ f\mapsto f(X)v\), with ideal kernel, and conversely multiplication on a finite free length-four quotient and a frame give the triple and \(v=1\). The Hilbert scheme here is reduced, including its nonplanar \(\mathbb A^3\times C\operatorname{Gr}(2,6)\) chart (Hauenstein–Manivel–Szendrői, §2 and Thm 2.1). The projection forgetting \(v\) is an open of a vector bundle and smooth surjective onto the cyclic locus, so reducedness descends. Transposing the three matrices gives the same conclusion at a transpose-cyclic point.

## 3. The only remaining faithful four-dimensional modules

Let \(A=\mathbb C[X_1,X_2,X_3]\) have dimension four; its action on \(V=\mathbb C^4\) is faithful. A product decomposition into local algebras decomposes \(V\) into faithful summands. For local dimensions \(1,2,3\), the corresponding faithful module has dimension at least the algebra dimension: a nontrivial commutative subalgebra of \(\mathrm{Mat}_2\) has dimension at most two, by the centralizer of a nonscalar matrix. Hence in a nonlocal product the dimensions agree summandwise. In dimensions at most three, the monogenic local cases are regular modules (the faithful nilpotent generator has a full Jordan chain), and the only additional case is the dimension-three square-zero algebra. For that case let \(U\) be the sum of radical images and \(L\) the common kernel. Then \(U\subseteq L\) and \(2\le \dim U(3-\dim L)\), forcing \(\dim U=\dim L=1\) or 2. The module is transpose-cyclic in the former case and cyclic in the latter, by evaluating radical maps on a dual covector nonzero on \(U\), or on a vector outside \(L\), respectively. There is at most one dimension-three summand, so the entire module is cyclic or transpose-cyclic.

In the local dimension-four case let \(\mathfrak m\) be the radical. Embedding dimension one again gives a regular module. For embedding dimension two, \(\mathfrak m^2\) is one-dimensional and \(\mathfrak m^3=0\). The induced symmetric pairing on \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) has rank either two or one. Rank two makes the socle one-dimensional. Any nonzero ideal then contains the socle, so a vector not killed by a generator of the socle has trivial annihilator and is cyclic. For rank one choose generators \(x,y\) with \(x^2\ne0,\ xy=y^2=0\). The operator \(x\) is Jordan \(3+1\), with chain \(e_0\to e_1\to e_2\), and complementary vector \(f\) killed by \(x\). We have \(y e_0=a e_2+b f,\ y f=c e_2+d f,\ y e_1=y e_2=0\); nilpotence forces \(d=0\), and \(bc=0\). Faithfulness forces \(b\ne0\) or \(c\ne0\), else \(y=a x^2\). The first case is cyclic with \(e_0\), the second transpose-cyclic with \(e_2^*\).

If the embedding dimension is three, then \(\mathfrak m^2=0\). Now \(3\le\dim U(4-\dim L)\) forces \(U=L\) of dimension one, two, or three. As before dimension one is transpose-cyclic and three is cyclic. In the remaining case the radical is a 3-plane in \(\operatorname{Hom}(V/U,U)\cong\mathrm{Mat}_2\). It contains an invertible map: if all were singular, fix one rank-one element \(uf\); every other rank-one \(vg\) must have \(v\parallel u\) or \(g\parallel f\) because \(uf+vg\) is singular. Two 2-planes cannot cover a 3-plane. We can arrange \(X,Y,Z\) by scalar shifts and invertible linear combinations so that their lower-left blocks are \(I,T,V'\) and all other blocks vanish, with \(I,T,V'\) independent. Such changes preserve the commuting scheme.

## 4. Eight independent linear constraints in the 2+2 chart

Fix vectors \(w_1,w_2\) spanning a complement to \(U\). Near the base point, use \(w_1,w_2,Xw_1,Xw_2\) as the frame. It gives eight free coordinates \(Xw_1,Xw_2\) on an open, and \(X=\begin{pmatrix}0&P\\I&Q\end{pmatrix}\). Eliminating the first block columns of \([X,Y]=[X,Z]=0\) yields
\(Y=\begin{pmatrix}S&PT\\T&S+QT\end{pmatrix}\), \(Z=\begin{pmatrix}D&PV'\\V'&D+QV'\end{pmatrix}\).
The remaining coordinate ambient dimension is 32; at the base \(P=Q=S=D=0\).

The bottom blocks of the second block columns of \([X,Y],[X,Z]\) have differentials \([\dot P,T],[\dot P,V']\). The top and bottom blocks of the first block column of \([Y,Z]\) have differentials \(\dot P[T,V']\) and \([T,\dot D]+[\dot S,V']+\dot Q[T,V']\), respectively. Nonscalar \(2\times2\) matrices have two-dimensional centralizers \(\langle I,T\rangle\) and \(\langle I,V'\rangle\), so their intersection is scalars, and \([T,V']\ne0\). The first three constraints force \(\dot P=0\). The last constraint is surjective onto \(\mathrm{Mat}_2\): a trace-pairing annihilator must centralize both \(T,V'\) and hence be scalar; \(\dot Q[T,V']\) then forces it to be zero. Thus the tangent dimension is at most 24, exactly the local dimension of the reduced support. The local ring is regular, hence reduced. This argument does not assume the \(2\times2\) commutator invertible.
