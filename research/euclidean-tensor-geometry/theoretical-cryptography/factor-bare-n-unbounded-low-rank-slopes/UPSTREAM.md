# Prior results, known ingredients and scope

* The divisibility relation behind exact edges q=mp+1, its chains, and their distribution are classical. See Ford, Konyagin and Luca, [*Prime chains and Pratt trees*](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.0473); for constant affine recurrences and Cunningham chains see [Stones](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2166). We do not claim prime chains, the frequency of prescribed long nonunit chains, or the search over neighbors of a known prime as new.
* Agrawal, Saxena and Shubham Sahai Srivastava, [*Integer Factoring Using Small Algebraic Dependencies*](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.MFCS.2016.6), introduction before Theorem 1.2, discuss univariate reconstruction from two factors and a supplied exact relation; §4.2 considers a small norm in the base of a prime, including no-oracle cases. For only two factors and polynomially many small offsets, enumerating the relation can already be a direct route. Similarly [Hales and Hiary](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11139-024-00959-7) use weighted geometric means in their extension of Lehman's two-factor/power-divisor method. This note does not introduce small-relation elimination or geometric means into integer factoring.
* [Źrałek](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-09-02262-5) derandomizes recovery for suitable smooth shifted primes and studies totient-oracle reductions. Our rank assumption is on the publicly prescribed slopes, not the prime factorizations of p−1 at all hidden p; no totient, group exponent or smoothness test on those p−1 is supplied. Some examples can naturally be handled by many simpler methods.
* Coprime bases and product-of-powers arithmetic are established topics. [Bernstein, *Factoring into coprimes in essentially linear time*](https://cr.yp.to/lineartime/dcba-20040404.pdf) addresses natural coprime bases; [Etessami, Stewart and Yannakakis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.5429) discuss comparing succinct power products. We neither compute a prime basis nor assert a new general algorithm for detecting multiplicative dependencies. The short-word ball bound is elementary integer-lattice counting via valuations. Here the words have polynomial length and fully expanded numerators/denominators have polynomial bit lengths.
* Earlier arithmetic graph results in this repository use bounded support or different edge scales: [fixed-support exact-affine package](https://github.com/mxym/math/tree/f2d900c214788ed575fe388c121850ba27d611bd/research/euclidean-tensor-geometry/theoretical-cryptography/factor-bare-n-affine-one-or-two-blocks); [fixed distinct-support centered-window package, commit](https://github.com/mxym/math/commit/2376fba2686cd79fa3de230638f3477d660456a5); and [fixed-support separated-polynomial package, commit](https://github.com/mxym/math/commit/b088b4a85c4f9b53d2a35b95aa5bd72aa01c7eb9). The fixed-r centered result allows errors growing as p^(1/r) times a polylogarithm, which may be much larger than the present window. We are not subsuming it. Instead, bounding the multiplicative rank allows arbitrary support size and multiplicity patterns without enumerating graph templates. On the opposite end, when all slopes are 1 the global prime span is at most L E; enumerating near an appropriate root of N is elementary and is not presented as a new case.
* [Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena](https://doi.org/10.4007/annals.2004.160.781) provide the standard deterministic prime test used in the complexity proof. The Euler-product count for smooth integers is the usual positive Dirichlet-series bound; the elementary prime-cluster illustration needs only the classical prime number theorem and averaging.

A targeted comparison searched for structural factoring with approximate ratios among many prime factors, multiplicatively dependent slope lists, short prime chains, smooth shifted factors, two-factor dependency results, and exact power-product arithmetic. No explicit matching arbitrary-support recognition theorem was identified in the sources above. This is not a global priority assertion. The proof does not learn an unspecified label list, factor its numerators, or address unrestricted classical factorization.
