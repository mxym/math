# Correlation constraints and exact invariant Fano subplanes at order twelve

A **collineation** preserves the point and line types of a projective plane. A **correlation** exchanges those types and preserves incidence; a **polarity** is an involutory correlation. We retain that distinction throughout: the known bound on the collineation group is not a bound on the possible permutations that exchange point and line types.

If a projective plane of order \(12\) were self-dual, then the following would hold.

* It has a polarity, and there is no correlation of order \(4\). No correlation can stabilize a subplane of order \(3\). In particular a plane with an order-three collineation fixing a subplane pointwise is **not self-dual**.
* For each polarity \(\sigma\) there are **exactly \(286\)** Fano subplanes setwise preserved by \(\sigma\). More generally any even-order plane with an orthogonal polarity has \(q(q^2-1)/6\) subplanes of this kind, with the exact point and point-pair profiles below.
* In the possible self-dual \(V_4\) type, the nontrivial collineation fixes setwise exactly **six** of the Fano subplanes invariant under either polarity. In the possible self-dual \(C_6\) type, the collineation of order three fixes exactly **seven points** and fixes setwise exactly **four** of the Fano subplanes invariant under its commuting polarity. These are necessary conditions, not existence statements. The \(S_3\) type has no commuting nonidentity collineation and polarity, so the \(C_6\) test does not apply to it.

[Akiyama–Suetake–Tanaka](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcd.21869) show that the collineation group is trivial or cyclic of order two or three. [Tomar](https://hexagonmath.org/2611.00389v1) establishes constraints on order-three subplanes and maximal arcs without hypotheses about polarities. Neither result is replaced here. The circle of classical ingredients includes [Baer's polarity theorem](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9904-1946-08506-7), the small-order cases of the [Temmermans–Thas–Van Maldeghem correlation count](https://cage.ugent.be/~hvm/artikels/185.pdf) (use its corrected compatibility formulation in the [subsequent account](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6190)), and [Tait's construction and cubic lower bound for Fano subplanes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06587). We derive the exact invariant census and the stated consequences for the type-swapping extensions; no claim is made to solve the order-twelve plane existence question.

## 1. Correlations of order four and invariant odd subplanes

Suppose a correlation \(\eta\) has order \(4\) in a plane of even nonsquare order \(q\), and let \(t=\eta^2\). The classical involution classification says that \(t\) is an elation with center \(C\) on its axis \(\ell\); exactly the points of \(\ell\) and the lines through \(C\) are fixed, respectively. In the fixed incidence structure \(C\) and \(\ell\) are the unique point and line of degree \(q+1\). As \(\eta\) commutes with \(t\), it interchanges \(C\) and \(\ell\). The only fixed point of \(t\) which can be absolute for \(\eta\) (i.e. incident with its image) is therefore \(C\): for \(P\ne C\) on \(\ell\) its image is a fixed line through \(C\) other than \(\ell\). If any absolute point \(P\) were moved by \(t\), the four-point/line orbit \(P,\eta P,\eta^2P,\eta^3P\) would give a \(4\)-cycle in the incidence graph, by applying \(\eta\) successively to the first incidence. Projective planes have no such cycle. Thus \(\eta\) has exactly one absolute point.

On the other hand, index the lines by images under \(\eta\) and set \(M_{P,Q}=1\) if \(P\in\eta Q\). Let \(P_t\) be the permutation matrix of \(t\). Then \(M^T=P_tM\), \(M\) commutes with \(P_t\), and \(MM^T=qI+J\), where \(J\) is the all-ones matrix. To see the transpose identity apply \(\eta\) to \(Q\in\eta P\), obtaining \(tP\in\eta Q\), and use \(t=t^{-1}\). On the \(+1\) space of \(P_t\) the matrix \(M\) is symmetric. It has eigenvalue \(q+1\) on the ones vector and eigenvalues \(\pm\sqrt q\) on the complement since \(M^2=qI+J\) there. On the \(-1\) space it is skew-symmetric and contributes no trace. Thus the integral trace of \(M\) is \(q+1+u\sqrt q\) for an integer \(u\), so it must equal \(q+1\). It also counts the absolute points, a contradiction. This is the order-four count directly, without needing a general correlation order bound.

For order \(12\), the square of any correlation is a collineation of order \(1,2\), or \(3\) by the known classification. Hence a correlation has order \(2\) or \(6\) after excluding order four. In either case an odd power of it is a polarity, so every self-dual plane here admits a polarity. If any correlation stabilized a subplane of order \(3\), that odd power would restrict to a polarity of the subplane. Its four absolute points form an oval by Baer, but all ambient absolute points of the polarity lie on one line, contradiction. Thus a polarity pairs the ambient order-three subplanes without fixed subplanes; their number is even.

For a planar collineation \(t\) of order three, its fixed points and fixed lines form a proper subplane. Bruck's bound allows order \(2\) or \(3\) at ambient order \(12\), and on each fixed line the fixed point count is \(13\equiv1\pmod3\); hence its order is \(3\). A correlation must normalize the whole order-three collineation group, the type-preserving kernel of the full incidence automorphism group. It then preserves the fixed subplane as a combined point and line structure, which has just been excluded.

## 2. Every nonabsolute triangle and only such a triangle

Let \(\sigma\) be an orthogonal polarity on a plane of even order \(q\). By Baer all \(q+1\) absolute points form a line \(L\). Put \(p=\sigma(L)\), the pole of this line, which is not absolute and is not on \(L\). Join two points in the polarity graph if either is incident with the other's polar line, using a loop precisely at an absolute point. Distinct vertices have exactly one common neighbor, since their polar lines meet in one point. Two distinct absolute points are nonadjacent, since otherwise their two loops would provide two common neighbors. A triangle on three distinct vertices cannot contain an absolute point \(a\): \(a\) and either adjacent vertex of the triangle would then share both \(a\) (by its loop) and the third vertex as common neighbors. The pole has exactly \(L\) as its neighbor set, so it also lies in no triangle.

For each \(a\in L\), let \(N_a=\sigma(a)\setminus\{p,a\}\), of size \(q-1\). These fibers partition all nonabsolute points other than \(p\) because the lines \(\sigma(a)\) run over the pencil at \(p\). A point in \(N_a\) has \(a\) as its sole absolute neighbor. No two members of \(N_a\) are adjacent: \(a\) would share itself and another neighbor with one of them. Every nonabsolute point other than \(p\) has exactly \(q\) nonabsolute neighbors. Every edge between nonabsolute vertices lies in a unique triangle, since its common neighbor differs from both endpoints (there are no loops there), and an absolute third vertex was just excluded. Thus on the \(q^2-1\) nonabsolute vertices other than \(p\) the edge count is \(q(q^2-1)/2\), and the triangle count is \(q(q^2-1)/6\).

A triangle \(xyz\) uses three different fibers at \(a,b,c\in L\). The seven points \(\{p,a,b,c,x,y,z\}\) and their seven polar lines form a Fano subplane. Indeed the restricted adjacency including loops has exactly three neighbors per vertex: the pole has \(a,b,c\); each absolute point has its own loop, the pole, and the corresponding triangle vertex; each triangle vertex has its associated absolute point and the other two triangle vertices. Each pair of distinct vertices has at most one common neighbor by ambient incidence. Counting the \(7\binom32=21\) pairs inside these seven 3-point lines forces every point pair to lie in exactly one such line, and the symmetric incidence gives the dual property. This is the seven-point projective plane, and its point and line sets are exchanged by \(\sigma\).

Conversely, in any Fano subplane preserved by \(\sigma\), the restricted polarity has exactly three absolute points. They lie on \(L\), so the subplane contains \(L\) as a line and its pole \(p\) as a point. Each of the three other points has at most one absolute neighbor, is nonadjacent to \(p\), and has three neighbors within the subplane. Thus those other points must be a triangle, and they recover the whole subplane by the construction. This proves the exact count without assuming that distinct subplanes have distinct triples of absolute points.

The incidence profile is as follows:

| Specified point(s) | Number of invariant Fano subplanes containing them |
| --- | ---: |
| The pole \(p\) | \(q(q^2-1)/6\) |
| One absolute point \(a\) | \(q(q-1)/2\) |
| One other point \(x\ne p\) | \(q/2\) |
| Two distinct absolute points \(a,b\) | \(q-1\) |
| An absolute point \(a\) and \(x\in N_a\) | \(q/2\) |
| An absolute point \(b\ne a\) and \(x\in N_a\) | \(1\) |

For the individual profiles, a vertex of the nonabsolute graph lies in \(q/2\) triangles because its \(q\) incident edges are partitioned in pairs by triangles. Sum over the \(q-1\) points of a fiber for the absolute count; a triangle uses a fiber at most once. For distinct \(a,b\), the edges between \(N_a\) and \(N_b\) form a perfect matching: \(\sigma(x)\) for \(x\in N_a\) meets \(\sigma(b)\) in a unique point, not \(p\) (else \(x\in L\)) nor \(b\) (else \(x\) has a second absolute neighbor). Thus the unique triangle through that edge supplies the pair profiles. In order \(12\) the triples of absolute points carried by the invariant Fano subplanes are a \(2\)-\((13,3,11)\) multidesign on the absolute line, not asserted to be simple.

## 3. The commuting small-group cases at order twelve

If the full collineation group has order two and there is a correlation, its full incidence automorphism group has order four. Since the \(C_4\) extension was excluded, it is \(V_4\). Let \(t\) be the nontrivial collineation and \(\sigma\) one polarity; \(t\sigma\) is the other. As before \(t\) is an elation with axis \(\ell\) and center \(C\in\ell\), and \(\sigma(C)=\ell\). The only point fixed by \(t\) that is absolute for either polarity is \(C\), by the incidence of the fixed flag. The two absolute lines are invariant under \(t\), and are distinct: otherwise at a point \(Q\ne C\) on their common line, \(\sigma(Q)\) would contain both \(Q\) and \(tQ\), forcing it to be that line; but its pole is off the absolute line. Denote the absolute line of \(t\sigma\) by \(L'\).

In the construction of §2, any setwise \(t\)-fixed triangle must have one fixed vertex and one swapped pair, since three fixed points on \(\ell\) cannot form a polarity graph triangle. Indeed any such triangle is noncollinear: the polar line of each of its vertices contains the other two, so if they were collinear their three polar lines would coincide. A swapped pair \(Q,tQ\) is adjacent exactly when \(Q\) is absolute for \(t\sigma\), so its points lie in \(L'\setminus\{C\}\). Conversely all six two-point orbits in \(L'\setminus\{C\}\) are such edges. The unique triangle through each is \(t\)-invariant (its third vertex is the unique common neighbor). This proves the six invariant subplanes.

For order-three collineation kernel, the type-swapping extension, if any, is \(C_6\) or \(S_3\). Suppose \(t\) has order three and commutes with a polarity \(\sigma\), the \(C_6\) case. Its fixed locus cannot contain a quadrangle because that locus would be a subplane, excluded in §1. A point set containing no quadrangle is collinear or all but one lie on a line: take a triangle if it is not collinear, observe any other point must be on one of its sides, and observe that additional points on two different sides together with two of the original vertices form a quadrangle. If there is an exceptional point off the line, the line is fixed by \(t\), and both its fixed-point count and the total fixed-point count are \(1\bmod3\), contradictory when their difference is one. Hence all \(f\) fixed points are collinear.

The absolute line of \(\sigma\) is \(t\)-invariant and has 13 points, so contains a fixed point. Its pole off that line is also fixed. Thus \(f\ge4\) since \(f\equiv1\pmod3\), and the one fixed absolute point is unique by collinearity of the fixed locus and the pole being off the absolute line. Consequently \(f\) is one of \(4,7,10,13\). If \(f=13\), \(t\) fixes the entire line pointwise. The \(13\) fixed lines (paired with the fixed points by \(\sigma\)) must be concurrent because every pair intersects at a fixed point on the axis, so the remaining fixed lines have a common intersection on it. This is an elation of order three, excluded by [Janko–van Trung's theorem, cited as Lemma 3.2 by Akiyama–Suetake–Tanaka](https://ajc.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/74/ajc_v74_p112.pdf).

Let \(N\) be the symmetric incidence matrix of \(\sigma\), so \(N^2=12I+J\). It commutes with the permutation matrix \(P_t\). Over \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\), \(\omega\) a primitive cubic root of unity, the \(\omega\)-eigenspace of \(P_t\) has dimension \((157-f)/3\) and is perpendicular to the ones vector. The restriction of \(N\) squares to \(12I\) on it, with irreducible polynomial \(x^2-12\) over the imaginary quadratic field \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\). The restriction therefore has even dimension (it is a vector space over the quadratic extension obtained by adjoining \(\sqrt{12}\)). This excludes \(f=4,10\), leaving exactly \(f=7\).

On the complement of the ones vector over \(\mathbb C\), \(P_t\) and \(N\) are simultaneously diagonalizable with eigenvalues cubic roots of unity and \(\pm\sqrt{12}\), respectively. Hence \(\operatorname{tr}(P_tN)-13\in \sqrt{12}\,\mathbb Q(\omega)\). The trace is integral, and \(\sqrt{12}\notin \mathbb Q(\omega)\), so \(\operatorname{tr}(P_tN)=13\). This trace counts points \(Q\) adjacent to \(tQ\); exactly one of them is fixed, the fixed absolute point. On each moved three-point orbit \(Q,tQ,t^2Q\), one adjacency implies all three by the action, so the remaining twelve counted points form four \(t\)-invariant triangles. A triangle fixed pointwise cannot occur since the fixed points are collinear. By §2 these are exactly the four claimed invariant Fano subplanes. This is a necessary test on the cyclic extension, not an exclusion of it.

## Reproduction

Run `python3 verify_factor_projective_plane_polarities.py`. The code builds the classical planes over \(\mathbb F_2,\mathbb F_4,\mathbb F_8\) in exact finite-field arithmetic and checks the Fano counts, point/pair profiles, and the triangle–subplane bijection (the latter also by direct seven-point enumeration for \(q\le4\)). It tests commuting involution and cubic coordinate permutations, and exhausts all \(168\) linear correlations of the Fano plane. There is no order-four correlation in that small test but there are order-eight ones, so the report does not silently generalize the order-four claim to every higher even order. No order-twelve example is assumed to exist or generated.
