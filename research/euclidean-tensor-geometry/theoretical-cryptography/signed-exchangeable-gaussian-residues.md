# Residue decomposition for signed-exchangeable integer Gaussian fields

**Scope.** The precision matrix of a centered integer Gaussian field is
given exactly. We compute a forward normalizing constant and a forward
residue law. There is no unknown quantity to infer, decoding input, or
claim about parameters of any scheme.

For a symmetric positive definite rational \(n\times n\) matrix \(Q\), set
\[
                         Z(Q)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb Z^n}e^{-x^\top Qx}.
\]
A signed interchange of \(i,j\) takes \(e_i\) to \(\sigma e_j\) and \(e_j\)
to \(\sigma e_i\), with \(\sigma=\pm1\), and fixes other coordinates.
Call two coordinates equivalent if some signed interchange between them
preserves \(Q\), and include reflexive pairs.

**Theorem.** These equivalence classes, and a gauge of coordinate signs
making all interchanges within each class ordinary, are found by exact
rational arithmetic in polynomial bit time. If the number \(k\) of classes
is fixed, there is a deterministic algorithm polynomial in the binary
input length and \(P\) that computes \(Z(Q)\) within relative \(2^{-P}\).
It also approximates the law of the signed block sums modulo their own block
sizes within total variation \(2^{-P}\). It does not require any numerical
upper bound on the entries, any numerical lower bound on the eigenvalues,
or a condition-number promise. The polynomial exponent can depend on \(k\).

In particular, this covers arbitrarily large positive or negative
interactions of the block sums, as long as \(Q\) is positive definite.
It is not a theorem for an arbitrary diagonal-plus-low-rank matrix:
the block repetition is essential to this construction.

## 1. Recognition and an exact finite decomposition

If signed interchanges on \((i,j)\) and \((j,l)\) preserve \(Q\), conjugating
one by the other gives a signed interchange on \((i,l)\). This proves
transitivity. Check both signs for each pair by comparing entries of the
signed conjugate matrix with the input. For each resulting class choose a
representative, and for each other member choose a sign of an allowed
interchange with that representative. Changing that member's coordinate
by its chosen sign makes all the chosen star interchanges ordinary.
Ordinary interchanges within the class are generated by its star, so they
all preserve the gauged matrix. Changing signs is an exact bijection of
\(\mathbb Z^n\) and leaves \(Z\) unchanged.

Write \(n_1,\ldots,n_k\) for the class sizes and \(J\) for the \(n\times k\)
block-indicator matrix. The gauged matrix has the form
\[
 Q=\operatorname{diag}(a_1 I_{n_1},\ldots,a_k I_{n_k})+J B J^\top,
 \qquad B=B^\top.
\]
Cross-block entries are constant on each pair of blocks, and the off-diagonal
entries within each block are constant. Their values give \(B\); for a
nonsingleton block, \(a_i\) is its diagonal entry minus its common
off-diagonal entry. Testing \(Q\) on \(e_u-e_v\) within the block proves
\(a_i>0\). For a singleton take \(a_i=1\) and adjust \(B_{ii}\) accordingly.
Put \(D=\operatorname{diag}(n_i)\) and \(C=B+\operatorname{diag}(a_i/n_i)\).
Testing on vectors constant within blocks shows \(C\succ0\).

For \(s_i=\sum_{j\text{ in block }i}x_j\) (using the coordinate entries of
the gauged vector), the energy splits into
\[
 s^\top C s+\sum_i a_i\sum_{j\text{ in block }i}(x_j-s_i/n_i)^2 .
\]
Define
\[
 H_i(t)=\sum_{\substack{z\in\mathbb Z^{n_i}\\ \sum z_j=t}}
         \exp\{-a_i\sum_j(z_j-t/n_i)^2\}.
\]
Adding the same integer to each coordinate shows that \(H_i(t)\) depends
only on \(t\bmod n_i\). Write \(T=\prod_i\{0,\ldots,n_i-1\}\).
For a positive definite \(G\) and a rational center \(c\), let
\(\Theta(G,c)=\sum_{w\in\mathbb Z^k}e^{-(w-c)^\top G(w-c)}\). Then
\[
 Z(Q)=\sum_{t\in T}
     \left(\prod_i H_i(t_i)\right)\Theta(D C D,-D^{-1}t).        \tag{1}
\]
The summand indexed by \(t\) is exactly the unnormalized probability
mass of that block-residue vector. There are at most \(n^k\) terms.
This identity works for indefinite \(B\); all terms in (1) are positive,
and \(D C D\) is positive definite.

## 2. All-width evaluation of a block fiber

We show how to evaluate \(H_i(t)\) to additive \(2^{-L}\) at polynomial
bit cost in the original input and \(L\). Write \(N=n_i\), \(a=a_i\),
\(0\le t<N\); the singleton case has \(H_i(0)=1\).

For a broad fiber use the root lattice
\(A_{N-1}=\{z\in\mathbb Z^N:\sum_j z_j=0\}\) in the zero-sum hyperplane,
with covolume \(\sqrt N\). Its dual consists exactly of projections of
\(\mathbb Z^N\) into that hyperplane: the dual condition is that all
coordinate differences are integral. Choose, for each integer vector
modulo a common integer translate, the representative with average in
\([-1/2,1/2)\). Distinct classes have distinct projections. If \(\bar z\)
is that average then \(\|z-\bar z\mathbf1\|^2\ge\frac14\|z\|^2\), since each
nonzero integer coordinate loses at most half a unit.
The zero coset has representative zero. By Poisson summation on the
fiber and this bound,
\[
 H_i(t)=\frac{(\pi/a)^{(N-1)/2}}{\sqrt N}(1+\varepsilon_t),\qquad
 |\varepsilon_t|\le
 \left(\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\pi^2 m^2/(4a)}\right)^N-1.       \tag{2}
\]
The relative estimate is uniform in the residue. The displayed prefactor has logarithm bounded in magnitude by
a polynomial in the binary input if \(a\le1\).
Thus an integer \(K\) polynomial in the input length and \(L\), chosen
large enough in the proof, makes \(a\le1/K\) sufficient for additive
\(2^{-L}\) using this one prefactor. For example bound the nonzero
one-dimensional sum by \(2e^{-\pi^2K/4}/(1-e^{-\pi^2K/4})\)
and use \((1+u)^N\le e^{Nu}\).

For \(a>1/K\), set \(E_t=a\,t(1-t/N)\). The exact identity
\[
                       H_i(t)=e^{-E_t}
          \sum_{\substack{z\in\mathbb Z^N\\\sum z_j=t}}
                        \prod_j e^{-a z_j(z_j-1)}               \tag{3}
\]
follows by expanding the square and using the sum constraint.
Each \(z_j(z_j-1)\) is a nonnegative integer, zero for \(0,1\).
The two exterior terms at \(-u,1+u\) both give \(u(u+1)\).
Keeping coordinates in \([-R,R+1]\) in (3) loses at most
\[
                 N e^{-a R^2/2}(2+\sqrt{2\pi/a})^N
\]
in absolute mass even before multiplication by \(e^{-E_t}\).
Indeed use the union bound over the first deleted coordinate, take out
\(e^{-aR^2/2}\), and bound the remaining product of one-variable sums
by \(2+2\sum_{u\ge1}e^{-a u^2/2}\le2+\sqrt{2\pi/a}\).
Since \(a>1/K\), a radius \(R\) polynomial in \(K,N,L\) suffices.
The sum of the retained product weights at a specified total is
a coefficient of a product of \(N\) one-variable Laurent polynomials.
Compute its finite convolution by dynamic programming on partial sums
of size \(O(NR)\). Rounding all weights to dyadics with sufficiently
many polynomially bounded bits controls the output: the number
of retained terms is at most \((2R+2)^N\), and there are \(N\) factors
per term, each in \([0,1]\). Values smaller than the necessary absolute
tolerance can be truncated without manipulating real numbers with
enormous negative exponents. In particular the binary bit sizes here do
not grow with the numerical value of a stiff \(a\).

## 3. A fixed-dimensional shifted theta with arbitrary conditioning

For fixed dimension \(k\), positive definite rational \(G\), and rational
\(c\), we need only approximate \(\Theta(G,c)\) to absolute error \(2^{-L}\).
The following direct argument is useful even when the shift is far from
the lattice in the metric of \(G\).

Use the rational positive-definite inner product given by \(G\) on
\(\mathbb Z^k\). Apply [LLL, §1](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01457454) to the full basis using exact Gram data; it gives a
unimodular basis \(b_i\) with Gram–Schmidt lengths \(g_i\)
such that \(g_i^2\le2g_{i+1}^2\), and its size-reduction coefficients have
absolute value at most \(1/2\).
We may refer to Euclidean lengths by the isometry given by \(G^{1/2}\);
this matrix need not be represented numerically.
For a small rational \(\delta>0\), take \(j\) to be the last index with
\(g_j\le\delta\), or \(0\) if none. Every \(b_i\) in the prefix \(i\le j\)
has length at most \(\sqrt{k}\,2^{k/2}\delta\). The dual of the prefix lattice
in its span has shortest length at least the reciprocal of this bound:
a nonzero dual vector has a nonzero integer pairing with some prefix basis
vector. By packing disjoint balls at that dual minimum distance,
its nonzero dual Gaussian sum is \(O_k(e^{-c_k/\delta^2})\) for small
\(\delta\), with a positive constant \(c_k\) depending only on \(k\).
Equivalently one can sum successive shells separated by the lower bound
on the minimum and use a polynomial shell count of degree \(k\).

Project the remaining lattice orthogonally to the prefix complement.
The prefix is spanned by part of an integral basis, so each projected
point has a fiber that is exactly a translate of the prefix lattice.
Poisson summation on each fiber approximates its Gaussian factor by
the common prefactor
\[
                     C_0=\pi^{j/2}/\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_0),
\]
with relative error \(O_k(e^{-c_k/\delta^2})\), uniformly in the translate.
The remaining Gaussian factors are precisely the shifted theta on
the projected lattice at the projected center. This is the partial
Poisson formula with a uniform error; the case \(j=0\) is exact with
prefactor one, and \(j=k\) has zero-dimensional quotient.

The projected lattice has Gram–Schmidt lengths \(g_i>\delta\) for all
\(i>j\), so its shortest length is \(>\delta\) by using the largest
nonzero integral basis coefficient. Enumerate only projected points
within radius \(U\) of the projected center. Recursion from the last
Gram–Schmidt coefficient restricts each integer to an interval of
length at most \(2U/\delta\) at a node. Squared norm comparisons and
coordinates of the centers use rational Gram data. At constant \(k\)
this enumerates polynomially many terms whenever \(U\) and
\(\delta^{-1}\) are polynomial. Outside that radius, the same packing
bound gives an absolute tail at most
\[
       \sum_{h\ge0}(1+2(U+h+1)/\delta)^k e^{-(U+h)^2},
\]
uniformly in the projected center.

To select numerical tolerances, rational determinant and trace bounds
give \(\eta>0\) with \(G\succeq\eta I\) and \(\log(1/\eta)\) polynomial in
the input bit length (use \(\eta\le1\)). The full theta is at most
\((1+\sqrt{\pi/\eta})^k\) uniformly in the shift; the maximum of a
shifted one-dimensional Gaussian sum is attained at zero by Poisson
summation. The prefactor \(C_0\) is at most \((\pi/\eta)^{j/2}\): an
integer prefix basis has Euclidean squared covolume at least one before
the change of metric. Thus both upper bounds are at most a computable
\(2^{\mathrm{poly}(\mathrm{bits}(G),k)}\). The partial-Poisson
relative error controls absolute error using the full theta bound
(and, once the relative remainder is \(<1/2\), the approximate theta
is at most twice the full theta). Choose \(\delta^{-1}\) and \(U\) as
sufficiently large polynomials in the bit length and \(L\) to absorb the
errors and the prefactor. All remaining entries, shifts, and ranges
have polynomial binary length. Determinant square roots and elementary
exponentials are evaluated to the required absolute precision, ignoring
only terms already below the tolerance. This proves the fixed-dimensional
absolute-evaluation claim without enumerating a wide direction.

## 4. Bit complexity of the full formula

All the rational matrices in (1), the \(a_i\), and the shifts
have polynomial binary length. Polynomial bounds on the logarithms of
the positive summands' factors suffice for precision bookkeeping.
For \(H_i(t)\), simply bound the constrained sum by the product of
the corresponding \(N\) unconstrained shifted one-dimensional sums;
this is at most \((1+\sqrt{\pi/a})^N\), and \(a^{-1}\) has polynomial
binary length. For the fixed-dimensional theta use the bound in §3.
Let \(M=2^{P_0}\) be a common bound on these factors with \(P_0\)
computed polynomially. Choose each additive factor error smaller than
\[
           2^{-P-5}/\bigl(|T|(k+1)(M+1)^k\bigr).
\]
A product telescoping bound shows the sum of the resulting errors
over all entries of (1) is at most \(2^{-P-5}\).
The required precision is polynomial because \(k\) is fixed and
\(|T|\le n^k\). Also \(Z\ge1\), by the zero vector, so this is a
relative error in \(Z\). Clip approximate summands to be nonnegative
and divide them by their approximate sum. The sum of absolute errors
before normalization still has the same bound; after normalization the
total variation error is at most this error divided by \(Z\).
This proves the theorem, including its statement about the known
block-residue law.

## Comparison with prior work

[Barvinok, *Computing the Theta Series*](https://www.theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v022a009/v022a009.pdf)
gives randomized integration under spectral hypotheses, and in §5
likewise considers Gaussian sums over integer points in subspaces.
[Elkies–Kieffer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22382) gives much faster
precision dependence for fixed-genus Riemann theta on reduced input.
We do not claim the fixed-genus evaluation problem or Poisson summation
as new; §3 gives the specific absolute guarantee needed in (1).
The concrete structural statement here is the recognizable signed
exchangeability condition and the use of its exact residue decomposition
to handle arbitrary binary stiffness in growing dimension. A previous
[fixed-rank numerical-range result](https://github.com/mxym/math/blob/5f6c1e65afcf5be512d935fdbf1df0428dcf58e9/research/euclidean-tensor-geometry/theoretical-cryptography/low-rank-shifted-theta-frontier.md)
does not make this claim for unrestricted weights, and the present proof
does not extend to arbitrary low-rank couplings. The companion finite
checks cover the algebra and representative bounded examples; they
are not assumptions about actual precision matrices.
