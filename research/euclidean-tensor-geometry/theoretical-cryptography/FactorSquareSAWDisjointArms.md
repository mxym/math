# Exact one-ended dead cells and a strict two-ended improvement for square-grid walk windows

Research note, 28 November 2026. A finite set of forbidden cells and one current endpoint already determine which cells can appear on any infinite self-avoiding continuation in the relaxed obstacle model. A single biconnected-component computation finds that set. In particular, for a viable step the tests (b) and (c) of [Tomar's Definition 3.1](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00474v1) are jointly exact in that model. Retaining both endpoints of a genuine walk segment provides strictly stronger information: separate reachability from each endpoint does not imply that disjoint infinite rays can be attached. The shortest obstruction has ten steps. At the same fixed window size, the stricter test provably decreases the growth rate of the corresponding finite-state relaxation.

These are local geometric and finite-state conclusions, not a new record upper bound on the square-lattice connective constant. Tomar's substantially larger automaton gives the stronger numerical upper bound \(2.6586513\). On the lower side, the earlier [stretching note](FactorSquareSAWStretchedBridges.md) improved a particular Numaro certificate; [Pinho's later publicly stated comparison bound](https://github.com/rpinho/math-certificates/blob/aed5011f71082ab1373bdd8692801dd6cb0ec608/c38/bridges-span18/PROOF.md) is \(8388608/3192535=2.62756962727\ldots\), filed by its author as a candidate and unrefereed, not replayed here.

## 1. Finite forbidden set, one endpoint

Let \(F\subset\mathbb Z^2\) be finite, and let \(t\notin F\) be the endpoint. A ray means a one-way infinite simple nearest-neighbor path. Assume \(t\) admits a ray in \(\mathbb Z^2\setminus F\). Call a cell \(z\notin F\cup\{t\}\) *live* if some such ray from \(t\) visits \(z\). No information about the past other than \(F\) is assumed.

Choose an axis-parallel integer rectangle containing \(F,t,z\) and pad it by one cell on all four sides. Let \(H\) be its grid graph with \(F\) deleted, plus a vertex \(\infty\) joined to all vertices of the padded perimeter. The perimeter is an unblocked cycle. When any one grid vertex \(w\) is deleted, the remaining perimeter is still connected. Therefore connectivity between surviving interior cells in the infinite grid after deleting \(F,w\) agrees with connectivity in the padded graph after that deletion: replace each excursion beyond the perimeter by a walk along the remaining perimeter. Likewise, reaching the perimeter is equivalent to reaching infinity, since from a reached perimeter cell one can proceed outward without meeting \(F\); if an outside cell is separately deleted, the exterior can be routed around that one cell. These observations also hold without \(w\) and with the interior cell \(t\) deleted.

For \(E=F\cup\{t\}\), consider the following tests:

- (b) In the grid with \(E\) deleted, \(z\) is not connected to infinity.
- (c) Some grid cell \(w\notin E\cup\{z\}\) has this property: in the grid with \(E\cup\{w\}\) deleted, \(z\) cannot reach infinity or any available neighbor of \(t\).

These are Tomar's tests interpreted with \(F\) as the forbidden cell set. They imply that \(z\) is not live: the part of a ray after \(z\) contradicts (b); in (c), its arrival from \(t\) and its departure toward infinity would both have to use \(w\).

Conversely, if there is no simple \(t\)-to-\(\infty\) path through \(z\) in \(H\), the vertex form of Menger's theorem gives a set of at most one vertex, excluding \(z\), separating \(z\) from the target set \(\{t,\infty\}\). Indeed, otherwise a two-path fan from \(z\) to these two distinct targets concatenates into the required simple path. If (b) fails, there is a \(z\)-to-\(\infty\) path avoiding \(t\), so this separator is not empty and is not \(\{t\}\). It is not \(\{\infty\}\) either: without the artificial vertex, \(z\) and \(t\) both connect to the same perimeter cycle, using viability of \(t\). Thus the separator is a grid cell \(w\notin E\cup\{z\}\); the finite-to-infinite connectivity equivalence gives (c). If the required finite path does exist, its last perimeter vertex can be continued by an outward coordinate ray without meeting any earlier vertex of the finite path. This proves both the exact characterization by (b) or (c) and the exactness of the finite path test.

In particular, Tomar's test (a), a cell with at least three neighbors in \(F\), is redundant once these two tests are applied exhaustively: a queried cell of degree at most one cannot be internal to the required simple path. This is not a claim that (b,c) find all cells excluded by previously forgotten history or global bridge restrictions.

### Computing the entire closure at once

In \(H\) add the artificial edge \(t\infty\). The live vertices are exactly the queried vertices in the biconnected block containing this edge. A simple \(t\)-to-\(\infty\) path through \(z\) becomes a cycle through the artificial edge; conversely deleting the artificial edge from such a cycle recovers the path. Every vertex of the block lies on such a cycle: subdivide the edge, then use the fact that in a 2-connected graph any two vertices lie on a common simple cycle. Edge subdivision preserves 2-connectivity for this edge, which is non-bridging by viability.

All cells outside the original unpadded bounding rectangle of \(F\cup\{t\}\) are live. For either test, any such \(z\) can reach infinity without entering that rectangle and without using any one other deleted cell: the successively expanded rectangular perimeter rings remain connected after one deletion, and consecutive rings have multiple distinct connections. Thus the single padded rectangle of \(F\cup\{t\}\) suffices for all queries at once. The usual [linear-time block decomposition](https://doi.org/10.1145/362248.362272) is linear here in the padded rectangle's area, not necessarily in the cardinality of \(F\).

The *dead closure* \(K(F,t)\) is the union of \(F\) and the unvisited cells that are not live. It is the largest forbidden set that defines the same rooted ray language: all rays avoid \(K\), and adding any live cell would remove an actual ray. Two obstacle sets at the same viable root have the same ray language exactly when their closures coincide. Inclusion of languages is equivalent to reverse inclusion of closures. Moreover, if taking a step from \(t\) to \(u\) is viable, keeping \(K(F,t)\) instead of \(F\) and then marking \(t\) forbidden preserves the exact language rooted at \(u\). This follows by prepending the edge \(tu\) to any continuation after that step. These statements are about the finite-obstacle relaxation, not the unseen past of a particular walk.

## 2. A genuinely recorded segment has two ends

Let \(S=(v_0,\ldots,v_m)\), \(m\ge1\), be a self-avoiding segment with endpoints \(a=v_0\), \(b=v_m\). Say it passes the *separate* test when each endpoint can reach infinity avoiding all the other vertices of \(S\). Say it passes the *joint* test if some two such rays can be chosen vertex-disjoint. Reverse the first ray and concatenate the segment and the second ray to see that the joint test is exactly local embeddability of the entire ordered segment in a two-sided infinite self-avoiding walk.

Take the padded rectangle of \(S\) and construct \(H\) as before, now with only the *internal* vertices of \(S\) removed and both endpoints kept. The joint test is equivalent to a simple \(a\)-to-\(b\) path through the artificial vertex \(\infty\). In one direction, truncate two disjoint rays at their respective first visits to the padded perimeter and join them through \(\infty\). In the other direction, delete \(\infty\) from the path and truncate both parts at their first visits to the padded perimeter. These perimeter vertices are distinct; their outward coordinate rays can be chosen disjoint and avoid the entire rectangle after the first vertices. For two distinct perimeter vertices, outward rays from the same side are parallel and distinct; rays from opposite sides diverge; outward rays from perpendicular sides could meet only at a shared corner, excluded by distinctness. This proves equivalence including corners. The joint test too can be computed by adding an artificial two-edge chain \(a-s-b\) and asking whether \(\infty\) is in the block containing \(as\). Using the chain handles the case when \(a\) and \(b\) are already adjacent.

For the exact computation below we use an equivalent bottleneck test. If both separate tests pass, the joint test fails exactly when removing a single nonterminal grid vertex blocks both arms' access to the perimeter. Apply the two-path form of Menger's theorem with endpoints \(a,b\) as the two sources and a shared sink \(\infty\) attached to the perimeter; authorize at most one unit through each grid vertex, including each source. A one-vertex separator cannot be \(a\) or \(b\) when both separate tests pass. Equivalently, a vertex-deletion witness is on *any* separately chosen escape path from \(a\); it suffices to search that path. When either endpoint is on the unpadded bounding rectangle's boundary, the separate test already implies the joint one: give that endpoint the single outward edge to a padded-perimeter cell \(w\). The other ray can be stopped on the perimeter at a different cell, because \(w\)'s sole inward neighbor is the first endpoint, which the other ray avoids.

[Couronné, §4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.16146v2), checks whether the oldest endpoint is enclosed, in addition to the distinct current-end logic in §4.1. The assertion here is about the precise two tests just defined; it is not a claim to replicate Couronné's complete checker. This two-arm test applies directly to *genuinely recorded* contiguous segments of a two-sided walk. It cannot simply be imposed on a synthetic shortcut path in a Tomar state as though that path were the genuine history.

### A shortest obstruction

Consider the ten-step segment starting at \((0,0)\) with successive directions \(E,N,W,W,W,S,S,S,E,N\); it ends at \((-1,-1)\). Each endpoint can leave through \(w=(0,-1)\), for example then heading east along that row. Once \(w\) is deleted, the only other possible outward neighbor of either endpoint is \((-1,0)\), whose remaining neighbors lie in the segment. Hence no disjoint rays exist.

An exhaustive exact enumeration fixes the initial vertex at the origin and the first step east, extends each direction word by each unit step to an unused vertex, and uses the bottleneck test above. The enumeration gives no obstruction at lengths 1–9. At length 10 it gives 4 words passing the separate but not joint test, 120 failing the separate test, and 10,901 passing the joint test. The four have endpoint distance 2 and form two classes if reflection in the first-step axis is also allowed. Thus the shortest obstruction has ten steps (minimum step length plus endpoint Manhattan distance is twelve). The separate [vertex-split integral-flow replay](verify_factor_saw_disjoint_arms_flow.py) independently reconstructs this census without the bottleneck pruning or the shared-access shortcut.

## 3. A same-window spectral comparison

Fix a window of \(m\) steps. A state is any rooted self-avoiding word of \(m-1\) steps modulo rotation; the first step is normalized east. Enumerate every self-avoiding \(m\)-step word with first step east. Depending on the test being used, its prefix state has an edge to its rotation-normalized suffix state if the word passes. Parallel edges, if present, count with multiplicity. The two matrices on the same states are \(A_{\mathrm{sep}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{joint}}\).

Every finite strict bridge can be preceded by the negative horizontal ray and followed by copies of itself translated from endpoint to endpoint; the resulting two-sided walk is self-avoiding. Every \(m\)-step subword of this bridge therefore passes the joint test. The bridge word is reconstructed from its initial prefix orientation and the ordered automaton edges: its tail normalization is determined by the word, so the running rotation can always be recovered. Consequently both matrices give growth-rate upper bounds for the bridge counts, hence for the square-lattice walk connective constant by the [Hammersley–Welsh bridge theorem](https://doi.org/10.1093/qmath/13.1.108).

The finite-state radii themselves can be strictly compared, not merely the numbers of retained words. The [exact replay](verify_factor_saw_disjoint_arms.py) gives the following intervals. Each displayed pair gives the endpoints of a closed enclosure.

| \(m\) | States | Failing separate | Passing only separate | Passing joint | \(\rho(A_{\mathrm{sep}})\) | \(\rho(A_{\mathrm{joint}})\) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| 10 | 4,067 | 120 | 4 | 10,901 | \(2.724751333,\ 2.724751334\) | \(2.724612788,\ 2.724612789\) |
| 12 | 30,073 | 1,280 | 46 | 79,907 | \(2.711148714,\ 2.711148715\) | \(2.710913857,\ 2.710913858\) |

The certificate uses positive integer weights \(v_i\). Start with all weights \(10^{20}\); repeat 72 times by computing \(z=Av\) then replacing \(v_i\) by \(100+\lfloor 10^{20}z_i/\max_j z_j\rfloor\). Nothing relies on convergence of this heuristic. Finally compute \(Av\) with integers; the largest rounded-up quotient \((Av)_i/v_i\) gives an upper bound. For the lower bound, take the strongly connected component of the all-east state, retain only internal arcs and use the smallest rounded-down quotient there, with the same positive \(v\). The upper inequality iterates on the entire matrix; the lower inequality iterates on a submatrix. All rounding in the final bounds is outward at scale \(10^9\). The strongly connected component used for the lower bounds has size 4,015 at \(m=10\) and 29,551 at \(m=12\), for both models.

In particular, the decrease at \(m=10\) is at least \(0.000138544\), and at \(m=12\) at least \(0.000234856\), in these specific same-state fixed-length matrices. These decreases concern relaxation radii, not the actual connective constant or Tomar's optimized order-30 certificate.
