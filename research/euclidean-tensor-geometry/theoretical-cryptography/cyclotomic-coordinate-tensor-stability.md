# Stability of the optimal Gaussian basis norm under cyclotomic tensor extension

For a Euclidean lattice \(L\), let \(\beta(L)\) denote the minimum operator norm of a matrix whose columns are an integral basis of \(L\), with an orthonormal coordinate system in its real span. For spherical canonical noise of width \(s\), integral trace coordinates of a basis \(B\) have width-squared matrix \(s^2B^{\mathsf T}B\); the least spherical width obtainable by independent Gaussian padding in those coordinates is \(s\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\). The change of covariance is the statistical step underlying Peikert–Pepin's structured-LWE comparison. We study this step over all integral bases, not the tensor constraints of the whole reduction.

## 1. Statements

A reusable tensor theorem is given below. Its consequences for the cyclotomic integer ring are as follows. Normalize the conductor \(m\) so \(m\not\equiv2\bmod4\), put \(\widehat m=m\) for odd \(m\) and \(m/2\) for even \(m\), and use the unscaled canonical Euclidean norm counting both complex conjugates.

* If \(m\) has exactly three distinct odd prime factors \(p<q<r\), with \(q\ge11\) or with \((p,q)=(5,7)\) and \(r\ge37\), the optimal norm is exactly \(\sqrt{\widehat m}\). Every minimizing basis uses only shortest vectors. In each normalized tensor block, up to signs and order, its column set is the complement of a torsion-free spanning cellular tree of the complete three-color complex, in the precise sense of §5.
* The exact norm is still \(\sqrt{\widehat m}\) for \((p,q)=(3,5)\), \(r\ge37\), or \((p,q)=(3,7)\), \(r\ge29\). But in these families there are minimizing bases with some **nonshortest** columns. For each fixed pair \((p,q)\) and sufficiently large \(r\), a finite local-contraction and integral-quotient criterion describes all optimizers, not just shortest-column ones.
* With \(k\ge2\) distinct odd prime factors \(p_i\) satisfying \(\min_i p_i\ge 2^k+2\), the exact norm is again \(\sqrt{\widehat m}\), and the same torsion-free complementary-tree description holds in the corresponding \(k\)-color complex.

Exponents of prime factors are arbitrary throughout. None of the statements addresses the parameter of any deployed scheme.

## 2. Stable addition of a tensor factor

Set \(B_b=A_{b-1}^*=P_b\mathbb Z^b\), where \(P_b=I-\boldsymbol 1\boldsymbol 1^{\mathsf T}/b\); write \(u_j=P_b e_j\). Let \(L\) be a rank-\(n\) Euclidean lattice with \(\lambda=\min_{0\ne v\in L}\|v\|^2>1/2\) and \(\beta(L)=1\). If
\[
   b\ge5,\qquad 2(1-2/b)\lambda>1,\qquad b>2n,                 \tag{1}
\]
then \(\beta(L\otimes B_b)=1\).

### Short vectors occupy one slice

Every tensor vector is represented by \(\sum_j w_j\otimes u_j\) with \(w_j\in L\), unique modulo adding one common vector in \(L\) to the \(w_j\)'s. This follows because the only integral relation among the \(u_j\)'s is their sum. Its squared norm is
\[
   \sum_j\|w_j\|^2-\frac1b\Big\|\sum_j w_j\Big\|^2 .
\]
Subtract one randomly chosen anchor \(w_a\) from all the \(w_j\)'s. The mean of \(\sum_j\|w_j-w_a\|^2\) over \(a\) is twice the displayed norm. Thus a vector of squared norm at most 1 admits a representation with total representative squared norm at most 2 and hence at most three nonzero \(w_j\)'s. If there are \(\ell\in\{2,3\}\) nonzero entries, Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the tensor's squared norm below by \((1-\ell/b)\ell\lambda\), which is greater than 1 by (1) (for \(b\ge5\), the \(\ell=3\) lower bound is no smaller). Consequently every nonzero vector of squared norm at most 1 has form \(w\otimes u_j\), and its factor satisfies \((1-1/b)\|w\|^2\le1\). This conclusion does not need \(b>2n\).

### The integral-basis condition and tensor norm

For an integral basis with operator norm at most 1, all its columns are pure as above. Let \(L_j\subset L\) be the sublattice generated by the chosen \(w\)'s at label \(j\). Each such group is independent because the full basis is independent. The quotient of the tensor lattice by these columns is
\[
    \left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^b L/L_j\right)\Big/\Delta(L),
       \quad \Delta(w)=(w+L_j)_j.                              \tag{2}
\]
The group before quotienting has rank \(bn-n(b-1)=n\). If the columns form a basis, \(\Delta\) is a surjection between a free rank-\(n\) group and a rank-\(n\) group, so its kernel has rank zero and is zero. In particular no \(L/L_j\) has torsion. Any complete slice with \(n\) columns is thus an integral basis of \(L\), not merely a real basis. Conversely a collection of \(n(b-1)\) pure columns generates an integral basis exactly when the diagonal map in (2) is an isomorphism.

For the positive semidefinite frame operators \(F_j=\sum_{w\text{ at }j}w w^{\mathsf T}\), identify the ambient tensor space with vectors \((y_1,\ldots,y_b)\) in \(\operatorname{span}(L)^b\) satisfying \(\sum y_j=0\). The total squared evaluation by the basis is \(\sum_j y_j^{\mathsf T}F_j y_j\), since \(\langle u_j,y\rangle=y_j\) on the zero-sum space; the input squared norm is \(\sum_j\|y_j\|^2\).

There are at least \(b-n\) complete slices: the sum over slices of their dimension shortages is \(n\). Each complete slice is an integral basis of \(L\), so its top frame eigenvalue is at least 1. Select a nonzero line of corresponding eigenvectors in each complete slice. If \(b>2n\), the direct sum of these lines has dimension \(>n\); hence it contains a nonzero tuple summing to zero. On that tuple the frame's squared evaluation is at least the input squared norm. Every candidate basis of norm at most 1 therefore actually has norm at least 1. Conversely a tensor product of optimal bases of \(L\) and \(B_b\) has norm 1. For \(B_b\), any \(b-1\) of the \(u_j\)'s form an integral basis; its frame is \(I-u_a u_a^{\mathsf T}\) on the \((b-1)\)-dimensional space and has norm 1 for \(b\ge3\). This proves the tensor theorem.

For sufficiently large \(b\) with \(L\) fixed, there is a sharper description. Every pure factor \(w\) in a basis of norm \(\le1\) is then in the fixed finite set \(\{w\in L:\|w\|^2\le1\}\), by discreteness of lattice lengths and \(\|w\|^2\le b/(b-1)\). Testing \(y\otimes u_j\) against the global frame, and dropping other nonnegative slice contributions, gives \(F_j\preceq b/(b-1)I\). There are only finitely many possible \(F_j\), since each slice has at most \(n\) independent vectors from a fixed finite set. An \(F_j\) with largest eigenvalue \(>1\) has a positive gap bounded below uniformly over this set. Hence for all sufficiently large \(b\), every optimal basis has \(F_j\preceq I\) in every slice. Conversely local contractions together with the integral-basis condition (2) give an optimal basis, by the zero-sum evaluation formula and the exact norm theorem.

An optimal basis need not consist of shortest vectors, even when every optimal basis of \(L\) does. Suppose an integral norm-1 basis \(C\) of \(L\) has some \(w\in L\) with \(\|w\|\le1\) and with one coordinate in \(C\) equal to \(\pm1\). Start with \(C\otimes\{u_1,\ldots,u_{b-1}\}\). Remove the corresponding basis column at one of the used labels, and insert \(w\otimes u_b\). The change has determinant \(\pm1\) because \(u_b=-\sum_{j<b}u_j\). Each local frame is a contraction, including the new single column; whenever (1) applies this is again optimal. If \(w\) is not shortest in \(L\) and a shortest pure tensor is available, the inserted column is not shortest.

## 3. The two-factor input and the three-prime range

Let \(u_i^{(p)}=P_p e_i\), so \(\|u_i^{(p)}\|^2=(p-1)/p\). The only shortest vectors of \(B_p\) are their signs. To see the gap, for an integer lift \(x\) its squared projected norm is \(p^{-1}\sum_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)^2\). If there is a coordinate difference of magnitude at least two, the corresponding pair and its pairs through every other coordinate contribute at least \(2p\) to the numerator. Otherwise remove a constant and get an indicator of a \(k\)-subset, with norm squared \(k(p-k)/p\). Thus the next squared norm is \(2(p-2)/p\) for \(p\ge5\), and is at least 2 for \(p=3\).

A rank-\(t\) tensor in \(B_p\otimes B_q\) has an integral decomposition into independent lists of \(t\) factors on both sides: in integral bases choose a lattice basis of the column submodule of its coefficient matrix, then use the coefficient rows from that basis. The volume squared of any \(t\) independent vectors of \(B_p\) is at least \((p-t)/p\). Indeed, for integer lifts \(X\), the squared Gram determinant is \(1/p\) times the sum of squared maximal minors of \([\boldsymbol1,X]\) by Cauchy–Binet and the Schur complement. There is one nonzero maximal minor; each unused row has first coordinate one and can replace at least one of its rows to give another nonzero minor. The \(p-t\) minors so obtained are distinct. For the Gram matrices of the two factor lists, AM–GM applied to their positive-definite symmetric product now gives
\[
       \|w\|^2\ge t\left(\frac{(p-t)(q-t)}{pq}\right)^{1/t}.    \tag{3}
\]
For \(p\ge5,\ q\ge7,\ t=2\) this is at least \(2\sqrt{3/7}>11/10\). For \(t\ge3\) it is at least \(t/(t+1)^{2/t}\ge3/4^{2/3}>11/10\); the inequality for the denominator follows from the decrease of \(\log(x+1)/x\).

For \(p=3\) the only other rank is 2. Write
\[
       3\|w\|^2=\|v_1\|^2+\|v_2\|^2+\|v_1-v_2\|^2
\]
using two independent \(v_i\in B_q\). All three vectors are nonzero and cannot all be shortest: differences of independent signed projected coordinate vectors have squared norm \(2\) or \(2(q-2)/q\). The rank-2 bound is accordingly \((4q-6)/(3q)\). These facts show for all distinct odd \(p<q\) that the minimum squared norm of \(L=B_p\otimes B_q\) is
\[
                  \lambda_{p,q}=\frac{(p-1)(q-1)}{pq}.         \tag{4}
\]

For \(q\ge11\) and \(r>q\), or for \((p,q)=(5,7),\ r\ge37\), equation (4) gives \(2(1-2/r)\lambda_{p,q}>1\). Thus every tensor vector of squared norm at most 1 in the triple product is a single \(r\)-slice, by the anchor argument of §2. In that slice a factor of squared norm at most \(r/(r-1)\) in \(B_p\otimes B_q\) must be a product of shortest vectors:
* Rank at least two is excluded by (3) when \(p\ge5\), and by \((4q-6)/(3q)\) when \(p=3\) and \(q\ge11\).
* Among rank-one vectors a nonshort factor forces squared norm at least \(12/11>13/12\) when \(q\ge11\). For \(p\ge5\) the minimum alternative is \(2(p-2)(q-1)/(pq)\); for \(p=3\) either use the lower bound 2 for its own nonshort vector or \(4(q-2)/(3q)\) for the other factor. At \((5,7)\) the minimum alternative is \(36/35>37/36\).

Consequently in the ranges of the first bullet of §1 all eligible columns are signed projected coordinate products. The rank argument in §5 gives exact norm 1 in each normalized block without requiring \(r>2\operatorname{rank}(B_p\otimes B_q)\).

### Exceptional pairs and nonshort minimizers

The two-factor norm is 1 also for \((p,q)=(3,5),(3,7)\). Here are complete small checks establishing the fact used in the tensor theorem. A signed short vector is \(u_i^{(3)}\otimes u_j^{(q)}\). At \(q=7\), the only other sign classes at squared norm \(\le1\) are the medium vectors \(u_i^{(3)}\otimes(u_j^{(7)}+u_k^{(7)})\), \(j\ne k\), of squared norm \(20/21\). There are no rank-2 vectors at squared norm \(\le1\) by the rank-2 bound. A contraction must satisfy \(|\langle v,w\rangle|^2\le(1-\|v\|^2)(1-\|w\|^2)\) on two columns. Two medium vectors have inner product of absolute value at least \(3/21>1/21\), since the integer numerator has factors \(3\boldsymbol1_{i=i'}-1\) and \(7|S\cap T|-4\). A medium vector is compatible with a short only for a different first label and a second label outside its pair: the absolute scaled inner product must be at most 3, and those are exactly the possibilities. Thus there are at most 10 short columns alongside a medium, fewer than the 12 total independent columns needed.

For \(q=5\), the complete list up to sign is 15 shorts of squared norm \(8/15\), 30 mediums of form \(u_i\otimes(u_j+u_k)\) and squared norm \(12/15\), and 60 longs of form \(u_i\otimes u_j-u_l\otimes u_k\) with \(i\ne l,\ j\ne k\), and squared norm \(14/15\). In rank two the three norm terms in the displayed rank-2 identity must be \(4/5,4/5,6/5\): all three cannot be short, two next terms are too large, and a term of at least 2 is too large. Changing the first-coordinate basis by its three-term relation puts the two short terms at \(v_1,v_2\), which must have opposite signs as projected coordinate vectors. The rank-one list follows from the one-factor gap. The finite verification accompanying this note tests all contracted lists of length eight containing a medium or a long, and finds none. It fixes one such vector by label permutation and whole-column signs, allows all sign classes when the fixed vector is long, and otherwise tests just the short and medium classes. Each prefix is checked by exact positive semidefiniteness of the \(15\)-scaled deficit using integral Schur complements. Thus every contracting basis in either pair uses only projected coordinate products. The frame rank argument of §5 gives norm exactly 1, and a product basis gives existence.

For \((3,5)\) with \(r\ge37\) and \((3,7)\) with \(r\ge29\), (1) holds with \(b=r\), \(n=8\) or \(12\), and \(\lambda\) from (4). An actual nonshort optimal column can be made from \(w=u_i^{(3)}\otimes(u_j^{(q)}+u_k^{(q)})\) with two different non-omitted labels in a product basis of \(L\), as in the construction of §2. The two-factor squared norms \(12/15,20/21\) are below 1 but above the respective minima. Pure shortest products are available, so these are genuinely longer columns in exact optimizers.

## 4. Many large prime factors: simultaneous anchors

In the \(k\)-factor product, write \(P=\bigotimes_i P_{p_i}\), \(M=\prod_i p_i\), \(n=\prod_i(p_i-1)\), and \(\delta=n/M\). Any vector \(v\in P\mathbb Z^M\) has an integer representative \(z\) with \(\|z\|^2\le 2^k\|v\|^2\). To see this, independently in each axis subtract the value at an anchor coordinate from the entire fiber. This operator annihilates constants in that axis, so acting on \(v\) is the same as acting on any original integer lift. Projecting after the subtractions gives back \(v\). For a zero-sum fiber, the average squared norm after uniform anchor subtraction is exactly twice the original; subtractions commute across axes and preserve the zero-sum condition in untouched axes. The expected squared norm of the resulting integer representative is therefore \(2^k\|v\|^2\).

If \(\|v\|^2\le1\), let \(h\le 2^k\) be the number of nonzero entries of this representative. The projected coordinate Gram has diagonal \(\delta\) and off-diagonal magnitudes at most \(\delta/(p_{\min}-1)\). For \(h\ge2\), the diagonal bound minus the off-diagonals (via Cauchy–Schwarz on absolute coordinates) gives
\[
       \|v\|^2 \ge \delta h\left(1-\frac{h-1}{p_{\min}-1}\right).
\]
The right side is concave in \(h\); for \(p_{\min}\ge2^k+2\), its endpoints at \(2\) and \(2^k\) are greater than 1. Indeed \(\delta\ge1-k/(2^k+2)\ge2/3\), and each endpoint without \(\delta\) is at least \(8/5\). Thus the representative is supported at one coordinate and must have coefficient \(\pm1\), since \(4\delta>1\). This proves that the admissible nonzero vectors are exactly signed projected coordinate products.

## 5. Frame rank, integral trees, and cyclotomic normalization

For all the shortest-column ranges, the frame of every projected coordinate product together is the identity on the tensor space, since it is the projection of the standard orthonormal basis. Any subset of \(n\) of them has frame bounded by the identity and attains norm 1 when \(M-n<n\): the omitted frame rank is at most \(M-n\), below the dimension \(n\). In the ranges above \(\delta=n/M>1/2\). This also proves the assertion used for the two small pairs. A product of \(p_i-1\) projected coordinate choices in each axis is an integral basis, so the norm is attained.

For clarity about the integer criterion, let \(\mathcal K\) be the join of \(k\) discrete sets with cardinalities \(p_i\), and index its top facets by coordinate tuples. Orient by color order. The real top cycle space is the range of \(P\), because its top boundary equations are the vanishing of every fiber sum. Over the integers, the top boundary \(\partial:\mathbb Z^M\to Z_{k-2}\) maps onto the integral reduced \((k-2)\)-cycle lattice: the join has zero reduced homology below its top dimension. One direct proof uses the augmented chain complex of each discrete color, whose augmentation onto \(\mathbb Z\) splits, and tensors the split complexes; only the tensor of augmentation kernels survives in homology. The cycle lattice is a primitive subgroup of the \((k-2)\)-chains, as the kernel of an integer boundary map. It follows that the integer kernel of the projection \(P\) is \(\operatorname{im}\partial^{\mathsf T}\): an integer functional vanishing on top cycles descends through the onto boundary map to an integer functional on \(Z_{k-2}\), and extends to all \((k-2)\)-chains by primitivity.

For \(S\) a set of \(n\) selected facets and \(T\) its complement, the quotient by the selected projected coordinates is therefore \(\operatorname{coker}\partial_T^{\mathsf T}\), with \(\partial_T:\mathbb Z^T\to Z_{k-2}\) square in rank. It vanishes precisely when \(\partial_T\) is unimodular, equivalently when the subcomplex with all lower faces and top facets \(T\) has zero reduced integral homology in dimensions \(k-1\) and \(k-2\). This is the stated torsion-free spanning cellular tree criterion; a rational tree with nontrivial torsion does not give an integral basis.

For an odd prime power \(p^a\), group the power basis into blocks \(\zeta_{p^a}^i\zeta_p^j\) with \(0\le i<p^{a-1}\) and \(0\le j<p-1\). The Ramanujan trace sum gives orthogonal blocks and each block has Gram \(p^{a-1}(pI-J)\). Dividing norms by \(\sqrt{p^a}\) realizes copies of \(B_p\). At conductor \(2^a\) (\(a\ge2\)) the power basis is orthogonal with squared length \(2^{a-1}\). For coprime prime-power factors the integer cyclotomic ring is the tensor product of these rings: adjoining the prime-power roots generates \(\mathbb Z[\zeta_m]\), and the degrees multiply; the canonical Gram products also multiply over embeddings. Thus normalization by \(\sqrt{\widehat m}\) gives orthogonal copies of the indicated product blocks. In all ranges stated, their minimum squared norm is \(>1/2\): this is immediate in the shortest-column ranges, and in the two nonshort ranges use the purity argument and (4). So a basis of norm \(\le1\) in the orthogonal sum has each column supported in one block, and each block must receive its own integral basis. The single-block results apply unchanged to arbitrary powers in the conductor.

## References and scope

[Peikert and Pepin, *Algebraically Structured LWE, Revisited*](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~cpeikert/pubs/structured-lwe.pdf), §5.3 and Corollary 5.5, analyze the trace-coordinate Gaussian comparison for a power basis, with further constraints for the full reduction. [Lyubashevsky, Peikert and Regev's Toolkit](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~cpeikert/pubs/toolkit.pdf), §4, develops the powerful tensor basis. [Martin and Reiner](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0402206) give the cyclotomic–simplicial matroid relation, and [Duval, Klivans and Martin](https://arxiv.org/abs/0802.2576) explain integral torsion in higher-dimensional trees. The two-odd-prime coordinate result is recorded in the [companion note](cyclotomic-gaussian-coordinate-optima.md). The new parts here are tensor stability over an arbitrary bottom lattice under (1), the passage from all columns to integral local bases, the triple and large-prime optimizer ranges, and exact cyclotomic nonshort optimizers. No assertion is made for the omitted small-prime ranges or that changing a Gaussian coordinate map alone changes a hardness theorem. The proof is self-contained apart from elementary cited background; the small \((3,5)\) search is replayable with the accompanying JavaScript verifier.
