# Unimodal CGF growth, atoms, and the lowest degrees at a given root order

A *basic* cyclotomic generating function (CGF) is a product of
\(\Phi_m(q)\) for \(m\ge2\) with nonnegative integer coefficients.
In particular it is monic, symmetric, and has constant coefficient 1.
Let \(U_n\) count the unimodal basic CGFs of degree \(n\), and let \(A_n\)
count those irreducible under multiplication **inside the unimodal
monoid**. We use the standard \([b]=1+q+\cdots+q^{b-1}\).

The results give lower bounds for [Billey–Swanson, Problems 49 and 50](https://doi.org/10.37236/12687),
not the exact entropy or the full generating set of the unimodal monoid.
They are distinct from the [HSOP entropy bounds](CGFHSOPDivisorTreeGrowth.md)
for Problem 62 and the [HSOP membership solution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22226)
to Problem 60.

## 1. An explicitly counted submonoid

Let \(M\) be generated by every \([b]\), \(b\ge2\), and
\[
                      T_k=(1+q^k)[k+1],\qquad k\ge2.              \tag{1}
\]
The coefficients of \(T_k\) are 1 except for a central coefficient 2.
Both \(1+q^k\) and \([k+1]\) are products of cyclotomic polynomials,
and their product is unimodal. Products of symmetric unimodal nonnegative sequences are
symmetric and unimodal: decompose each sequence into concentric
uniform blocks with nonnegative weights, and convolve. Every pair of
blocks gives a symmetric unimodal trapezoid with the same product center.
Thus \(M\) is contained in the unimodal monoid.

Write \(M_n\) for its **number of distinct polynomials** of degree \(n\).
Then
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}M_n z^n=\frac{(z^2;z^3)_\infty}
                            {(z;z)_\infty(z^2;z^2)_\infty},
 \qquad (a;b)_\infty=\prod_{j\ge0}(1-ab^j).                       \tag{2}
\]
For the proof, observe \(T_k[k]=[2k][k+1]\). In a representation
\(\prod_b[b]^{a_b}\prod_k T_k^{t_k}\), repeatedly use this identity to
remove simultaneous positive powers at \(a_k,t_k\). Each rewrite
lowers \(\sum_k t_k\) by one, so it terminates with \(a_k t_k=0\).

Signed exponents \(d_m\) in the \(q\)-integers \([m]\) for \(m\ge2\)
determine the product and are uniquely determined by it: at the largest
different \(m\) a primitive \(m\)-th root detects the difference.
In a reduced representation these exponents give
\[
       a_m-t_m=d_m-t_{m-1}-\mathbf1_{2\mid m}t_{m/2},
       \qquad t_1=0.                                            \tag{3}
\]
The right side involves only earlier \(t\)'s. The two nonnegative
integers \(a_m,t_m\) with product zero are uniquely recovered as its
positive and negative parts. Thus every reduced representation is
counted once. At \(m\), independent powers of \([m]\) and \(T_m\)
have respective weights \(m-1,2m\); not allowing both positive gives
\((1-z^{3m-1})/((1-z^{m-1})(1-z^{2m}))\). Multiply over \(m\ge2\).
In the two progressions of weights \(3m-1\) and \(2m\), extend down
one term; each extension adds \(1-z^2\), so these changes cancel.
This gives (2).

In fact there is a full asymptotic for this submonoid. Put
\(B=7\pi^2/36\). Then
\[
             M_n\sim \frac{B^{2/3}}{2\pi\,3^{1/6}\Gamma(2/3)}
                     n^{-7/6}\exp(2\sqrt{Bn}).                  \tag{4}
\]
Consequently
\[
 \frac{\pi\sqrt7}{3}=2.770624\ldots
 \ \le\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log U_n}{\sqrt n}
 \ \le\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log U_n}{\sqrt n}
 \ \le\ \frac{\sqrt{105\zeta(3)}}{\pi}=3.576080\ldots .            \tag{5}
\]
The first bound improves the ordinary partition construction using
only \(q\)-integers, with constant \(\pi\sqrt{2/3}=2.565099\ldots\).
The last bound is the classical unrestricted product count of
[Boyd–Montgomery, *Cyclotomic partitions*](https://websites.umich.edu/~hlm/rpubs.html);
the [preceding HSOP note](CGFHSOPDivisorTreeGrowth.md#3-upper-benchmark-for-all-hsops)
also derives its logarithmic constant.

**Details of (4).** The exponent of \((1-z^j)^{-1}\) in (2) is
\(b_j=1+\mathbf1_{2\mid j}-\mathbf1_{j\equiv2\bmod3}\ge0\).
The Dirichlet series of these weights is
\[
 D(s)=(1+2^{-s})\zeta(s)-3^{-s}\zeta(s,2/3).
\]
The unique pole is simple at 1 with residue \(7/6\). The continuation and
polynomial vertical-growth conditions in the weighted partition theorem
follow from the usual Riemann and Hurwitz zeta continuations (or
Euler–Maclaurin on the periodic weight sequence). Also
\[
 D(0)=-5/6,\qquad e^{D'(0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi\,3^{1/6}\Gamma(2/3)},
\]
by \(\zeta(0,a)=1/2-a\), \(\zeta'(0,a)=\log\Gamma(a)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)\).

For completeness the angular hypothesis of
[Meinardus's theorem, as stated by Granovsky–Stark–Erlihson, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0701584v2)
holds with a stronger-than-required bound. For \(t/(2\pi)<|\alpha|\le1/2\)
one needs a power lower bound on
\(\sum_j b_j e^{-tj}\sin^2(\pi j\alpha)\).
If \(\|6\alpha\|\ge t/10\), use \(j=6l,\ l\ge1\).
For \(r=e^{-6t}\) and \(x=12\pi\alpha\) their geometric sum is
\[
 \sum_{l\ge1}r^l\sin^2(lx/2)
 =\frac{r(1+r)(1-\cos x)}
        {2(1-r)((1-r)^2+2r(1-\cos x))}\gg t^{-1}.
\]
If instead \(|6\alpha-u|<t/10\) for an integer \(u\), then
\(1\le|u|\le3\). Use \(j=6l+1\), \(0\le l\le1/(4t)\). Each
\((6l+1)\alpha\) is within \(1/40+t/60\) of \(lu+u/6\), hence a
uniform positive distance from every integer, and its exponential
weight is bounded below. This again gives \(\gg t^{-1}\).
Both progressions have \(b_j\ge1\). Now the theorem at pole parameter 1
gives exponential constant \(2\sqrt{(7/6)\zeta(2)}=2\sqrt B\),
power \((2D(0)-3)/4=-7/6\), and prefactor
\(e^{D'(0)}B^{(1-2D(0))/4}/(2\sqrt\pi)\), which is (4).

## 2. Sharp root-order gap and the next two complete degree slices

Suppose the unimodal basic CGF \(f\) has \(\Phi_m\) as a factor, \(m\ge3\),
and degree \(d\). Necessarily \(d\ge m-1\). The complete list in the next
three degree slices is:

| Degree | All unimodal possibilities |
|---|---|
| \(m-1\) | \([m]\) only |
| \(m\) | \([2][m]\), and \(T_{m/2}\) if \(m\) is even |
| \(m+1\) | \([m](1+a q+q^2)\) for \(a=0,1,2\); additionally, for even \(m=2k\ge6\), \((1+q^k)[k+2]\) and \([2]T_k\) |

These do not require \(m\) to be the largest factor order. The
log-concave subset of the table is \([m]\), \([2][m]\), and the
degree-\(m+1\) cases \(a=1,2\), respectively. For \(m=2\) the
unimodal lists have only the common forms shown (no additional
even-case entries); all are log-concave, including \(a=0\) giving
\([4]\). This last case follows directly from the degree-one and
degree-two cyclotomic factors.

**Proof of the table.** For nonnegative integers \(c_j\) with \(c_0=1\),
decompose \(f\) into its centered uniform blocks:
\[
                     f(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
                                   c_j q^j[d-2j+1].             \tag{6}
\]
After substituting \(q=e^{2\pi i/m}\), removing the common phase, and
multiplying by \(\sin(\pi/m)\), the zero condition becomes
\[
                 \sum_j c_j\sin((d-2j+1)\pi/m)=0.               \tag{7}
\]
For \(d<m-1\) all summands with positive \(c_j\) are positive.
For \(d=m-1\), only \(j=0\) gives zero sine; any other nonzero
coefficient would destroy the root. Thus \(f=[m]\).

At \(d=m\) the \(j=0\) term equals \(-\sin(\pi/m)\), and the
other sines are positive, at least \(\sin(\pi/m)\) each. Equality
is possible at \(j=1\) and, if \(m\) is even, \(j=m/2\).
Thus exactly one eligible \(c_j\) is 1, yielding
\([m+1]+q[m-1]=[2][m]\), or the polynomial with a central
coefficient 2, \(T_{m/2}\).

At \(d=m+1\), \(j=0\) contributes \(-\sin(2\pi/m)\), and \(j=1\)
contributes zero. Write \(a=c_1\). For even \(m\ge4\), all later
positive sines are at least \(\sin(2\pi/m)\), with equality
only at \(j=2,m/2\) (possibly the same index). For odd \(m\ge5\),
only the last sine is smaller than \(\sin(2\pi/m)\).
It equals \(\sin(\pi/m)\), and the ratio to it
\(2\cos(\pi/m)\) lies strictly between 1 and 2. It cannot give
equality by any multiple when used alone, nor can it be used
together with a different positive sine. Thus \(j=2\) is the
only eligible positive index for odd \(m\); \(m=3\) has only
that positive index anyway.

At \(j=2\), (6) gives \(f=[m](1+a q+q^2)\). The remaining
quadratic is cyclotomic exactly for \(a=0,1,2\) among nonnegative
integers. In the second even case \(m=2k,\ k\ge3\), one obtains
\[
 f=(1+q^k)\bigl([k+2]+a q[k]\bigr).
\]
For \(a=0,1\) the bracket is \([k+2]\) or \([2][k+1]\), respectively.
For \(a\ge2\), let \(g=[k+2]+a q[k]\). At \(0<r<1\),
\[
                  (1+r)g(-r)=1-ar+(-1)^k r^{k+1}(a-r).
\]
This is negative at \(r=1/a\) for odd \(k\). For even \(k\ge4\)
use \(r=3/4\); the expression decreases with \(a\) and at \(a=2\)
is bounded above by \(-1/2+(3/4)^5(5/4)<0\).
Since \(g(0)=1\), there is a root inside the unit circle, excluding
the remaining possibilities. Conversely all listed polynomials
are visibly unimodal (or products of symmetric unimodals) and
cyclotomic. The explicit coefficient sequences of \([2][m]\),
\([3][m]\) and \([2]^2[m]\) are log-concave. Each other listed polynomial
has adjacent coefficients \(u,u,u+1\) on its increasing side for
some \(u>0\), violating log-concavity.

## 3. Many atoms of the full unimodal monoid

Every unimodal basic CGF with coefficient of \(q\) equal to 1 is an
atom in this monoid: both factors of a nontrivial decomposition
would have constant coefficient 1 and coefficient of \(q\) at
least 1. This applies to all the \(T_k\) in (1) and to
\((1+q^k)[k+j]\) for \(k\ge2,\ j\ge1\).
It refers to factorization into **unimodal** factors, not into
arbitrary nonnegative cyclotomic products.

More generally, for any unimodal basic CGF \(F\) of degree \(u\),
\[
                          [k]F(q^k),\qquad k\ge2,                 \tag{8}
\]
has each coefficient of \(F\) repeated \(k\) times, and is a
unimodal atom of degree \(k(u+1)-1\). There is no loss of
information about \(F\). The further multiplier \(1+q^D\) also
preserves this conclusion for \(2\le D\le k\): in (6) all inflated
uniform block lengths are at least \(k\); translating a block by
\(D\) gives two overlapping or touching blocks, whose sum is
unimodal with the same overall center for every layer. The new
coefficient of \(q\) is still 1. The same argument works for
\(D\le2k\) if \(u\) is odd, since all layer lengths before inflation
are even and at least two.

For odd \(n\), take \(k=2\) in (8), no further multiplier, and
\(u=(n-1)/2\). For \(n\equiv2\bmod4\), take \(k=2,D=3\) and
\(u=n/2-2\), which is odd. Inject the polynomials counted by \(M_u\).
In both progressions
\[
     \liminf\frac{\log A_n}{\sqrt n}
          \ \ge\ \frac{\pi\sqrt7}{3\sqrt2}=1.959127\ldots .       \tag{9}
\]
For a bound uniform over all degrees, take the least \(k\ge2\)
not dividing \(n\). Then \(k=O(\log n)\): otherwise the least common
multiple of all integers up to \(k-1\), whose logarithm is
\(\gg k\) by Chebyshev's prime estimate, could not divide \(n\).
Set \(D=1+(n\bmod k)\in[2,k]\) and \(u=\lfloor n/k\rfloor-1\).
The resulting atom has degree \(n\), and the injection from \(M_u\)
gives \(\log A_n=\Omega(\sqrt{n/\log n})\) for all large \(n\).
These are counting lower bounds for the minimal generating set,
not a description of all its members.

## Replay

Run `python3 verify_cgf_unimodal_generator_growth.py` without
`-O`. The integer replay enumerates 30,532 unrestricted products
through degree 20 before checking the angular gap and all 57
near-order slices, without using that gap to prune the search.
It independently builds, deduplicates and normalizes the 11,005
submonoid polynomials through degree 24, verifies the weighted
partition counts, and tests 16,345 inflation examples. The proof
and Meinardus's theorem, rather than the finite counts, yield (4).
