# A geometric restriction on tripled maximal arcs in order twelve

## Statement and scope

Let \(\Pi\) be a hypothetical projective plane of order \(12\). Let \(F\) be a maximal arc of degree \(3\) whose Steiner triple system is a tripling: \(F\) is the disjoint union of three nine-point affine planes \(A,B,C\), the triples within each are their affine lines, and every other triple has one point in each of \(A,B,C\). There is no restriction on the associated Latin square of order nine or on its affine labellings. Call such other secants **crossing**. For \(X\notin F\), let \(k(X)\) be the number of internal \(A\)-secants through \(X\); it is also the corresponding number for \(B\) and \(C\). In particular, \(k=3\) means that \(X\) is on no crossing secant.

1. At most one point off \(F\) has \(k=3\).
2. If exactly one point \(X\) has \(k=3\), the degree-four maximal arc in the **dual plane** formed by the lines disjoint from \(F\) contains an order-three subplane. Its line corresponding to \(X\) consists of the four external lines through \(X\). Its other nine points correspond exactly to the external lines not through \(X\) that avoid every type-two point.

Thus if the dual degree-four arc contains no order-three subplane, every point off \(F\) is on a crossing secant. This is a geometric restriction on a full-plane realization, not an exclusion for all abstract class covers, all tripled arcs, or all planes of order twelve.

**Comparison.** Tomar, [Subplanes of order 3 and maximal arcs in projective planes of order 12, v1](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00389v1), §5 and §8, introduces the class-cover approach and discusses tripling; Theorem 5.3 excludes the affine STS(27). The [preceding repository note](../Plane12TriplingTransversalObstruction.md) gets the type counts and the restriction \(n_3\in\{0,1,4\}\) already for abstract class covers. Those counts are recalled below. The new step uses ambient external lines to exclude \(n_3=4\) and extract a dual subplane in the \(n_3=1\) case. Only the exclusion of \(n_3=4\) invokes Tomar's Theorem A (equivalently its single-line exclusion in §7). No new program or formal verification is used.

## Preliminary counts

A line is external if it misses \(F\); otherwise it contains exactly three points of \(F\) and is a secant. An off-arc point sees \(F\) partitioned into nine triples on its secants and lies on four external lines. There are 117 secants and 40 external lines. If \(t\) of the nine secants through a point \(X\notin F\) are crossing, the nine points of each of \(A,B,C\) give \(3k+t=9\), proving that \(k\) is common to the three groups.

Write \(n_i=\#\{X:k(X)=i\}\). Every internal \(A\)-secant contains ten off-arc points, so \(\sum_i i n_i=12\cdot10=120\). Every internal \(A\)-secant meets every internal \(B\)-secant at an off-arc point, so \(\sum_i i^2n_i=12^2=144\). There are 130 off-arc points. Solving gives
\[
n_2=12-3n_3,\qquad n_1=96+3n_3,\qquad n_0=22-n_3.
\]
At a type-three point the three internal secants from each group are the three lines of one affine direction. A direction cannot be used by two type-three points, because its three ambient lines have at most one common point. Call the other directions unused, of which there are \(u=4-n_3\) in each group. In each unused direction the three pairs of internal lines give three distinct type-two intersections: each is off the arc, and a coincidence would make the direction used. A type-two point uses an unused direction and two of its lines in each group. Two different type-two points cannot have the same direction in both \(A\) and \(B\), because the two corresponding two-element subsets of each three-line direction would share an \(A\)-line and a \(B\)-line. The argument also holds for \(A,C\) and \(B,C\). Thus if \(u>0\), the three points from an unused \(A\)-direction use three different unused \(B\)-directions, so \(u\ge3\). This yields \(n_3\in\{0,1,4\}\).

Write \(Z\) for the set of type-zero points, which lie on no internal secant. For an external line \(L\), each of the twelve internal \(A\)-secants meets \(L\) at one point, so the sum of \(k\) over \(L\) is twelve. From the thirteen points on \(L\) it follows that
\[
|Z\cap L|=1+\#\{\text{type two on }L\}+2\#\{\text{type three on }L\}.
\tag{1}
\]
For a crossing secant \(M\), four internal \(A\)-secants meet \(M\) at its point of \(A\), and the other eight meet it off \(F\). No point on \(M\) has type three. Summing \(k\) over its ten off-arc points therefore gives
\[
|Z\cap M|=2+\#\{\text{type two on }M\}.
\tag{2}
\]

## Excluding four type-three points

Suppose \(n_3=4\), hence \(n_2=0\), \(|Z|=18\). The line \(L\) through any two type-three points \(X_1,X_2\) is external. It is not crossing, since neither point lies on a crossing secant. Nor is it internal, since that would make both points use the same direction of the corresponding group.

Every line through \(X_i\) and a point of \(Z\) is external, for the same crossing obstruction and since \(Z\) avoids the internal secants. For \(Z\setminus L\) there are only three choices of external line other than \(L\) at \(X_1\) and likewise at \(X_2\). Their intersections give at most nine points. With \(a=\#\{X\in L:k(X)=3\}\), (1) now gives \(18-(1+2a)\le9\). Hence \(a=4\), and all four type-three points lie on this one line \(L\).

Put \(D=Z\setminus L\), with nine points, and write \(I\) for the four type-three points. At each \(X\in I\), the three external lines other than \(L\) partition \(D\). For any two distinct points of \(I\), the nine intersections of the two three-line families are distinct off \(L\), and all nine points of \(D\) must occur. Consequently each of these twelve lines contains three points of \(D\). No pair of points of \(D\) is repeated on different lines, and the twelve lines account for \(12\binom{3}{2}=\binom92\) pairs. Together with \(L\), these lines give an order-three projective subplane on \(D\cup I\): pairs of finite points are accounted for, each finite point has one line through each infinity point, and each line has four of the thirteen points. Equivalently, this completes the four affine direction partitions of \(D\).

Likewise each of \(A\cup I,B\cup I,C\cup I\), with its internal lines and \(L\), is an order-three subplane. Indeed, the four direction centers for each group are precisely the four different points of \(I\). For the subplane \(A\cup I\), no point of \(B\cup C\cup D\) lies on an internal \(A\)-secant or on \(L\). These are all its 27 free points: an order-three subplane has thirteen points and thirteen lines, the latter with nine distinct off-subplane points each, since two of the lines meet in the subplane. Its degree-four arc, the subplane plus its free points, would therefore contain **four** subplanes on the common line \(L\). This contradicts Tomar's Theorem A, which bounds the number on a line of a subplane in this arc by two.

## Extracting a dual subplane when there is one type-three point

Suppose \(n_3=1\), with sole type-three point \(X\); put \(Y=\{P:k(P)=2\}\), of size nine, and \(|Z|=21\). There are three unused directions in each group. By the injectivity of the two-direction projections above, the nine direction triples of \(Y\) form a Latin square of order three. After permuting the direction labels in each group it is the addition table of \(\mathbb F_3\): normalizing the first row and column leaves just the standard reduced square. So we can label these points by \((r,c,r+c)\). Two different points of \(Y\) share an internal secant exactly when they agree in a direction coordinate, since any two two-element subsets of one direction share a line. The pairs which do not share an internal secant are therefore precisely the pairs within each of the three triples \(r-c=\text{constant}\). Indeed, if the row and column differences are nonzero, their sum is nonzero precisely when the two differences agree.

Each point of \(Y\cup Z\) joins \(X\) by an external line: neither \(Y\) nor \(Z\) can share an internal direction line used by \(X\), and \(X\) lies on no crossing secant. Each of the four external lines through \(X\) can contain points from only one of the three \(Y\)-triples, as pairs from different triples already share an internal secant. So the only possible split of a \(Y\)-triple is \(2+1\), with the other two triples each unsplit. If this occurs, let \(P\) be the singleton point on one external line, and \(M\) the external line carrying the other two points of its triple. The two points of \(Y\) on \(M\), and the five points of \(Z\) on \(M\) given by (1), do not share an internal secant with \(P\). They give seven distinct non-internal lines through \(P\) other than \(PX\). But \(P\) lies on three crossing and four external lines, one being \(PX\), so only six are available. This excludes the split.

Thus we can name the four external lines through \(X\) as \(L_1,L_2,L_3,L_4\), with \(L_1,L_2,L_3\) each carrying one \(Y\)-triple and \(L_4\) carrying none. Formula (1) gives six points of \(Z\) on each of \(L_1,L_2,L_3\) and three on \(L_4\). Let \(U_i\) be the three remaining points of type one on \(L_i\) for \(i=1,2,3\), and \(Z_4=Z\cap L_4\).

For any \(P\in Y\cap L_i\) and \(j\in\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{i\}\), the six lines from \(P\) to \(Z\cap L_j\) are different, are not internal, and are not \(PX\). So each of the six non-internal lines through \(P\) other than \(PX\) meets both other \(Y\)-bearing rays at a point of \(Z\). None of these lines contains a second point of \(Y\): a point in the same triple lies on \(PX\), and a point in a different triple joins \(P\) by an internal secant. In particular, by (1), a non-\(PX\) external line through \(P\) has exactly two points of \(Z\), already on the two other \(Y\)-bearing rays, and avoids \(Z_4\). (By (2) its crossing counterparts each contain one point of \(Z_4\).)

There are \(9\cdot3=27\) external lines not through \(X\) which contain some \(Y\), counted without repetitions. Thus there are \(40-4-27=9\) external lines \(E\) not through \(X\) that avoid \(Y\). Precisely these are the three external lines other than \(L_4\) through each of the three points of \(Z_4\): all nine such lines are distinct and none can contain \(Y\). Each \(E\) has one point of \(Z\) by (1) and this is already on \(L_4\). Hence each \(E\) meets \(L_i\) at a point of \(U_i\) for \(i=1,2,3\). Every point of \(U_i\) lies on at most three of these lines, as it has just four external lines including \(L_i\). Their nine incidences with \(U_i\) imply that every point of \(U_i\) lies on exactly three.

Now each of the nine \(E\)'s meets exactly one point in each of \(U_1,U_2,U_3,Z_4\), and every point in these four three-sets belongs to exactly three \(E\)'s. The twelve points account for \(12\binom32=\binom92\) pairwise intersections of the \(E\)'s, without repetition by the plane axioms. In the dual plane, take as thirteen points the nine \(E\)'s and the four \(L_i\)'s. The dual line of \(X\) contains the four \(L_i\)'s. The twelve dual lines corresponding to \(U_1,U_2,U_3,Z_4\) each contain three \(E\)'s and the indicated \(L_i\). These lines join every pair of the thirteen points, so they form an order-three projective subplane (each selected line has four selected points, and each selected point lies on four selected lines; counting the \(13\binom42=\binom{13}{2}\) pairs through selected points shows that all pairs of selected lines meet in the subplane). All thirteen dual points are external lines of \(F\), giving the claimed containment in the dual maximal arc.

For the dual-arc assertion directly, a primal point is incident with zero external lines if it is in \(F\), and with four if it is outside \(F\). Its dual line therefore meets the set of the forty external-line dual points in zero or four, which is exactly the maximal-arc property. The constraint proved above imposes no extra conclusion on the case \(n_3=0\).
