# Nine-set planar (4,3) piercing: small exact companion lemma

Let 4 ≤ n ≤ 9 be the size of a family of nonempty compact convex sets in the plane with the (4,3)-property. It is pierced by at most three points. For n=9, every such family also has four members with a common point. The hypothesis in the four-intersection assertion and conclusion of the three-point bound apply to arbitrary convex shapes.

1. Any disjointness graph of a (4,3) family is empty or a star: two disjoint graph edges make their four endpoints violate (4,3); a triangle and any fourth vertex likewise do. A graph whose every two edges share a vertex is a star or a triangle.
2. In any such family of five sets, designate the disjointness-star center (arbitrary vertex if no edges). If it belongs to a good (intersecting) triple, the other two sets intersect. If not, (4,3) on each triple of the other four plus the center forces every triple among the four to intersect, so Helly gives a common point for the other four. Thus five sets always need at most two points. For 4–7 sets, choose a good triple and, if at least four sets remain, another; if the remainder after the first triple contains three sets, those can always be pierced by two because the disjointness graph is a star. For eight sets, choose a good triple containing the center and another in the remainder, leaving an intersecting pair. If no good triple contains the center then apply Helly to all the other sets as before.
3. Nine sets have at least 30 intersecting triples: their complementary triples give a tetrahedron-free 3-graph, and ex_3(9,K_4^(3))=54. The finite bound is old computationally, with a compact independent proof by pair-excess and deletion in https://www.erdosproblemaday.com/report/500 §§2–4. For a self-contained version, write C(v,k,t) for the covering number by k-blocks of all t-subsets. It satisfies C(v,k,t)≥ceil(v C(v−1,k−1,t−1)/k) by counting incident blocks. We show C(7,4,3)≥12: if 11 blocks (add distinct blocks if necessary), every pair occurs at least three times, since it has five triple completions and each containing block covers two. Excess pair degrees above three add to three; excess incident at each vertex divisible by three since it equals three times its block-degree minus eighteen. Thus there are precisely two vertices with excess three and the pair between them has excess three. Of seven blocks at one of these vertices, six contain the other and one does not, covering at most 6+3=9 internal pairs of the other five vertices after deleting the first vertex, short of ten. The deletion bound twice gives C(8,5,4)≥20 and C(9,6,5)≥30. Complements of good triples are exactly a C(9,6,5) cover.
4. If no four of the nine convex sets intersect, the nerve has no 3-simplices. Its H_2 over F_2 vanishes: finite compact convex families have the same nerve as sufficiently small open convex enlargements (choose epsilon below the positive gap for each of the finitely many empty intersections). The nerve theorem identifies homology with that of an open set of R^2; degree-two homology is zero. Therefore the boundary map from its triangles is injective. Its image is in the cycle space of the complete 1-skeleton on nine vertices, dimension binom(8,2)=28, contradicting the ≥30 triangles. One point thus pierces four sets, and the five-set argument yields two more points.

McGinnis, https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.13195 gives nine points for unrestricted n. This is not a global improvement, only a necessary lower size of a counterexample to three points. No direct matching result found in quick prior-art query; no exhaustive novelty claim.

In fact there are at least two different intersecting 4-subfamilies. If there were at most one, the kernel of the triangle boundary would have dimension at least 30−28=2, whereas the image of the tetrahedron boundary would have dimension at most one. This contradicts vanishing H₂ independently of which pairs are edges.
