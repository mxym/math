# An exceptional-root blocker pattern distinguishes any two kissing directions

Normalize all roots and sphere points to squared norm 2. For a fixed $E_7$ or $E_8$ root system $\Phi$, attach to any point $x$ on this sphere the signed set
$$
 B(x)=\{r\in\Phi:r\cdot x>1\}.
$$

**Theorem.** If $B(x)=B(y)$ then $x\cdot y>1$. In particular no two different points of a kissing configuration can have the same pattern. This is a pointwise strict comparison, not a claim that there is a uniform positive angular gap from $60^\circ$ in every open cell. Along with the [six-dimensional proof](KissingE6GlobalMaskCapacity.md), it covers all three exceptional simply-laced root systems.

This is not automatic for simply-laced roots. In $A_4$ (all $e_i-e_j$ in the sum-zero subspace of $\mathbb R^5$) take
$$
x=(54,-31,-31,4,4)/\sqrt{2435},\qquad
y=(54,4,4,-31,-31)/\sqrt{2435}.
$$
Both norms are $\sqrt2$, and both patterns consist of the same four roots $e_1-e_j$, $j=2,\ldots,5$: the positive differences from the first coordinate are 50 or 85 before scaling, and all other differences have magnitude at most 35. But $x\cdot y=2420/2435<1$.

**Why it helps.** If some signed exceptional roots are retained in a kissing code, each nonroot point must have a pattern disjoint from those retained roots. The theorem allows *one site per candidate signed pattern*, even without fixing a chamber or assuming that any root line is represented. Counting different patterns is not in itself a proof that their corresponding sites can coexist.

## Proof

Roots themselves have distinct singleton patterns. The nonroot patterns have sizes from 6 through at most 27 in $E_7$, and from 8 through at most 29 in $E_8$ (these extremes are sharp by the [all-aperture spectra](KissingE7E8AllCapAngles.md); no assertion about every intermediate size is needed). The [standalone exact checker](verify-kissing-exceptional-mask-capacity.js) proves that every potentially occupied spherical cell has the required property.

1. **Enumerate cell shapes, including boundaries.** Fold a point into a closed fundamental chamber and use simple-root products $w_i=\alpha_i\cdot x\ge0$. With $G$ the simple-root Gram matrix, the squared norm is $w^\top G^{-1}w$. If a positive root has coefficient vector $r$, its product with the point is $r^\top w$. The strict blocker set is therefore an upper ideal $C$ of positive roots. Generate all ideals up to the stated endpoints by adding a maximal remaining root in all possible ways. Use the polytope cut out by nonnegativity, $r^\top w\ge1$ for minimal roots of $C$, and $r^\top w\le1$ for maximal roots outside $C$. A coordinate otherwise unbounded above may be capped at the valid Cauchy bound 2. All vertices are obtained from independent active bases.

   To eliminate a strictly impossible ideal, take independent constraints $Aw\ge b$ from nonnegativity and its lower blocker inequalities. If $z=(AGA^\top)^{-1}b\ge0$ and $b^\top z\ge2$, Cauchy–Schwarz forces squared norm at least 2. A point strictly satisfying the blocker constraints could be scaled slightly toward zero and still satisfy those weak lower constraints, so could not have squared norm 2. The checker also finds such certificates for every ideal at the first size above the upper endpoint. At the lower endpoint, if the polytope reaches but does not cross the sphere, strict convexity leaves just individual vertices; test their actual masks and whether they are roots.

2. **Enclose the sphere slice.** Call vertices of squared norm above 2 high and all others low. For each low vertex $v$, choose a common dyadic $0\le t<1$ such that every $t u+(1-t)v$ with $u$ high has squared norm at most 2. Include those edge points, all high vertices and all low vertices of norm exactly $\sqrt2$ in the generator list. This hull contains the sphere slice: in the barycentric simplex, cut by $\sum_u\lambda_u=\sum_v [t_v/(1-t_v)]\lambda_v$. The low side is generated by low vertices and edge cuts; by strict convexity any sphere point it contains is an included norm-$\sqrt2$ generator. The high side is already in the stated hull. With no high vertex, only the attained sphere vertices with the actual strict mask need be kept.

3. **Account for all possible relative chambers.** For an upper ideal $C$, its sum $s=\sum_{r\in C}r$ is dominant. Indeed the simple reflection pairs of roots contribute nonnegatively to $s\cdot\alpha_i$, since $C$ is upward closed. When this product is zero, the simple reflection fixes $C$ as a set. The stabilizer of the dominant vector $s$ is generated by precisely these simple reflections, and so fixes $C$. If two dominant-chamber upper ideals are Weyl equivalent, their dominant sums agree, and this argument shows they are identical. Thus to compare two points with the same mask, fold the first; the second may lie in a different chamber, but its corresponding generator list is obtained by the stabilizer of the same $s$. The checker closes the list under those exact reflections.

4. **Strict angular comparison.** For every generator $a$ of the first hull and every stabilized generator $b$, the checker verifies $a\cdot b>0$ and $4(a\cdot b)^2\ge\|a\|^2\|b\|^2$ in the physical metric $G^{-1}$. At equality, the two rays normalized to squared norm 2 do **not both** have the actual signed mask $C$. This is checked with signed integer root products, before squaring. By bilinearity and the triangle inequality the cosine bound $1/2$ extends to all positive combinations. Equality would require collinearity of all used generators on each side and equality for every contributing generator pair. Their normalized rays would then both carry the actual pattern, a contradiction.
