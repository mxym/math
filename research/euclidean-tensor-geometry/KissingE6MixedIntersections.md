# A mixed-orientation obstruction for two E6 line systems

**Theorem.** Let R and S be two distinct E6 root systems in the same real six-dimensional Euclidean space, with root squared length two. Suppose a kissing configuration contains at least one representative of every root axis of each system. If R and S have at least 12 common axes, their intersection is exactly a D5 root system (20 common axes).

The orientation hypothesis is essential to our argument for a D4 intersection: rotating the two-dimensional perpendicular space of D4 generally gives distinct E6 root systems whose intersection is D4. What is excluded is choosing one sign on each axis of both while maintaining all scalar products at most 1. No assumption is made that common axes occur with both signs.

### A5 determines its E6 extension

Realize A5 as the roots e_i−e_j in the sum-zero subspace of six coordinates. A root of E6 not in this A5 has horizontal component v satisfying max v−min v≤1, by integrality and the root-product bound. Let a and b be the minimum and maximum. Summing (v_i−a)(b−v_i)≥0 and using Σv_i=0 gives ‖v‖²≤−6ab≤3(b−a)²/2≤3/2. Equality requires three coordinates +1/2 and three −1/2. In particular an additional root cannot lie in the span of A5.

If z is its signed coordinate normal to this span, the lattice generated by A5 and the extra root has determinant 6z². It is full rank in the E6 lattice of determinant 3, so 6z²=3I² for some positive integer index I. Thus z²=1/2 or 2. In the first case v has squared norm 3/2 and is one of the 20 stated half-sign vectors; in the second v=0. There are at most 2·20+2=42 possibilities, exactly the required number 72−30. All must occur, proving uniqueness of the extension with no orientation assumption.

### E6 around D4

The classical D4-fiber description of the six-dimensional configurations is discussed in [Cohn, Jiao, Kumar and Torquato, §4.3](https://msp.org/gt/2011/15-4/gt-v15-n4-p11-p.pdf). We derive the exact fiber structure needed here for an arbitrary E6 containing a fixed D4, then prove the mixed-orientation obstruction.

Write the D4 roots as ±e_i±e_j in four coordinates, with a perpendicular plane. For every other root (v,w), v lies in the dual D4 lattice (all entries integral or all half-integral), and |v_i|+|v_j|≤1. Thus either v=0 and ‖w‖²=2, or ‖w‖²=1 and v belongs to one of the following three sets of size eight: the signed coordinate unit vectors, the half-sign vectors with even parity, or those with odd parity. Write these sets as C₁,C₂,C₃. Each is four antipodal orthonormal axes; the absolute product of vectors of different sets is 1/2.

For a fixed unit w, the D4 Weyl group acts transitively on each C_i, so any occupied fiber contains at least an entire C_i. It cannot contain two such sets: roots at the same w would have nonintegral products 1±1/2. The roots with v=0 form a simply-laced subsystem in the perpendicular plane with 0,2,4 or 6 roots. Their number is a multiple of eight because all the other 48 roots outside D4 come in eight-point fibers. Hence there are none, and there are exactly six unit vectors w, in opposite pairs of the same color C_i. In particular, E6 never contains D4 and an orthogonal A1.

The resulting three distinct *unoriented* axes in the plane have different colors. Two axes of different colors have absolute scalar product 1/2 by integrality; two axes of the same color would be perpendicular. All three cannot be perpendicular. If just two perpendicular axes had the same color, a unit vector of the other color would have squared products 1/4 and 1/4 against a planar orthonormal basis, impossible. Thus we have a regular hexagon of unit directions with one color for each opposite pair.

### Why two systems cannot be jointly represented when their intersection is exactly D4

Use the same three horizontal colors for both E6 systems. For a planar axis of color i choose a unit direction w. Its eight off-D4 axes are represented by (v,w) as v ranges over C_i; in any proposed code their signs can, initially, vary with v.

A planar axis of the first system is always at projective angular distance strictly less than 60° from an axis of *different* color in the second. If it does not coincide with one of the second triple, two axes of that triple are strictly within 60°, and they have distinct colors. If it coincides with one, that axis must have different color, for coincidence with the same color would give common roots outside D4. The same observation applies with the two systems reversed.

For such a cross pair choose the two planar references w,w′ so that w·w′>1/2. For all v∈C_i and v′∈C_j with v·v′=1/2, the two reference roots have product above 1, so their selected signs must be opposite. The bipartite graph defined by v·v′=1/2 is connected. Indeed C_i and C_j are signed orthonormal bases with cross products ±1/2. Any two nonopposite distinct elements of C_i are orthogonal and have two common positive neighbors in C_j, by orthogonality of their two rows of signs; opposite vertices can be connected through a third element. Every vertex on the other side has a neighbor as well. This forces a constant sign on each side separately. Reversing a planar reference just permutes the horizontal labels and reverses all signs at that fiber; the property of constant sign is unchanged.

Therefore each of the six relevant planar axes (three from each system, counting the two systems separately) contributes its entire eight-point horizontal color at one selected planar direction. Distinct colors require scalar product at most 1/2 between their planar directions: some horizontal pair has product +1/2. The two occurrences of the same color require planar product at most zero, since equal horizontal vectors have product 1. For this last comparison the two roots are distinct: same-color axes cannot coincide in the case at hand.

The six planar directions are thus distinct unit-circle points, all separated by at least 60°. They form a regular hexagon. Its three same-color pairs must each be at distance 120° or 180°. They cannot all be at distance 120°: such edges stay inside the two parity triangles, each of odd order, so cannot give a perfect matching. A 180° pair has identical unoriented planar axis and identical horizontal color, giving common roots outside D4. Contradiction.

### Finish the intersection count

The common roots form a simply-laced crystallographic root system of rank at most six, closed under every common root reflection. If it has at least 24 roots, the complete ADE list is:

* E6, D6, A6, D5+A1, D5;
* A5+A1, D4+A2, A5, D4+2A1, D4+A1, A4+A2;
* A4+2A1, A3+A3, D4.

E6 would give equality of the systems. Among full-rank entries, D6, A6, D5+A1, D4+2A1, A4+A2, A4+2A1 and A3+A3 have determinants 4,7,8,16,15,20,16 respectively, none three times a square. Every entry containing A5 gives equality by the uniqueness argument. Every entry containing D4 with an orthogonal factor was already ruled out. The pure D4 case is the mixed-orientation obstruction above. Only D5 remains.

**Application to the 73-point complete-line branch.** Suppose 12≤p<36 and all 36 axes of an E6 are represented in a 73-point code with p antipodal pairs. These pairs all lie on original axes by the 36-line projective bound. The negative graph of its 37−p outsiders must contain two vertex-disjoint edges. Otherwise it is triangle-free and has a cover of size at most one, leaving 36−p independent outsiders after deleting one. These and the paired axes give a second E6 by [Anstreicher’s projective uniqueness theorem](https://optimization-online.org/2001/01/248/). The common axes are precisely the p paired original axes. The theorem forces D5, and now *both* signs of every D5 axis are present. That contradicts the [known D5 cross-section bound of 72; see also the short proof here](KissingE6TwentyPairs.md). This eliminates the no-matching branch uniformly; it does not eliminate codes with a matching or solve the kissing problem.
