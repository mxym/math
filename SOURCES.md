# Source scope and prior work

- Amos Beimel, Yuval Ishai, Eyal Kushilevitz and Hanjun Li, [Cryptography with Weak Privacy](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1978), TCC 2025, [mirrored author PDF](https://opencryptology.org/2025/1978/versions/20260428T172000Z.pdf). The last open problem on printed p. 7 explicitly asks for o(log n) share size for the (n−1)-out-of-n threshold with weak privacy, after reviewing the 2-out-of-n example. Definition 2.2, printed p. 12 (terminology continues onto p. 13), compares every share tuple and every ordered pair of secrets on each unauthorized set, and asks for correct reconstruction for every random choice on each authorized set. Theorem 1 above meets those exact conditions. The paper does not prescribe a particular positive p for this question. The construction is stronger: any fixed p<1, and a specified inverse-logarithm approach to p=1, retain O(log log n) shares. No claim about other open items or any computational amplification theorem is made. Their §3.3 Theorem 3.4 and Corollary 3.6 address general access structures through dominated graphs; their share bound grows with the largest unauthorized size, so it does not provide this threshold result.
- Amos Beimel and Matthew K. Franklin, [Weakly-Private Secret Sharing Schemes](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-70936-7_14), TCC 2007, pp. 253–272, [author PDF](https://www.cs.bgu.ac.il/~beimel/Papers/BFTCC.pdf). Section 5.1, Lemma 4 is the two-bit, threshold-two construction. The closest high-threshold precedent is §5.3, Theorem 4: using a restricted Shamir alphabet over a field of prime-power order Q≥n, its (n−1)-threshold construction has alphabet floor(Q−(Q−1)/4)+1, asymptotically 3Q/4, rather than polylogarithmic in n. Its proof also analyzes completions, but it reconstructs an evaluation polynomial and encodes one bit by its top coefficient, not by a nonlinear color of a parity word. Theorem 6's earlier generic lower bound has different parameters; it does not establish a logarithmic lower bound for the weak-privacy model here.
- Andrej Bogdanov, Siyao Guo and Ilan Komargodski, [Threshold Secret Sharing Requires a Linear-Size Alphabet](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v016a002/v016a002.pdf), Theory of Computing 16(2), 2020, §1.1 Theorem 1.1 and §3's proof and Eq. (3.3), pp. 8–10 for the latter. For perfect privacy, the (n−1)-threshold case requires alphabet at least n in the equal-size setting, and their unequal-share extension gives Σ_i 1/q_i≤1. Theorem 2 above explicitly recovers that endpoint; the contribution there is a quantitative extension for p<1 via the first and second moments of commuting averaging projections. The sign consistency used for reconstruction is the same underlying restriction used by the perfect-privacy proof. This note does not claim a new perfect-privacy bound or a sharp weak-privacy tradeoff. Their cited Kilian–Nisan secrecy-threshold lower bound concerns perfect privacy and does not apply directly to the new construction.
- [AICR c/0075](https://aicr.info/c/0075/) is a locator, not the primary formulation. It correctly identifies the one-bit, perfectly correct threshold n−1, and distinguishes this question from anonymity, different reconstruction thresholds, or amortization of long secrets. The only setup of our scheme is a public deterministic table, independent of the secret and dealer coins.

The targeted duplicate check included the 2007 construction and its high-threshold theorem, the 2025 full text and publication listings, perfect-privacy linear-size-alphabet lower bounds, and nearby recent threshold and support-privacy work. No prior all-n result with these weak-privacy parameters was located in those sources. This is a scoped check rather than a comprehensive priority assertion. The construction uses elementary parity checks, a binomial tail and standard conditional expectation; novelty is not claimed for any of those individual tools.
